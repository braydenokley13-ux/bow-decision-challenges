# Teacher test — run this before October

**Status: ready to run. Needs a room, five teachers and a morning.**

District 26 was told, unprompted, that *"a small group of teachers review or test parts of the platform"* before October. This is that. It is written to be handed to whoever runs it and used without further preparation.

Its real purpose is narrower than "get feedback." **Every judgement in this gauntlet that begins *a teacher would* is a guess, and this is the only instrument that can prove one wrong.** Four of them are named at the bottom as falsifiable predictions, written before anybody sits down, so the session cannot be read as agreement with whatever we already believed.

---

## Setup

**Who.** Five teachers. Middle school, and at least two who do **not** teach financial literacy — the letter's use case is a post-instructional tool inside somebody's existing unit, and a specialist will forgive things a generalist will not.

**How long.** 50 minutes each, one at a time. Not a group session: a room of teachers converges on one opinion within ten minutes and you lose four data points.

**What you need.** A laptop per teacher, the platform on a real URL, a class code you have *not* pre-made, and a second device (phone or tablet) for Task 4.

**Recording.** Screen and audio if they consent. If not, the observation sheet is the record — but write timings as they happen, not afterwards.

**What the runner does not do.** See "No coaching," below. Read it before the first session, not during.

---

## Onboarding script — read this aloud, verbatim

> Thanks for doing this. I want to be clear about what this is: **I am testing the software, not you.** There is no way for you to do this wrong, and anything that confuses you is a defect I need to know about. If you get stuck, stay stuck for a moment and say out loud what you're looking for — that sentence is the most useful thing you can give me.
>
> I'd like you to talk while you work. Say what you're looking at, what you expect to happen, and what you're trying to do. If you go quiet I'll ask "what are you thinking?" — that's not a hint, it's just me asking.
>
> BOW is a set of financial-literacy simulations for grades five to eight. Students play a scenario and make decisions; teachers see what their students did and what it says about their understanding. It's meant to be used **after** you've taught something, not to teach it.
>
> I'm going to give you five short tasks. I won't help you with them. That's going to feel unfriendly and I apologise in advance — it's the only way I find out what's actually hard. At the end I'll ask you some questions and you can ask me anything.
>
> Any questions before we start?

## No coaching — the rules for the person running this

These are the rules that make the data worth having. Breaking them is easy and invisible.

1. **Do not touch the laptop.** Not to scroll, not to point at the screen. Your finger is a hint.
2. **Do not name anything on screen.** Not "the class page," not "the reading queue." If they can't find it, that is the finding. Say: *"Where would you look?"*
3. **Answer every question with a question, until they give up.** *"What do you think it does?"* · *"What would you expect?"* · *"What would you do if I weren't here?"* Only after they say some version of *"I don't know, I'd stop"* do you help — and then **write down that you helped, and at what**.
4. **Do not defend anything.** When a teacher says something is confusing, wrong, or stupid, the only correct responses are *"say more"* and *"what did you expect instead?"* Explaining why it is the way it is ends the useful part of the session.
5. **Do not ask leading questions.** *"Was that clear?"* gets a yes from everybody. *"What did that tell you?"* gets the truth.
6. **Silence for ten seconds before you speak.** Most of the good material arrives at second seven.
7. **Never say "just."** *"You just click there"* tells a teacher they are slow.

## The five tasks

Read each aloud, hand over, and stop talking. Time from the moment you stop speaking.

### Task 1 — Set up a class and give it to your students *(target: under 6 minutes, unaided)*

> You've taught a unit on budgeting. You want your class to do one of these simulations for homework tonight. Set that up, and tell me what you'd write on the board for your students.

**What is being measured.** Whether the class → assignment → class-code path is walkable cold. The thing to watch for is what they write on the board: if it isn't a class code, they have not understood how a student gets in.

### Task 2 — The ten-second question *(target: under 10 seconds to a correct answer)*

Open the class overview with the seeded class before you hand it over.

> This is a class that has been working. Give it ten seconds and tell me who needs you tomorrow.

**What is being measured.** The single most important teacher claim in this product. Stop them at ten seconds whether or not they've answered. Write down **what they looked at first** — that tells you what the page is actually for, as opposed to what it was designed for.

### Task 3 — Read one student *(target: under 3 minutes)*

> Pick the student you just named. Tell me what they understand and what they don't, and show me how you know.

**What is being measured.** Whether the evidence trail supports a defensible statement about a child. Listen for whether they cite **a moment in the run** or **a level**. Citing the moment is the product working; citing only the level is a teacher trusting a number they cannot check.

Two things to note without prompting:
- Do they notice any cell marked `—` rather than a score? Do they ask what it means? **Do they read it as a zero?**
- Do they treat the written answer as scored by BOW? It is not — a person scores it and the product says so. Whether they *believe* that is the question.

### Task 4 — The interrupted student *(no target; this is a trust task)*

Have a student run half-finished on the second device.

> One of your students started this on a Chromebook in class and got pulled out for an appointment. They're finishing it at home tonight on a different computer. Show me what happens to their work.

**What is being measured.** Promise 9 in the letter, from the teacher's side. What matters is not whether it works — it does, and is tested — but whether the teacher can *satisfy themselves* it works without being told.

### Task 5 — Your own question at the end *(target: under 4 minutes)*

> You want to ask your students one question of your own when they finish — something specific to what you taught this week. Add it, then tell me where you'd expect to read the answers.

**What is being measured.** The end-of-simulation question, and specifically whether a teacher understands it is **theirs** — not scored by BOW, not part of the evidence. Watch whether they expect it to appear in the rubric. If they do, the separation is not communicated well enough, no matter how structurally real it is.

## Interview — after all five tasks, in this order

Order matters: the open questions come first, before you put any of our language in their mouth.

1. What would you use this for, in your own classroom, in your own words?
2. What would you *not* use it for?
3. Who is this for — what kind of teacher, what kind of class?
4. What was the most annoying moment?
5. If you could change one thing, what?
6. Would you use this next month? *(If yes:)* What would have to be true for you to still be using it in February?
7. **Show them the objectives page.** *"BOW can assess 1 of the 23 New York objectives right now. What's your reaction?"*
   - This is the question the whole session is for. Ask it flat, offer nothing, and write down the first sentence exactly.
8. What would you tell a colleague about it in one sentence?
9. Is there anything I should have asked you and didn't?

Do not ask whether they liked it. Nobody has ever answered that question truthfully to the person who built the thing.

## Four predictions, written before the room

Recorded now so the session can prove us wrong. Each is a claim this gauntlet has been running on, and each is falsifiable in one morning.

| | Prediction | Falsified if |
|---|---|---|
| **P1** | A teacher answers the ten-second question correctly in under ten seconds without training | 2 or more of 5 cannot |
| **P2** | Honest coverage reads as credibility, not as a broken product. *"1 of 23"* is our biggest deliberate bet: every instinct in this category is to say "aligned to NYS standards" and mean nothing by it | 2 or more of 5 react to Q7 with a version of *"so it doesn't do anything yet"* |
| **P3** | A teacher reads `—` as *not attempted*, not as *zero*. The whole rubric refuses composite scores on the strength of this | 2 or more of 5 read it as a zero, or ask and are surprised |
| **P4** | A teacher understands their own end-of-simulation question is theirs and unscored, from the screen alone | 2 or more of 5 expect it in the rubric |

**If P2 is falsified, that is the most important result this gauntlet could produce**, and it changes the product's positioning rather than a screen. Do not soften it, do not re-ask it a friendlier way, and do not average it against how nice everyone was about the simulation.

## After

Write the five sheets up the same day. A finding is *reproduced* when two teachers hit it independently; a single teacher's stumble is a lead, not a defect. Everything reproduced goes into `DEFECTS.md` under a round of its own, with the teacher's own words attached — not a paraphrase, and not our name for the thing they were looking for.

**And one line for the record.** Until this runs, no artifact in this repository may say *teacher-tested*. The claim belongs to teachers, and none has sat down yet.
