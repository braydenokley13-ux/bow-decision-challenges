# The mechanics war — sixteen designs, as submitted

Sixteen candidate Worlds, four for each NYSED module BOW cannot assess. Four independent
designers per module: three anchored on a mechanical family from the prior Mechanics Lab
(workbench, timeline, inspection desk) and one told explicitly to ignore that lab entirely.
None saw another's work, the competency file's rubrics, or this repository's own worlds beyond
a two-paragraph summary.

**They are recorded here because they existed only in a workflow journal outside the
repository.** §87 asks for the losers to be kept with the reason they lost — and the reason is
worth less without the design it is a reason about. Verdicts are added when the courts file.

**A fault in the brief, recorded because it shapes what these are worth.** Three of the four
Module 2 designs came back as a paper credit tab at a small shop, and my brief supplied that
vehicle in its own *known traps* section — *"a school or team purchase"*. The wildcard slot
was meant to be free of the prior lab and I anchored it myself. The **verbs** did diverge,
which is the axis that decides whether two worlds are one world in two costumes, so the war is
still informative; premise diversity in Module 2 was not tested and is not claimed.

## Module 2 · Credit and Debt Management

### The Tab at Ruiz Hardware

**Carries** `decide-to-borrow`, `keep-credit-costs-down`, `sort-by-need-want-goal`  
**Verbs** PLACE · TIME · PAY · PROMISE · SETTLE  
**Budget** 2250 words / 24 minutes

*The student keeps a school robotics team's paper credit tab — a long card on a hook — and decides what to buy on it, on which day, and how fast to pay it down, while a red thread and its brass beads show exactly what each choice costs in time.*

Ruiz Hardware keeps a paper tab card for the Harbor Middle robotics team, on a hook behind the
counter. You're the keeper this season. Last year's team left $80 on it, and one payment landed
late — so the shop's first-month deal is gone for good. The robot needs a drive motor before the
qualifier on day 24, and the team voted for a soldering station that's on sale until day 33.
Your money comes four times in 105 days. Everything you owe hangs on that card, in a red line
you can see.

**Signature mechanic.** A paper tab card hangs across the screen: one horizontal strip, 105 days left to right,
perforated into three statement panels. Green $25 cash chips sit on the four days the club's
money arrives (day 5 dues $100, day 47 car wash $150, day 70 bottle drive $100, day 82
concessions $75). Dashed vertical lines mark where each statement closes (days 30, 60, 90); red
DUE pins mark days 45, 75, 105. A red cotton thread already runs from the left edge — last
season's $80, inherited.
The student grabs a **purchase tag** from the rail (motor kit $150; soldering station $200
through day 33, $230 to day 60, $260 after) and hangs it on a day. The instant it lands, the
thread stretches from that day rightwards and **brass beads drop onto it — one bead per $50
still owed at each dashed line it crosses**. Beads are countable objects, never a labelled
dollar total. Slide the station tag from day 28 to day 32 and you watch it pass the dashed line
and a whole row of beads vanish; slide it past day 33 and the price on the tag's face flips to
$230. Those two lines pull opposite ways and are four days apart. That collision is the world.
Before a tag can hang, the student **flips it over**: the back is a cost card with blanks that
must be filled — the day the club's own cash would first cover it, the price on that day, what
waiting forfeits, and the total paid by the day the tab is clear. The shelf sign behind the tags
reads TAKE IT HOME TODAY — $25 A MONTH, and never says how many.
At each dashed line the student sets the payment (a stepper in the statement panel with a
notched $25 minimum and a "clear it" end stop), counts the beads and types the interest line,
and drags a **brass payoff pin** to the day they promise Ruiz the tab will be clear. Time only
advances when a statement is closed — the strip is pulled left through the counter roller with a
paper-drag. If the strip runs past the payoff pin with the thread still red, the pin turns red
and stays on the card for the rest of the run.
After the last close the card is unhooked and handed to next season's keeper: whatever thread is
still on it goes with it.

**The tradeoff.** Total cash across the season is $425. Fixed cash obligations take $130. The tab can hold $80
inherited + $150 kit + $200 station = $430 plus beads. The arithmetic is deliberately
unsurvivable in full: **you can hand on a clear card, or get the station, or get all fifteen
hoodies — not all three.**
The live tensions: placing the station earlier costs a row of beads and eats the payments that
would shrink the kit, which pushes the payoff pin out and makes the day-88 counter bite harder;
placing it later costs $30, then $60, in price steps, and it is not in the shop for build week.
Paying the tab down hard is cheap in beads and leaves nothing at day 88 for the team's promise.
**Where there is a near-dominant move, it is the lesson, and it is not free.** Day 32 beats day
28 — same price, one fewer close line — and finding it is exactly NYSED 2.3. The student has to
find it; nothing points at it. Across the real axis (station on the tab in cycle 2 vs cash at
$260 in cycle 3) neither wins outright, and `balance.ts` sweeps every placement × payment plan
and fails the build if any single station placement wins under more than 60% of priority
weightings over {money kept, station in hand for build week, promise to the team kept, card
handed on clear} — the same sweep shape that already proves no housing choice is right in
Basketball.
The motor kit is deliberately near-settled: $4–$6 of beads against a $90 forfeited entry and the
qualifier slot. That is the world's proof that credit is sometimes right, and it is not scored
as a right answer — er3 reads whether the student's placement matches the two numbers *they*
wrote, so a student who waits and records the forfeit as a cost they accept scores exactly the
same.

**Good thinking, bad outcome.** A student prices both sides of both tags correctly, spots the dashed line, hangs the station on
day 32 at $200, pays $60 on day 39 to beat the freeze, and sets an honest payoff pin at day 118
— past the end of the season, because that is what their plan truly produces. Then the day-88
counter arrives and they fund the entry and the bus and only the eight starters' hoodies, and
the team is unhappy. The run ends with a red thread still on the card, a broken promise to seven
teammates, and the pin pointing past the strip. On any surface that reported outcomes, this
student looks worse than one who bought nothing.
The evidence survives because **no evidence requirement in this world reads the final balance,
the payoff day, or whether the team got its hoodies.** C1.er1–er3 read arithmetic and the
coherence of the placement with the student's own figures. C2.er1 reads free cash against carry,
close by close. C2.er2 scores the pin against the plan *in force when it was set* — an honest
pin pointing at day 118 is a 5, and later changing the plan does not falsify it. C2.er3 records
the day-39 payment. B1.er1–er3 read reach and the truth of the reason, not which claim survived.
The teacher's screen shows twelve rows, ten of them at 5, and a run that ended hard.

**Bad thinking, good outcome.** A student taps through the cost cards with the shelf sign's $25 in one field and the sticker
price in the other, hangs both tags on day 8 without ever looking at a dashed line, sets the
minimum payment at every close, and drags the payoff pin to day 105 because it is the far end of
the strip. The club's income is generous enough that the thread does clear at day 105 anyway,
and the last screen looks fine.
The product does not report mastery. C1.er2 stored `25` and `150` verbatim — 2 and 2. C1.er3
records two placements neither of their own numbers supports, with no reason chip — 2. C2.er1
shows free cash sitting at closes 2 and 3 — 2. C2.er4 has `12` on the history line — 2, the
misconception named on the reteach card in the product's own words. C2.er3 shows a late post
followed by an identical Statement 2 payment — 2. The lucky pin scores 5 and the entry and bus
got funded, so B1.er1 scores 5.
Two 5s cannot become a mastery claim because **there is no composite anywhere in this product**.
A teacher sees twelve rows and reads seven low ones. The pin's 5 is further guarded: the
observation carries `setBeforeClose` and the plan in force, and the trail sentence beside it
says the plan it matched was minimum payments throughout — which is what the other rows are
already saying.

**Why not a button.** The signature action is hanging a purchase tag on a **day**. A button says "Buy on credit." The
strip says "Buy on day 32."
The financial fact NYSED 2.3 asks for — understanding a billing cycle — is a fact about *where
in the month a purchase falls*. There is no button label that carries it. A Buy now / Buy later
pair collapses 105 answers into two and hides the only thing that distinguishes them, and the
same purchase costing $200 on day 32 and $208 on day 28 becomes something BOW would have to
*say* rather than something the student bumps into. The moment BOW says it, its own support
rules cap that row at 3 for everyone in the room.
The thread does the other half. A balance shown as a number is an amount; a balance shown as a
line with a length and a bead every $50 per close is what a debt actually is — an interval that
charges rent. Shortening the thread and watching beads disappear is the definition of simple
interest performed by hand. And the tag's back — the terms you must physically flip it over to
fill in before the shop will let you hang it — is the honest representation of the step every
adult skips.

**Would clicking be better.** Clicking is better in two places and this design concedes both. **Payment amounts:** composing
$75 out of three chips is motor skill wearing a hat, so payments are a stepper in the statement
panel with a notched $25 minimum and a "clear it" end stop — one control, keyboard-trivial, and
the log reads identically. **Precise dates:** dragging to day 47 is slower and worse than typing
it, so every tag and the payoff pin carry a day field beside them that does exactly what the
drag does; the drag is never the only route and never the fast route.
What the drag genuinely earns is one thing a control cannot: **discovery of the close line.** It
is a visible wall you cross while moving a tag, and crossing it is unmistakable — a row of beads
vanishes under your hand. No button, dropdown or date field surfaces that unprompted. The only
alternative is BOW telling the student about billing cycles, which under this product's own
support caps scores everyone a 3 on the row the whole module exists for. The interaction is not
there to be fun. It is there so BOW does not have to give away the answer it is supposed to be
measuring.
If a class trial shows students find the line at the same rate with a plain date field, the drag
should be deleted and the strip kept.

**Accessibility path.** The construct is: a day, a length in statements, and a count of beads. All three are text, so
all three survive everywhere.
**Keyboard.** The strip is a rail of three statement panels; every tag, payment stepper and the
payoff pin is a focusable control whose value is a day. Left/Right = ±1 day, PageUp/PageDown =
±7, Home/End = the strip's ends, and `[` / `]` jump to the previous and next close line — the
dashed line is a **first-class keyboard target**, which is how a keyboard user discovers the
billing-cycle effect rather than being told about it. Every move announces the whole construct:
"Soldering station, $200, day 32, Statement 2. Owing at this close: $200. Beads: 4. Thread runs
to Statement 3." Crossing a line announces "crossed — Statement 1 closes; now on Statement 2;
beads 8 to 4."
**Screen reader.** An inline table of the same live model sits on the same screen, not behind a
link and not on a separate route: `Statement | closes | owed at close | beads | interest |
payment | due | payoff pin`. It is driven by the same `runTab()` output the thread is drawn
from, updates on the same events, and sighted keyboard users can open it too. It is not a
fallback worksheet — it is the identical numbers the thread encodes, and the "drag" is a date
stepper for every user.
**Reduced motion.** Beads appear rather than drop; the strip jumps between statements rather
than scrolling; the roller animation is a state change.
**Muted audio.** The till and the paper tear are decorative only; each close prints "Statement 2
closed — 7 beads — $7" and the late mark prints as a word, "LATE — $12".
**200% zoom and 640px.** The rail becomes a single-statement viewport with previous/next
statement controls and a sticky summary line: "Owed $234 · 4 beads at this close · payment due
day 45 · pin: day 118". Tags for that statement are listed inside the panel, each labelled with
its day, and the close line remains a control sitting between two panels. Position in time
survives because every object carries its day and its statement number in text; the picture is a
convenience, never the carrier.
**No colour-only meaning.** Beads are counted in text, the thread carries a hatch pattern, DUE
pins are labelled DUE, and the late mark is a word.

**Where it could teach something false.** **1. Interest on the closing balance.** Real cards use average daily balance; this tab charges
on what is owed when the statement closes. Guard: the card's own wording is exactly "$1 a month
for every $50 you still owe when a statement closes" — it is stated as this shop's rule, never
as how cards work. The teacher note says the simplification out loud and names average daily
balance.
**2. The rate.** $1 per $50 per month is 2% a month — about 24% a year, realistic for store
credit and higher than most cards. Guard: the teacher note states the annual equivalent, so
nobody leaves thinking 2% is small, and the world never prints an APR figure it has not earned.
**3. The intro deal.** Real intro rates are 0% APR for months, not one statement. Guard: this is
a shop tab, not a card, and the card says so. The teacher note maps it: "the shop's version of a
0% introductory rate — a card's version lasts months instead of one cycle, and ends the same
way, for the same reason."
**4. Credit reporting.** A missed payment on a real account also hits a credit report, and this
world would teach that a late payment costs only a fee and a rate. Guard: BOW says nothing about
credit reports inside the world — a neighbourhood shop tab genuinely has none — and the teacher
note names what the world therefore does *not* cover, so the omission is declared rather than
implied.
**5. Who can legally owe.** A 13-year-old cannot hold credit. Guard: the tab is in the school's
name; the student is the keeper of the card and the ledger, and the card's fifth line says the
school is responsible for what is on it. That is also how NYSED 2.1's "legal responsibilities"
gets touched honestly, rather than by pretending a minor signed something.
**6. Sunk cost.** The $90 qualifier entry is treated as a cost of *waiting* (you lose it if the
robot cannot run), which is correct, and never as a cost of *borrowing*. Guard: the cost card's
forfeit field is only on the wait side, and the observer checks it there.
**7. "Credit = bad."** Guarded structurally: borrowing is the better call twice (the kit against
a $90 forfeit; the station against $60 of price steps), the world's harm comes from *dates*
rather than from credit itself, and C1.er3 scores coherence with the student's own numbers so
neither borrowing nor waiting is ever the marked-correct answer.

**Kill condition.** If, in class trial, fewer than half of unhinted students place a purchase on the later side of a
close line — that is, if the billing-cycle discovery requires BOW to point at it — then abandon
the design rather than patch it. The whole world exists to produce that row at 5; a version that
has to scaffold it caps its own central evidence at 3 for the median student, and at that point
a two-question worksheet is cheaper and equally honest. Second kill: if the balance sweep cannot
be brought under 60% dominance for any station placement after two rounds of renumbering, the
timing decision is fake and the world is an animation with a form attached.

**What it does not prove.** Nothing about compound interest — this tab is simple interest by design and never claims
otherwise. Nothing about credit scores, credit reports or how a late payment follows a person,
because a neighbourhood shop tab has none and inventing one would teach something false. Nothing
about APR as an annual figure, or about the difference between revolving and installment credit.
Nothing about the student's own future borrowing: they are the keeper of somebody else's card,
which is the honest position for a 13-year-old and also a real limit on what the evidence means.
It does not show whether a student could originate a borrowing decision unprompted — every
purchase in this world is offered to them. And it does not show whether they can budget from
scratch: the income here is fixed, dated and given, so `plan-within-income` is deliberately not
claimed.

**Visual identity.** Take the title off and you would know it by **the long paper card on a hook**: one horizontal
strip of manila stock, warm off-white, printed with a faint day grid and torn along perforations
at each statement close, with a single red cotton thread running its length and brass beads
strung on it. Nothing else in BOW is a line.
**Environment:** a hardware-store counter — pegboard grey-green, a bin of tags on wire hooks, a
receipt spike, a rubber-mat foreground. Not a gym floor, not a night market.
**Object language:** everything the student touches is a physical shop object — a price tag you
flip over to read the back, a brass pin, a stepper that looks like a till key, a paper strip
that pulls left through a roller. Money arrives as green chips clipped to dates; debt is red
thread; the cost of time is brass.
**Typography:** a condensed grotesk on the shop signage (the TAKE IT HOME TODAY — $25 A MONTH
card), a dot-matrix/typewriter face for the tab card's printed rules and the statement panels,
and tabular monospace figures so every column of dollars and beads lines up. Numbers are the
loudest thing on screen.
**Colour:** kraft and manila card, pegboard grey-green, one red (thread, DUE pins, the late
mark), one brass (beads, payoff pin), green only for cash chips. Four colours, each meaning
exactly one thing, each also carried in text.
**Motion:** the strip advances with a paper-drag ease as it is pulled through the roller; beads
drop onto the thread one at a time at a close, with a count that ticks; a tag crossing a dashed
line snaps with a small paper catch. Under reduced motion all three become instant state changes
with the same announcements. Sound is a till and a paper tear, and carries no information the
panel does not print.

### Nothing Down

**Carries** `decide-to-borrow`, `keep-credit-costs-down`, `sort-by-need-want-goal`  
**Verbs** SPLIT · COMMIT · RUN · REPAIR  
**Budget** 2200 words / 24 minutes

*The student splits a phone-screen repair between cash and a payment plan by dragging a divider along a price bar, then runs six months of statements forward one at a time, paying from a tray of dated money that has to survive the turn of each month.*

Your phone came home in July on the shop's Pay Monthly plan. Your aunt signed it with you; you
make the payments. There is $240 left and $20 is due on the 12th of every month. Last week the
screen cracked. The shop will fix it for $130 today, or $180 once the crack reaches the edge,
which they say is December. They will also add the repair straight onto your plan for a flat
$20. You have $80 in the tray and six statements ahead of you.

**Signature mechanic.** THE BENCH (one screen, then it becomes the rail).
On screen: a pale paper strip on a dark desk with a printed scale along its top edge — $10 = one
notch. Everything in this world is a length on that ruler.
1. THE PRICE BAR. A 13-notch bar labelled "Screen repair $130". Behind it, ghosted, an 18-notch
bar labelled "$180 from December".
2. THE DIVIDER. The student grabs a divider inside the $130 bar and drags. Left of it is PAY AT
THE COUNTER, right is ADD TO THE PLAN. Dragging moves the same length from one side to the other
— that is literally what borrowing is: identical length, paid at a different time. As the
divider moves left, $10 coins physically leave the CASH TRAY below and stack under the left
segment. The divider hits a hard stop at $80, labelled "all your cash". You cannot drag past it;
there is no error message because there is no error state.
3. THE FEE TAB. The instant any length sits on the plan side, a small orange block appears past
the bar's right edge: "+$20 to add it". It does not change size when the divider moves. That one
non-scaling block is the whole flat-fee lesson, visible rather than told.
4. THE WAIT RAIL, bottom left. A calendar strip Oct–Dec with the money card's chips. The student
drags a save handle to set how much to hold back each month; the fill grows right toward the
$130 bar and reports the week it reaches it. A red flag sits at the end of November. If the fill
crosses the flag, the target bar it is measured against grows from 13 notches to 18 — waiting
past December makes the thing longer, felt on the ruler.
5. THREE SUM TICKETS. Small paper tickets the student writes a figure on and slides into slots
on the bench: "what the plan costs on top" ($20), "what waiting costs on top" ($50), "what the
tray is left with". A wrong ticket does not seat: the bench names the line on the terms card it
disagrees with (natural consequence, caps at 4). After two attempts a step-by-step opens (direct
scaffold, caps at 3).
6. THE COMMIT SLIP. A slip with three lines the arrangement has already filled in (route, amount
on the plan, cash left in the tray) and one blank line the student writes: "Why this and not the
other one?" The slot is the shape of a filled slip — it will not take a slip with a blank line.
Pushing it in is the commit. The bench locks.
AFTER THE COMMIT the same board is the RAIL: the price bar is gone, replaced by one graphite
balance block at the left of six month cards. Each card carries money-in chips at their dates
(the 5th, the 20th), fixed costs as small dark blocks, a perforation notch at the 12th, and a
payment well with a floor line at $20. The student drags $5 and $10 coins from the tray into the
well. Coins that came from the chip arriving on the 20th sit visibly on the right of the notch —
a late payment is a position, not a checkbox. Press RUN THE MONTH: the payment leaves, the
balance block shortens by exactly that length, the statement prints, and whatever is left in the
tray CARRIES to the next card. The tray never resets, which is the entire billing-cycle lesson:
money has to survive the turn of the month or you cannot make the 12th. A month that has run
cannot be rewound. A grey ghost tail past the right edge shows how many further months the
balance needs at the current payment size; at the floor it runs off the board and the board re-
labels its scale.

**The tradeoff.** The $20 fee is FLAT, so the divider does not change what credit costs in money at all — $130
paid any way costs $150. What it trades is exposure against liquidity. Drag it to the all-cash
stop and the balance is small and the interest exposure is near nothing, but the tray is empty
and only $15 of December's money exists before the 12th, so the ended intro lands on a student
who cannot pay on time. Drag it to nothing-down and the tray is intact and every notch is
makeable, but you carry a $150 larger balance into the month interest starts. Waiting is a
genuine third pole: no fee, no obligation, no exposure, and $50 more plus two months of a
spreading crack. Three defensible poles, same order of money, different risk shapes.
On the rail the trade is that money paid onto the balance is money not in the tray on the 12th
of the following month, and the December card puts $100 against $190 of claims.
IS THERE A DOMINANT LINE? In pure money, yes, and I will not pretend otherwise: nothing down,
maximum payment every month, the whole $100 onto the balance in December is the cheapest
possible run. Three things stop it being the answer. (a) No evidence requirement reads money —
not the ending balance, not the total paid, not which route was taken. (b) The December claims
are built so the money-optimal line is not obviously optimal: the $60 owed to your sister is
owed either way, so skipping it defers rather than saves, and what is actually being traded
against $35 of interest is $35 of a thing you wanted and a promise to a person, neither of which
this product prices. (c) The settle screen states the cheapest possible run out loud, as a fact,
and the world never scores against it — the same stance `balance.ts` already takes for the pop-
up's booth spots.

**Good thinking, bad outcome.** A student drags the divider to $60 at the counter, keeping $20 in the tray, finances $70 plus
the $20 fee, writes all three sums correctly, and explains on the slip that the fee is flat so
putting cash down buys nothing except a smaller balance to be exposed on, and that they are
keeping $20 back for the 12th. That is better reasoning than most adults produce. Then
December's autopay fails — it fails for everyone — and $20 in the tray is not enough to both
absorb the $29 and clear the notch. They finish the six months still owing money, worse off than
a classmate who put nothing down out of pure inertia.
The evidence survives because not one of the fourteen requirements reads the ending balance, the
total paid, or which route was chosen. er1 reads whether both routes were built, er2 and er3
read named sums against a terms card that never changed, er4 reads what the tray held against
what was due on the next 12th at the moment of commit, er5 reads a sentence written before any
month had run. On the rail, er1 and er3 read each month's decision against the money that
existed when it was made, er4 reads one arithmetic ticket, and er5 reads the repair against what
was actually available afterwards — which explicitly permits "could not clear it, and here is
what is still owed" as a level 5. The shock changes the story. It cannot change a row. The
settle screen reports money; the evidence trail reports evidence; they are different surfaces
and there is no composite that could average one into the other.

**Bad thinking, good outcome.** A student who believes the monthly payment is the price puts nothing down, keeps all $80, pays
the $20 floor every month because "that's what it asks for", spends the rest, and in December
puts the whole $100 onto the balance because it is the biggest number on the card. The autopay
failure is absorbed by the $100 and the run ends with the balance nearly clear and a cheerful
settle screen.
The product cannot report mastery, because the rows that fired are: decide-to-borrow er2 at 2
(wrote "$25 a month" as what the plan costs, with the misconception "the monthly payment is the
price" attached by name); keep-credit-costs-down er1 at 2 (three months at the floor with money
idle); er3 at 2 or 0 (paid from the 20th chip in months where they had held nothing back); er4
at 2 (wrote the fee-only integer and left it, carrying "one late payment is one late fee"); er2
at 2 or 0 (never used the minimum-only box). Those 2s stand as themselves — there is no
composite anywhere in this product to dissolve them into an average, and the teacher's card
names both misconceptions verbatim.
The luck channel is closed at the source: the $100 in December is printed on the money card from
the first screen and is planning information, not a bailout, and nothing in the world is
randomised. What is left is not luck at all — it is a student whose ending looks fine and whose
four scored rows say exactly what is wrong with them.

**Why not a button.** The financial construct is money as length that has to be conserved across a boundary in time. A
button labelled PAY $50 destroys both halves of that: it hides which money paid — the chip that
arrived on the 5th or the one that arrives on the 20th — and it hides what is left behind.
Dragging a coin from the tray into the well makes source and residue simultaneous: the tray gets
shorter, the balance gets shorter, and what remains in the tray is the thing that has to survive
to the 12th of the next month. No button can show a residue, and no numeric field can show that
the money you would use arrives after the due date.
The divider is the sharper case. FINANCE IT with a cost readout beside it is precisely the
mistake this module exists to correct: it presents the cost of credit as a fact you are told.
The divider presents the same length moving between "paid now" and "paid later" — which is what
borrowing literally is — and puts a $20 block on the end that visibly does not change size as
you move it. A student who drags that divider from stop to stop has discovered that the fee does
not scale. A student who read "$20 fee" in a modal has been informed of it, which is a different
thing and does not stick.
And the notch. A checkbox reading "pay on time" is vocabulary labelling dressed as assessment. A
perforation at the 12th, with your coins sitting visibly to the right of it because the money
did not arrive until the 20th, is a billing cycle.

**Would clicking be better.** Honestly argued, clicking wins in three places and I have conceded all three. The payment AMOUNT
should not be set by pixel-dragging — hitting exactly $65 with a mouse is motor skill wearing a
finance costume — so coins are $5 and $10 denominations you drop or type, and the meaning lives
in the drag's source and destination, never in its precision. Choosing the repayment term,
picking one of the four reason ids, and writing the slip are taps and typing, because there is
no truthful representation of "which of four sentences do you mean". RUN THE MONTH is a button
and should be, because committing a month irreversibly is genuinely an act of pressing
something.
The case for making the whole thing buttons: a form with "amount down: $__", "monthly payment:
$__" and a table of six months would take four minutes instead of twenty-four, would be
trivially accessible, and would emit the same event log for most of the requirements. That is a
real argument and it is why most of this genre is a worksheet.
It fails on exactly two facts the form cannot hold, and both are load-bearing. First, the fee
tab that does not resize — a form would have to print "the fee is flat" and a student would skim
it; on the bar it is the only thing that stays still while everything moves, and that is a
discovery. Second, which side of the notch the money sat on. A form asks how much you paid; the
board records that you paid it with money that arrived on the 20th, and that single fact is what
makes NYSED 2.3's billing cycle assessable at all rather than quizzable. If either of those two
could survive in a form, I would build the form.

**Accessibility path.** There is ONE board. The keyboard path and the pointer path drive the same reducer and emit byte-
identical event logs — a transportParity-style test asserts a full keyboard run is
indistinguishable from a full pointer run. No worksheet fallback exists, so none can be
inferior.
KEYBOARD. Every container — cash tray, each month's payment well, each claim tray, the divider —
is a focus stop in a roving-tabindex grid. Moving money is: Space to pick up a coin, arrows to a
container, Space to drop. The divider is role="slider", arrows step $5, Home/End go to the
stops, and the stop at $80 is a real boundary the key cannot cross. Sum tickets are the shipped
CalculationInput. Nothing requires a drag, a hover, a gesture, or a timed action — there are no
timers anywhere in this world.
SCREEN READER. The lengths carry their values as text on the same elements, not in a parallel
view. A coin announces "$10, from the chip on the 20th". A well announces "Payment well,
December. $45 in it. Floor $20. Due on the 12th. This money arrived after the 12th." The balance
block carries aria-label="Balance $390". The fee tab is a listitem: "Fee to add it, $20, does
not change." The ghost tail is a polite live region that speaks only on RUN: "At $20 a month
this takes 21 more months and costs $412 in total." Every relationship the geometry expresses —
before/after the notch, longer/shorter than the price bar, the overhang — is also stated in
words on the element itself. Nothing is carried by colour or by position alone.
REDUCED MOTION. No transition is load-bearing. The board's re-scale when the tail runs off is a
re-label, not a zoom. The statement appears rather than slides.
MUTED AUDIO. There is no audio on the critical path.
200% ZOOM AND 640px. The board reflows to a single column of month cards. The ruler's scale
stays $10 = one notch at any width because the notch is a CSS custom property, and the
comparison that matters is preserved by keeping the balance block and the current month's card
adjacent in DOM order rather than by keeping them side by side on screen. The rail scrolls
inside its own overflow-x container; the page body never scrolls sideways.
WHY THE CONSTRUCT SURVIVES rather than merely the interface: the construct is "money is a
conserved quantity moving between named containers across dated boundaries", and every one of
those words — quantity, container, date, boundary — is a first-class labelled object with a text
value, not a picture. The picture is a second rendering of facts that are already in the
accessibility tree.

**Where it could teach something false.** 1. "Simple interest" said loosely. $2 per $100 each month on a declining balance is monthly
simple interest on the amount outstanding. It is not APR, and because the charge is added to the
balance it does attract charge next month — which is how cards actually work and is not what
"simple interest" strictly means. GUARD: the word APR appears nowhere; the statement states the
rule in the words above; the teacher note says plainly that this world charges monthly on what
is owed, interest included, and does not claim to model APR.
2. The rounding. "$2 for every $100 OR PART OF $100" is generous rounding chosen so a 13-year-
old can do it in their head. GUARD: it is printed on the statement, not hidden, and a student
who computes the exact percentage instead gets a natural-consequence ticket response ("the
statement rounds up to the next $100 — check that line") rather than a wrong answer. Being right
by a different rule must not score as being wrong.
3. Order of operations. Fee first, then interest, gives a different integer from interest first
— and er4 hinges on an integer. GUARD: one printed line on the statement fixes the order, and
the observer's expected value is derived from that line rather than hard-coded.
4. The flat $20 add-on. Real add-on and origination fees are often a percentage. GUARD: the
terms card says "$20 to add it, whatever the amount" as a term of THIS shop. The settle never
generalises it, and the ending never says "fees are flat".
5. "One late payment ends the intro." True of many real agreements, not all. GUARD: sourced to
the agreement on screen; the settle says "your agreement said", never "credit cards do".
6. Teaching credit = bad. The biggest risk in this module and the reason for the flat fee:
because the fee does not scale, borrowing $70 and borrowing $130 cost the same $20, so "borrow
as little as possible" is NOT automatically right, and the settle must be able to say that a
student who financed the lot to keep $80 of cushion, and used that cushion to clear every notch,
did something sensible. GUARD: the settle itemises what each call cost in money and does not
rank the routes; an endingParity test asserts no route's ending is written as the good one.
7. Dignity. The crack is an accident, the autopay failure is an expired card on the co-signer's
account, the money is modest but adequate, and "don't repair it" is presented as a real and
respectable option rather than a failure. Nobody in this world is poor and nobody is blamed.

**Kill condition.** If a keyboard-only run cannot carry the "which chip, which side of the notch" fact without a
second, separate control, the billing-cycle construct has degenerated into a checkbox and I
abandon the notch entirely — and with it er3, and with it any honest claim on NYSED 2.3. I would
kill the design before shipping a "paid on time ✓" tickbox with a ruler drawn behind it.
The second kill: if pilot timing puts the bench above six minutes, the whole thing exceeds a
class period once the rail is added, and the bench is the part that cannot be cut without losing
decide-to-borrow. At that point the honest move is to split the world in two Challenges rather
than compress the reading, and a two-Challenge design is not what was asked for here.

**What it does not prove.** It does not prove the student understands APR, compounding, credit scores, credit reports,
secured versus unsecured credit, or revolving versus instalment structure — none of those appear
and none should be claimed.
It does not prove transfer to a real credit card. The student manipulates one agreement with
printed terms and generous rounding; a real cardholder faces variable rates, grace periods and
statements designed to be skimmed.
It does not close NYSED 2.3 or 2.4. keep-credit-costs-down ships with explanationRequired:
false, and both objectives' verbs are "Explain" and "Describe", so the mapping table holds them
at partial no matter how well this world runs. This world takes the module from zero of four to
2.1 (bundle complete — this world produces all of decide-to-borrow, all of keep-credit-costs-
down, and all four rows of the already-available sort-by-need-want-goal) and 2.2 (full, via
decide-to-borrow), with 2.3 and 2.4 covered partially and honestly. Lifting those two to full
requires either a required explanation row on C2 or a fourth competency, and both are content-
owner decisions, not a designer's.
It does not prove the student would do any of this with their own money.

**Visual identity.** Take the title off and a reviewer knows it in one second, because every quantity in this world
is a LENGTH ON A PRINTED RULER and the ruler reads as a till roll.
ENVIRONMENT: one long pale paper strip lying on a dark walnut desk, daylight coming through a
shop window from the left. Basketball is court-wood and warm sodium light; the market is night-
blue and string lights; this is daylight on paper. No screens inside the screen, no dashboards,
no charts.
OBJECT LANGUAGE: a printed scale along the strip's top edge with $10 notches. $5 and $10 coins
as slightly worn discs. The balance as one solid graphite block that only ever gets shorter.
Exactly one orange element in the entire world — the fee tab — and it is the only thing that
never changes size. One red element — the flag at the end of November, and the late line on a
statement. Month cards separated by a perforation cut at the 12th, so the due date is a physical
tear in the paper. The commit slip is the only piece of paper with a handwriting line on it.
TYPOGRAPHY: a monospaced figure face for all money, so digits and lengths line up column-true
down the six month cards; a warm humanist face for the shop's voice and the aunt's. Nothing is
set in a game font.
COLOUR: paper cream, graphite, one orange, one red. Four values, and two of them are reserved
for cost and lateness, so colour carries meaning and never decoration.
MOTION: nothing in this world moves except money, and money only moves horizontally, because
everything here is either a length or a date. Under reduced motion nothing moves at all and
nothing is lost.

### The Tab at Ridgeline

**Carries** `decide-to-borrow`, `keep-credit-costs-down`, `sort-by-need-want-goal`  
**Verbs** BORROW · PAY · CARRY · SETTLE  
**Budget** 2300 words / 25 minutes

*The student runs a bike-repair side job on a shop tab for four monthly statements: they decide whether to borrow $240 for a tool that earns, then choose what to pay each cycle while interest, a late fee and a rate change land on whatever balance they left behind.*

You fix bikes in your garage. Ridgeline Cycles lets you run a tab — take the parts now, pay when
the statement comes. You already owe $70 on it. In the window there is a repair stand, $240, and
with it you could take the jobs you keep turning down. Ridgeline will sell it to you for $66 a
month for four months. Four statements between now and the end of the season. What you owe, and
what owing it costs you, is yours to work out.

**Signature mechanic.** THE STATEMENT STRIP AND THE CHIP COLUMN. One wide 2D scene at counter height, unchanged all
world. Left: your cash as a column of brass $5 chips, total printed under it. Centre: this
month's statement — a paper strip about 300px tall standing on the counter, dot-matrix printed.
The strip is divided into stacked segments, bottom to top, IN THE ORDER RIDGELINE TAKES MONEY:
any late fee (red sticker) at the floor, then the fixed plan installment (green) if you took the
plan, then the tab balance (blue), the tallest. Heights are to scale at $1 = 1px, so $186 of tab
towers over a $10 fee. A thin white line crosses the strip at the minimum, with "$31 minimum"
printed in the margin. A due-date clip sits at the top.
You pay by moving one value. Drag chips onto the strip; or arrow keys ±$5, PageUp/Down ±$25,
Home = $0, End = pay in full; or type into the number field that lives under the strip and is
always visible. All routes write the same PAYMENT_SET event. As the value rises the strip fills
with brass FROM THE BOTTOM, so you watch your money clear the fee, cross the installment, and
only then start eating the blue — and the chip column shortens by the identical height at the
same instant, so the two objects read as one conserved quantity moving across the counter. One
live sentence sits under the strip and is also the aria-valuetext, verbatim: "$65 — clears the
fee and the plan payment, leaves $121 on the tab. Interest next month at 3%: $3.63."
Then CLOSE THE CYCLE (a button; some things should be buttons). Chips go in the drawer. If the
strip filled completely it stamps PAID and drops onto the spike — a brass spindle at the right
where every cleared statement stacks, so the season's history is a physical pile you can count.
If anything is left, a red segment GROWS ONTO THE TOP of the blue at the same $1=1px scale — a
big balance visibly grows, a small one barely does — and the whole remaining strip slides to the
back wall and becomes the opening height of next month's statement. Screen after: an emptier
chip column, one more page on the spike or one taller strip waiting at the back wall, and the
brass rate dial on the wall still reading 1%.
The same shop printer does the borrow beat. Ask what the plan is and it prints FOUR SLIPS, each
reading $66, and hangs them on the counter rail. It never prints their sum. That withholding is
the mechanic, not decoration: the total is the student's to make.

**The tradeoff.** Three doors, none dominant, and the numbers are what make that true rather than the framing.
WAIT: costs $0 in credit, forgoes roughly $50 a month of repair work for three months, and you
still do not own the stand when the season ends. PLAN: costs exactly $24, known in advance,
requires no judgement — and the $66 is rigid, arriving in the squeeze month whether or not the
jobs did. TAB: cheapest of the three if you kill it fast (about $13 of interest at $90/month)
and the worst of the three if you drift (the 20% minimum is $62, which feels like an
instruction, and 3% from month 3 on a drifting balance ends the season with $120 still owed and
more coming). So the tab is the highest-expected-value line AND the highest-variance one, and it
is only better than the plan for a student who then does the thing this world is measuring.
There is a second, quieter tradeoff inside keep-credit-costs-down that stops "always pay in
full" being the virtuous answer: paying the $70 tab in full in month 1 leaves $30 in the chip
column right before the stand decision, while paying the minimum leaves $85. Liquidity has a
price and so does interest. ER1 is written so that money held back and then actually spent on
something the world offered is not scored as a failure to pay down.
Second offer, month 3, at the tightest moment: the team hoodie, $35, "or four payments of $10."
Taking it is correct for a student who cleared the tab early and has room, and is the "monthly
payment is the price" misconception walking into daylight for a student who has not.

**Good thinking, bad outcome.** A student computes $264, names the $24, works out that waiting means month 5 and about $150 of
repair work forgone, and then reasons: only three jobs a month are actually booked, and $66 is a
promise that arrives whether or not the fourth person shows up. They take the WAIT door. The
seeded draw then gives five jobs in month 3 and five in month 4 — a classmate who borrowed ends
the season owning the stand and with more cash. Our student ends poorer and right.
The evidence is untouched by that. ER1–ER3 are their own arithmetic, all correct, all unaided:
5, 5, 5. ER4 reads their committed outgo against the reliable floor, and theirs sits inside it:
5. ER5 is scored on whether the sentence names a door, cites a figure they made, and turns on
what the thing does — the rubric never asks which door. keep-credit-costs-down still produces in
full, because the tab exists on every path and the four statements happen to everyone. The
teacher's screen carries five 5s, their sentence, and no statement anywhere about who ended
richer, because there is no composite and the closing screen prints the season's numbers rather
than a verdict.

**Bad thinking, good outcome.** A student reads "$66 a month", sees $105 coming in, and takes the plan in nine seconds. At the
gate they type $240 for plan-total (the price is the price) and $0 for cost of credit. They pay
the tab minimum every cycle because it is the number printed largest. The seeded draw gives them
five jobs twice, so they clear everything, keep the stand, and finish with the most cash in the
class.
Nothing reports mastery. ER1 is $240 against $264 — level 2 with 'The monthly payment is the
price' on the reteach card. ER2 is $0 — level 0 with 'Credit is free if I pay it back'. ER5's
sentence quotes the monthly payment as the price and a person scoring it can see that in one
read. keep-credit-costs-down ER1 is 0: every cycle had slack and every payment was the minimum.
ER3's miss-cost comes back as $10 against a live balance — 0, with 'One late payment is one late
fee'. Their good ending is a fact about the seed, and the seed is in the log; not one rubric row
anywhere reads the ending. The closing screen shows what they paid Ridgeline and what of it
bought nothing, which is $24 plus a fee plus interest they never priced.

**Why not a button.** Two answers, for two different actions.
The payment. PAY MINIMUM and PAY IN FULL as a pair of buttons reproduces the exact framing that
causes the misconception: it presents $31 and $186 as two options of equal standing, which is
what the shop's own statement design already does to adults. The strip refuses that. The minimum
is a thin line low on a tall bar, so its relation to the balance is seen and not asserted; the
payment is a magnitude on a continuum, so the interesting answers between the notch and the top
exist at all; the interest has somewhere to attach, so "less left grows less" is watched rather
than read; and Ridgeline's allocation order — fee, then installment, then tab — is invisible in
a number field and unmissable in a bar that fills from the floor.
The plan. A BUY ON CREDIT button hides the manufactured obligation; a terms table that prints
"Total $264" does the arithmetic that IS the competency. The printer prints four slips reading
$66 and does not add them. The representation is the deliberate withholding of a sum, which is
the one thing a button cannot be.

**Would clicking be better.** Argue the other side properly. For setting a payment, a plain number field labelled with
"minimum $31 / balance $186" is cheaper to build, faster to use, precise, keyboard-native, and
free of motor skill. It carries the numbers. Most of what I claim for the bar is available to a
student who reads two figures carefully. And drag is genuinely worse for the third of a class
who will fumble it.
So I concede the drag. The number field is the canonical input and is present at every moment,
not a fallback; chips and arrow keys write the same event. What I do not concede is the bar,
because the misconception being trapped is about PROPORTION, and a number field is exactly the
surface on which "the minimum is the expected payment" survives — students pay the minimum
because it is printed largest and looks like what is being asked, which is true in a field and
false against a notch on a tall strip. The bar is the intervention; the drag is an affordance.
And I use plain buttons wherever the button is honestly better: choosing a door, taking or
declining the hoodie offer, closing the cycle. Turning a commitment into a drag would be
theatre. Only the two things whose meaning is a magnitude or a withheld sum get a
representation.

**Accessibility path.** The construct is four things: a balance (a magnitude), a payment (a magnitude the student
chooses), a minimum (a floor somebody else set), and growth proportional to what is left. All
four survive in the same DOM every user gets.
The statement is a figure whose visible geometry is aria-hidden and whose segments are a real
ordered list underneath it, built from the same values the bar is drawn from: "Statement 3. Late
fee $10. Plan payment $66. Tab balance $186. Minimum payment $31. Due in 6 days." Nobody gets a
different document.
The payment is one value behind a paired range and number input. Arrows ±$5, PageUp/Down ±$25,
Home $0, End pay in full, or type it. aria-valuetext is the same live sentence sighted users
read under the strip — "$65 — clears the fee and the plan payment, leaves $121 on the tab.
Interest next month at 3%: $3.63." That sentence is the construct, and it is the primary
surface, not a description of one.
Reduced motion: the growth animation is decoration. The red segment appears, and role="status"
announces "Interest added: $3.63. New balance $124.63." Muted audio: the rate dial's clunk is
mirrored by a persistent wall card reading "Season rate: 3% a month (was 1%)" and by the same
status region. Segments are distinguished by label and by fill pattern, never by hue alone. At
200% and at 640px the scene stacks: the strip becomes a full-width horizontal meter, the segment
list is unchanged, the chip column becomes a total with a count. Nothing anywhere is a worksheet
version of something else.

**Where it could teach something false.** 1% and 3% are MONTHLY, and a world that lets a child think 3% is small has taught something
false. Guard: every surface says "a month" in the same breath ("3% of what is left, every
month"), and the teacher page states the annual equivalents plainly — 12% and 36% — and says
that real card penalty rates run near the second number.
Real store financing is often DEFERRED interest, which is retroactive and much worse. This world
uses simple, non-retroactive interest charged once per cycle on the balance after the payment
posts. Guard: the statement's flap says exactly that, and the teacher page names the
simplification and says deferred-interest offers exist and behave differently.
An installment plan whose payment cannot be reduced is true of installment credit and false of
revolving credit. Guard: the tab door is the contrast, in the same world, at the same moment —
that is what the three doors are for.
The interest here is charged on the closing balance, not on an average daily balance. Guard:
stated on the flap; the teacher page notes real cards usually use average daily balance and that
this makes the world's arithmetic simpler, not different in direction.
A single missed payment triggering a fee AND a rate change is real in many agreements but is not
universal. Guard: it is Ridgeline's posted term on the wall card from the first screen, framed
as this shop's rule, never as how credit works.
Credit scores: a 13-year-old has none, and a shop tab does not report. The world says nothing
about scores, and the teacher page says why.
Last one, and it is the risk a reviewer should press on: month 3's late fee is imposed on
everyone. It must never be narrated as the student's failure. The notice is about Ridgeline
changing when its cycle closes, and the resolution screen is explicit that the interesting thing
is what it cost each student, which for an empty bar is $10 and nothing else.

**Kill condition.** Playtest the payment control first and cheapest, before any art. Instrument the distribution of
PAYMENT_SET values across cycles in a class of thirty. If the values are bimodal at the minimum
and the full balance — if the middle of the strip is empty — the bar is a slower version of two
buttons, and I abandon the signature mechanic and ship a number field with the projection
sentence, keeping the printer and the slips, which is where the rest of the design's weight
actually sits. Second kill condition, equally cheap to test: read thirty month-3 responses. If
students consistently describe the imposed late fee as the world being unfair rather than as
something that cost them a specific amount, the miss has to be re-sourced and the design's ER3
rebuilt around it.

**What it does not prove.** It does not prove a student can read a real credit card agreement, compute an APR, handle
compound interest, work with a credit limit, or manage debt across years rather than a season.
It does not observe habit — one season of four cycles is four data points, and a student who
paid in full four times has shown four decisions, not a disposition. It does not assess building
a budget from nothing: plan-within-income is deliberately not claimed here, and the world hands
the student their income rather than asking them to plan it. It says nothing about credit
reporting, secured borrowing, or borrowing from a person rather than a business. And the sort-
by-need-want-goal evidence it produces is about one squeeze at one moment with three claims —
real evidence, and one instance of it.

**Visual identity.** Take the title away and a reviewer knows it in one frame. It is the only world that is a single
unbroken 2D scene at counter height, held for twenty-five minutes — no map, no calendar, no
board. Warm sodium light on a dark blue-green room, closing time, the shop behind the counter in
shadow.
The object language is paper and brass, and the rule is legible without being explained: IF IT
IS MONOSPACED, RIDGELINE PRINTED IT; IF IT IS NOT, IT IS YOURS. Statements, plan slips, the
late-fee sticker and the wall card are dot-matrix on perforated paper strips. Every prompt
addressed to the student is the app's humanist sans. Money you hold is brass — a column of
chips, never a number floating in space. Money you owe is a printed strip standing upright.
Colour carries meaning and never carries it alone: tab balance ink-blue solid, plan installment
green hatched, interest red dotted, fees red-on-white stickers, cleared statements grey-green on
the spike. The spike is the world's memory — four pages tall by the end, or one.
Motion is close to zero, which is the identity. Two things ever move: the red interest segment
growing onto the top of a strip, and the brass rate dial on the back wall, which moves exactly
once in the entire world, in month 3, and never moves back.

### The Tab at Ferro's

**Carries** `decide-to-borrow`, `keep-credit-costs-down`, `sort-by-need-want-goal`  
**Verbs** INSPECT · COMPARE · TRACE · SETTLE  
**Budget** 2200 words / 24 minutes

*The student pulls the terms out of three ways to buy the same bike, prices what each one really costs including the wait, recommends one, then makes six months of payments on the account and traces the late fee that was already sitting on it.*

Your bike died Tuesday — the frame, not the tire. Five dogs on your route, and two of them are
across town: without a bike you keep three and lose twelve dollars a week. A new one at Ferro's
is $120. You have $92. Ferro has three ways to get it today, written on three forms, and a spike
on the counter with the rest of your paper on it — the tab you already have here, two payments
left on your headphones, the note you wrote your cousin. Ray signs. You read.

**Signature mechanic.** THE PEG RAIL. Top third of the screen: one document open at a time, from a spike of six papers —
Ferro's Payment Plan, Ferro's Tab Agreement, the Wait Slip, your June tab statement, the
headphone split slip, the note to your cousin. Every offer form is the same six-line form filled
differently, so the work is comparison, not hunting. Bottom two-thirds: a steel parts rail, a
real 4-row x 3-column table. Columns are the three ways to get the bike. Rows are four plain
questions: WHAT YOU PAY EACH MONTH / HOW LONG YOU OWE / WHAT HAPPENS IF YOU'RE LATE / WHAT IT
COSTS YOU IN THE END.
The student drags a line out of the open form. It tears along a perforation into a chip carrying
its own words and its source ("Tab Agreement, line 5"), and hooks into a cell. The rail does NOT
validate — a line hooked to the wrong question hooks anyway. What the rail does instead is read
across: the row now shows three answers to the same question in the three documents' own words,
and when they are in different units ("$22 a month" | "$10 is the least we take" | "nothing")
the mismatch is on screen and unresolved. Rows 1-3 are hooked. Row 4 is not hookable at all: it
is three blanks the student types, and the totals are theirs — 6 x $22 + $9 for the plan; $120
for the tab and $120 + $2.60 x 6 if it isn't cleared; and for WAIT a number no document on the
desk contains, because the six weeks of route money is on the route card and nothing tells you
to fetch it.
After row 4, a rubber stamp appears. The student stamps one column RECOMMEND, places a brass
flag on a punch-card payoff strip ("clear by month ___"), and writes two to four sentences on
the carbon slip. The screen after: nine chips hooked with visible provenance, three totals in
red grease-pencil, a stamped column, a flagged card, and a carbon copy going onto the spike. Red
is used for nothing the product writes — every red mark on the desk is the student's hand.
Act 2 keeps the same desk. Each month a statement arrives (a form carrying the minimum-payment
warning box real US statements carry), the student writes an amount on the perforated stub and
spikes it, and the spike of paid stubs is the audit trail the trace beat later needs.

**The tradeoff.** Three live tensions, none with a dominant answer. (1) Certainty against price: the Plan is $141
no matter what you do; the Tab is $120 if you clear it in six months and $170-plus if you drift
to the minimum — and the evidence about whether you will clear it is the April late mark sitting
on this very account. (2) Cash cost against opportunity cost: waiting costs $0 in interest and
$72 of route money you will not earn, and a student whose own carry figure shows $22 a month
does not reliably fit is right to wait and can say so. (3) In Act 2, paying the tab down fast is
cheapest and is exactly what makes month 3 impossible, because the cousin's $45 and the trip's
$20 land in the same month as a low route week.
The near-dominant strategy is "pay the maximum every month", and month 3 is what stops it: it
makes a fast payer break a promise to a person. The other near-dominant strategy is "never
borrow", and the route card is what stops it — waiting is the most expensive column on the rail,
if you count. Clearing the tab by month 6 is possible but requires pre-paying in months 1-2 and
giving up one month of the camp fund; it is not reachable by a student who pays a flat amount
and hopes.

**Good thinking, bad outcome.** A student opens the June statement, traces the April mark, totals the rail correctly, works out
that clearing the tab needs $35 a month and that their carry figure leaves $46, and stamps TAB
with the flag on month 6. The reasoning is right and the arithmetic is right. Then month 3 lands
on a seeded low route week with the cousin's $45 and the trip's $20 in it, they pay the tab $10,
the promotion purchase does not clear by month 6, and the bike costs $135.60 instead of $120.
The run resolves against them.
The evidence survives because no requirement is keyed to the ending balance. C1 er1-er4 are read
off the rail, the carry strip and the flag, all committed before month 1 exists. C2 er1-er3 are
already in the log. er4 asks what they did after the miss, which is exactly where this student
is strongest. And er5's rule ends "or how much is left and what finishing it will cost" — a
student who lands short and states the remainder correctly scores 5 on it. The only place the
outcome enters the record at all is the closing screen, which puts their desk total beside what
it came to and scores neither against the other.

**Bad thinking, good outcome.** A student never opens the spike. They hook lines in document order, type $22 into the plan's
end-cost cell (the monthly payment is the price), type $120 into the wait cell, stamp WAIT
because borrowing is bad, and coast: no bike payments, no fees, and the run ends with nothing
owed on the bike. It looks like a clean finish.
Nothing reports mastery, for four structural reasons rather than judgemental ones. There is no
composite score on any surface, so "finished with no debt" is not a number anywhere. Every
decision requirement is keyed to an artifact of reasoning — chip provenance, typed arithmetic,
marks on a trace — and this student's rail is wrong in four cells and two totals. The stamp
itself is read by no decision requirement at all, so a lucky one-in-three guess earns nothing;
only the written justification touches the call, and a person reads it beside the rail. And the
wait path does not escape Act 2: the $90 tab with the April mark exists on every path, so C2
er1, er2, er3 and er5 all still run. What this student avoids is a second missed payment, not
the assessment.

**Why not a button.** The button version of the signature action is "Which offer costs least? (A) (B) (C)", or a grid
of dropdowns pre-loaded with the facts. Both fail for the same reason: the extraction is the
objective. NYSED 2.1 says examine fees and repayment terms; finding the $9 processing fee on
line 5 of a form that has no fee heading IS the skill, and a dropdown whose options are already
the extracted facts has performed it for the student. The rail's chips carry a source, so the
log records where the student looked and what they thought that line answered. A dropdown
records only the answer.
And the empty cell only works in a document world. Row 4 of the WAIT column can be filled by no
line on any offer; the number lives on the route card. In a dropdown UI that number would have
to appear in an option list, which gives away that waiting has a cost at all. The rail can leave
a hole and say nothing, and the hole is the single best piece of evidence in the world.

**Would clicking be better.** Honestly: clicking would be better for part of this, and clicking is what we shipped. The drag
is not load-bearing. What is load-bearing is binding one specific line of one specific document
to one specific question, and on the keyboard that act is a menu — a button. So the pointer drag
and the keyboard menu are the same verb with two affordances, not a rich version and a fallback,
and the drop targets are whole cells, so no motor precision is being measured.
The stronger case against the design is the desk itself: three forms plus a spike of six papers
is reading load, and reading load is this stance's known failure mode. The mitigation is
structural, not cosmetic — all three offer forms are the same six-line form filled differently,
so a student learns the form once and thereafter compares line 4 to line 4, and the whole
critical path is counted at about 2,200 words. If playtest shows students reading the forms
linearly instead of across, the mechanic has failed and no polish saves it; that is the kill
condition.
What we refused is the button that answers the question. The rail never validates a hook, never
ranks a column, never computes a total and never reorders itself. All it does is let a student
put three answers to one question next to each other, which is the one thing three sheets of
paper on a screen make genuinely hard.

**Accessibility path.** The rail is a real table: th scope="col" per way-to-buy, th scope="row" per question. Cells are
buttons; an empty one announces "empty — hook a line here". Keyboard: from a document, arrow to
a line and press Enter for a four-item menu of the questions; or from a cell, press Enter for a
listbox of the open document's lines, each announced whole ("Ferro's Plan, line 4: a late
payment adds fifteen dollars and moves the rest to twenty-two percent"). Every row header
carries a READ ACROSS control that speaks the three cells together with their sources — the
comparison is the row, so the row is what reads.
At 640px and 200% zoom the layout switches columns, never rows: one way-to-buy on screen with
all four questions, and READ ACROSS still renders the three answers to one question as a stacked
group. Rows are never collapsed into per-offer cards, because per-offer cards destroy the
construct — you would be comparing offers instead of comparing answers, which is the whole thing
being taught.
The trace strip is an ordered list of postings, each a button with aria-pressed; marking a
clause uses the same hook mechanism as the rail. The payoff strip is a list of month cells with
aria-current on the flag plus a "set payoff month" listbox — no slider, no dragging — and its
length is stated in words ("at $30 a month, cleared in month 6"). Stubs are labelled number
inputs inside a fieldset whose legend is the statement's amount due; the spike is a reviewable
list at every point, so a screen-reader user has the same audit trail the trace beat needs.
Reduced motion: chips appear in place, no tear animation, identical text confirmations. Muted
audio: nothing carries information in sound. Colour: the student's red marks announce as "your
mark" and use a distinct stroke texture, so red never carries the only signal.

**Where it could teach something false.** 1. Deferred interest. "Same as cash" back-interest is charged on the ORIGINAL purchase amount
from the purchase date, not on the remaining balance — get it wrong and the world teaches a
comfortable falsehood. Guard: the tab agreement states it in one sentence and the closing
statement prints the arithmetic line, so a teacher can check it.
2. Simple against compound. NYSED 2.1 and 2.3 say simple interest; real revolving cards
compound. Guard: this is a shop tab, not a credit card, and the agreement says "we charge
interest on what you borrowed, not on interest we already added." The world must never call the
tab a credit card, and the teacher note in the evidence trail says so out loud.
3. Opportunity cost is not cash. The WAIT column's $72 is route money not earned, not money paid
out. Merging them into one dollar figure teaches a category error. Guard: row 4 is labelled WHAT
IT COSTS YOU IN THE END, the wait cell takes two parts — cash paid and money not earned — and
the closing screen keeps those on separate lines.
4. Minors cannot sign. Line 1 of every form reads "signer must be 18 or older", Ray signs, the
student earns and pays. Noticing who is legally on the hook is part of the inspection, and the
consequence line reads "a late payment goes on Ray's record, not yours" — 2.1's legal-
responsibility clause made concrete without shame.
5. APR against monthly dollars. Both are printed once, side by side ("26% a year — on $120 that
is $2.60 a month"), so the conversion is taught rather than hidden and the student's remaining
work is deciding what to multiply.
6. No credit score. The world models a credit agreement, a fee and a rate change. It never
models a score or a report, and no screen implies one.

**Kill condition.** If opening the June statement does not shift the distribution of Act-1 stamps or of C1 er2
correctness, the premise is false: the students who read got nothing the students who skipped
did not, and the desk is decoration around a three-way choice. Abandon it rather than patch it
with a prompt telling students to read the statement — a world that has to point at its own
evidence is not an inspection world, and the prompt would turn every requirement downstream of
it into direct_scaffold anyway, capping them at 3.

**What it does not prove.** It does not prove a student can handle compound interest, revolving credit cards, amortised
loans, or a market of competing lenders — it models one shop, one tab and simple interest. It
says nothing about credit reports or credit scores, which the world does not contain. It does
not prove they can read real consumer credit documents, which are an order of magnitude denser
than a six-line form and are what 2.1 points at in adult life. It does not prove they would
behave this way with their own money over a real six months rather than twenty-four minutes. And
the recommendation stamp itself proves nothing at all — no decision requirement reads it, by
design.

**Visual identity.** Take the title away and a reviewer knows it in one frame: kraft-paper ground under a single work
lamp, a steel parts rail bolted across the bottom of the screen with hooks on it, and a receipt
spike with paper stacked on it. Object language is entirely shop paperwork — carbon-copy forms
with perforated tear-off stubs, a punch-card payment strip with a brass flag, rubber stamps
reading PAID, PAST DUE and RECOMMEND. Typography: monospace for every document body, a heavy
condensed sans for Ferro's own headings, a soft grease-pencil face for anything the student
writes. Colour: oxidised steel and shop blue against warm paper, with one reserved signal red
used for nothing the product writes — every red mark on screen is the student's own hand, which
is an identity rule and an assessment-integrity rule at the same time. Motion is paper: chips
tear along a dotted line and settle onto a hook, stubs slide onto the spike. Nothing on screen
is a chart, a gauge or a progress bar. Against a basketball court and a night market, it reads
as a counter you stand at.

## Module 3 · Earning Income

### Every Other Friday

**Carries** `gross-to-net`, `what-taxes-fund`  
**Verbs** SET (write your own stub for a payday that hasn't happened) · PLACE (put a dated claim on the strip) · MOVE (re-date a claim) · TRADE (take a Saturday shift, displacing whatever was on that Saturday) · ROUTE (send a deduction to the dated public payment it actually funds) · NAME (mark a day you will be short, and by how much)  
**Budget** 1650 words / 24 minutes

*The student fills in their own pay stub before payroll does, builds a summer of dated promises on the take-home number they wrote, and finds out on a specific Monday whether they were right.*

You got the summer job at the Riverbend Pool snack window: sixteen hours a week at $15.50, paid
every other Friday. In May you told the Two Rivers Summer Studio you'd have their $560 balance
by June 30 — $70 in your jar plus your first check of $496 is $566, so that worked. Camp starts
July 7 and the seat goes if the money doesn't. Your first Friday is June 27. Fill in your own
pay stub before payroll does.

**Signature mechanic.** THE PAY STRIP. One horizontal ruled strip owns the screen: 68 days, June 16 to August 22,
Fridays notched. Above the rail, money in, drawn upward as four solid bars — one per payday.
Below the rail, money out, drawn downward as pinned date tags. A single balance line threads
between them, day by day.
The bars start at zero height and the student sets them. Grabbing a bar opens a blank stub card
pinned to that Friday, in payroll mono: GROSS $496.00 (given), SOCIAL SECURITY ____, MEDICARE
____, FEDERAL INCOME TAX $0.00, NY STATE $0.00, TAKE-HOME ____. Nothing auto-fills; no field
validates against another. The hiring packet is one tap away and always on screen ("Social
Security 6.2% and Medicare 1.45% come out of every paycheck"), as is a plain calculator. When
the student types a take-home, the bar rises to that height and the balance line redraws across
all 68 days behind it. You cannot place a claim after a payday whose bar you have not set — you
may not spend money you have not named.
Now the student builds the summer: drag or arrow the claim chips onto dates. The $560 studio
balance is already pinned to Monday June 30 and cannot be un-promised. Drop it while your bar
says $496 and June 30 prints "end of day: $6.00" and holds.
Friday June 27, payroll's stub arrives and overlays the student's, differences boxed. The bar
corrects its own height in place — dropping 37.94 pixels-worth of dollars — and two slivers peel
off its top: $30.75 and $7.19. They travel to the right-hand column, RIVERBEND COUNTY — THIS
MONTH, a small civic-serif list of dated public money events (Sept 3 · Social Security deposit ·
Devin Hale, retired highway dept · $1,412 / Riverbend Clinic · Medicare pays hospital care for
patients 65+ / Aug 6 · county repaves Route 9 · county sales tax / Sept 2 · Riverbend Middle and
Bell Ave Library open · property tax). The slivers do not vanish; they hover unplaced in a tray
marked "these left your check." The student drops each on a row. A wrong drop is accepted and
the row prints its own funding line back — "funded by: county sales tax. $0.00 of this came from
a paycheck deduction" — which is information, not a buzzer, and no elimination.
Meanwhile June 30 has gone from $6.00 to −$31.94 and the strip has grown a notch there. The
student repairs on the strip with three real verbs: MOVE a claim to a later date (bounded by
third-party deadlines), DROP it, or TRADE — take one of Devin's 4-hour Saturday shifts, which
raises that pay period's gross to $558 and raises both slivers with it, and which lands on the
same Saturday slot as whatever chip is already there. Money-in and money-out compete for the
same days. That is the whole world.
July 7 the schedule board posts: hours cut to 12 from July 14. Bars 3 and 4 are wiped and must
be re-set from a new gross of $372 — the same operation on a different number, which is where a
remembered $458.06 stops working.
August 22 the strip is finished: a balance line that either never crossed zero or is marked with
the student's own NAME markers, four stub cards showing what they wrote against what payroll
wrote, and two routed slivers. One writing prompt, once: "$132.78 left your checks this summer
before you ever saw it. Devin's deposit lands September 3. Write what that money bought, and who
it bought it for."

**The tradeoff.** Cash now against the day you actually need it, and hours against the things hours are for. Three
dominant-strategy risks, each priced out:
(1) "Just work every Saturday." A shift is worth $57.26 net and would cover the June 30 gap
twice over — so every earning Saturday is also occupied. July 19 is Marisol's quinceañera.
August 2 is the bike sidewalk sale. August 16 is the studio's final showcase. A Saturday shift
always costs a placed chip, and the strip shows the collision before you commit.
(2) "Buy nothing and coast." The $560 is required, dated, and priced off the job posting, so the
take-home question decides something for every student regardless of appetite. The student also
names their own summer goal in one tap at the start, and unspent money sits against it at turn-
in.
(3) "Always move it later." Later is often right — waiting to August 15 makes the sneakers $62
instead of $85, waiting to July 21 makes the bike $270 instead of $310. Later is sometimes fatal
— the studio seat is released July 1, the bike is gone after August 2. Which is which is dated
and published from the start, so the trade is a judgement, not a gamble.

**Good thinking, bad outcome.** A student reads the packet on day one, writes $458.06 before June 27, sees immediately that June
30 will be $31.94 short, and repairs the right way — takes Devin's June 21 Saturday shift for
$57.26 net rather than dropping a claim. That Saturday is the studio's orientation. Missing it
drops them to the waitlist and the seat goes anyway, and the $180 deposit with it. Best
reasoning in the room, worst summer.
The evidence is untouched, because not one of the seven rows reads an outcome. ER1 reads the
fields on a stub they wrote. ER2 reads whether their placements fit the real balance at their
dates. ER3 reads whether they freed at least the shortfall from money that could still move,
before the date, which they did. ER4 reads the final series and their markers. The two tax rows
read a stub and two routings. Nothing anywhere asks "did you keep the seat," and the teacher's
card shows a student who converted correctly, anticipated a dated shortfall a week out, and
covered it — beside a summer that went badly. Those are two different sentences and the product
prints both.

**Bad thinking, good outcome.** A student never opens the packet, sets all four bars to $496 because that is the number on the
poster, drops only the cheapest chips, ignores June 30 entirely, and finishes August 22 with
$900 unspent and a balance line that mostly looks fine. The strip reads like success.
It reports no mastery, and three separate guards do it. ER1 is scored from the first real
opportunity: a take-home value first entered after payroll printed it is recorded as
answer_supplied and scores 0, and here it was never entered at all. ER3 is not dodgeable by
underspending, because the $560 is required, dated, and priced off the offer letter, so the
shortfall arrives for everyone; a student who lets June 30 fail without a single MOVE, DROP or
TRADE scores 0 there no matter what the ending balance is. And ER1 for `what-taxes-fund` demands
the rate applied a second time to $372, so a student who got July 25's numbers by reading
payroll's own stub cannot produce them for a period whose bar had to be set before that stub
existed.
The two competencies then return `not-yet-demonstrated`, not a low score and not a zero on a
scale — and the unspent $900 sits on the teacher's card next to the goal the student themselves
named in June, as a fact and not as a mark.

**Why not a button.** For the signature action — producing take-home — a button reading CALCULATE TAKE-HOME is the
hard fail written down: the system performs the target reasoning. Worse, it teaches that take-
home is something a machine hands you, which is the exact belief this module exists to break. A
blank stub is the truthful object; payroll fills one in on a Friday, and the entire assessment
is the student filling it in first and finding out on a date whether they agreed.
The bar is not decoration on top of that. The financial construct here is "your promises cannot
be taller than your take-home," and the bar makes taller-than literal: the balance line is drawn
resting on the bar's height, so when payroll disagrees on June 27 the disagreement arrives as a
distance and a dip in a specific place, not as a recomputed figure in a box. The number and the
magnitude are the same value with two renderings, and the typed one is primary.
The other place representation genuinely beats a control is the sliver. A dropdown reading "this
funds: [Social Security ▾]" asserts where the money went. A piece of your own bar peeling off,
travelling, and landing on a dated payment to a named person demonstrates it — and it is aimed
squarely at "taxes are money that disappears," which is a claim about motion, not about
vocabulary.

**Would clicking be better.** Argue the other side honestly: for most of this world, clicking is better, and the design should
stop pretending otherwise. Re-dating a claim is a date picker. On a Chromebook trackpad at 200%
zoom, a 68-day drag is worse than a select. Taking a Saturday shift is a confirm dialog with the
collision named in words. So drag is offered and never required anywhere, and the keyboard/typed
path is the primary implementation with the pointer path built on top of it — not the other way
around, which is how these mechanics usually rot.
Where the button would genuinely be better and I am declining anyway, I have to name the cost.
The sliver routing could be two dropdowns and would take eleven seconds instead of forty. I keep
the travel because the destination question is the only place in this module where the
misconception is about where money goes, and a dropdown answers that question in the same breath
as asking it. If playtest shows students routing correctly and still writing "the money is gone"
in the August 22 answer, the travel bought nothing and it becomes two dropdowns.
And the one thing that must never become a button is the take-home field. Everything else in
this design is negotiable interaction; that field is the assessment.

**Accessibility path.** The strip is authored as a date table, not as a canvas with a table bolted beside it. Rows are
the 68 days; a roving tabindex is the playhead; arrow keys move it and Home/End jump to the next
money event. Each row announces one sentence: "Friday June 27. In: your paycheck, take-home not
set. Out: none. End of day: $70.00."
Placing a claim: focus a chip in the tray, Enter to pick up, arrow to a date, Enter to drop. The
announcement is the consequence, at the instant of placement: "Studio balance, $560, placed
Monday June 30. End of that day: minus $31.94." That is the same information the line-crossing
gives a sighted student, delivered at the same moment — the construct, not a summary of it.
The stub is four labelled numeric inputs and a given gross. The bar is its visualisation; the
value is the shared object, and the typed path is the one the pointer path calls. Nothing
requires a drag to be assessed.
Zero-crossings live in a persistent, focusable "Short days" list — "Jun 30, minus $31.94" —
updated live, so the dip is a fact you can navigate to rather than a shape you must see. NAME
markers are added from that list.
The slivers are two focusable items in a "these left your check" list; the county column is a
listbox of five dated rows; Enter routes; the row's funding line is read back through the same
live region.
At 200% zoom and at 640px the strip reflows to a vertical date list — which IS the table the
screen reader path already uses. One model, two skins, so the accessible path cannot drift from
the visual one without breaking the visual one too.
Reduced motion: the June 27 bar correction becomes a was/now pair printed on the stub and the
sliver flight becomes an instant reposition plus the announcement; both motions are
informational and both have exact static equivalents by construction. Muted audio: no sound
carries information anywhere. Colour: a zero-crossing is a notch plus a minus sign plus text,
never red alone, and the wrong-route feedback is a printed funding line, not a colour.

**Where it could teach something false.** 1. Overstating a teenager's tax burden. Guard: federal and NY State withholding print as $0.00
with one true sentence — "you'll earn about $1,700 this summer, which is under the amount you
can earn before federal income tax starts; Social Security and Medicare come out no matter how
little you earn." That single contrast is better content than any labelling exercise, and a
content test asserts both lines are $0.00 for this earner and this gross.
2. Claiming FICA funds schools, libraries or roads. This is the trap that kills this module and
it is killed here in the data model: every county row carries `fundedBy`, and a test asserts no
deduction can be scored correct against a row it does not fund. Social Security routes only to a
benefit payment; Medicare only to hospital care for patients 65+. The roads row is county sales
tax and the schools-and-library row is property tax, and both print, when a sliver is dropped on
them, that $0.00 of them came from a paycheck deduction — which is where a student learns that
the things they use most are paid for by taxes that are not on their stub. That is more accurate
and more interesting than the false version.
3. Implying FICA sits in an account with your name on it. Guarded on purpose: the September 3
row is another person's deposit, and the explanation prompt asks who the money bought it for.
The world never says the student will or will not receive a benefit later; it says where this
money went this month, which is the part that is true.
4. Rates and rounding. All money in integer cents; 6.2% and 1.45% applied per line and rounded
half-up per line, as payroll does, so the printed lines sum exactly to the printed deduction
total. Added Saturday hours recompute the whole period's gross rather than being taxed as a
separate lump.
5. Hours. Sixteen hours a week rising to twenty on a Saturday, no shift past 7pm, sits inside
New York's summer rules for a 15-year-old with working papers.
6. Sales tax appears only as a printed register line on the bike (8%, stated as Riverbend
County's rate, a fact of this world) and no required evidence hangs on it.

**Kill condition.** Two, and the first is the real one. (1) If the balance-line preview makes the bar solvable by
trial and error for a meaningful share of students, I first remove the live preview from unset
bars — and if the world stops being legible without it, the design dies, because a stub the
student can brute-force is a pay-stub labelling exercise with extra steps and it deserves the
same 47/70. (2) If the content sweep cannot find a claim set where every plan a student can
build against $496 fails on a dated day against $458.06 while still leaving three genuinely
different repair routes open, the trap is not structural and ER3 is being produced by luck
rather than by design — at which point `gross-to-net` cannot be claimed in full and the world
should not ship claiming it.

**What it does not prove.** It does not touch `compare-earning-paths` and therefore leaves objective 3.1 exactly where it
is. That is deliberate and it is the design's main concession: `read-and-judge` needs a separate
information set — several kinds of work, their training time, their training cost, their likely
pay — plus a second written explanation, and folding that in would have turned one strip into a
strip plus a comparison table, which is the family this brief calls multiple choice with
decoration. Two competencies fully produced, 3.2 and 3.3 both demonstrable, module 3 from 0 of 3
to 2 of 3.
It also does not prove: that the student can compute a percentage without a calculator (the
calculator is standard access and the arithmetic is not the skill); that they understand tax
brackets, filing, refunds or withholding choices (federal is $0.00 here on purpose and the world
says why); that they can save toward a goal — the laptop goal is real pressure in the design and
`save-toward-a-goal` is not claimed, because its five shipped requirements are not all produced
here; that they can build a budget from scratch, which `plan-within-income` already covers in
two worlds and which this world produces evidence toward and does not claim; and that any of
this predicts behaviour with real money.

**Visual identity.** Take the title away and you still know it in one glance, because no other world in BOW is a
single horizontal line. One ruled strip owns the screen, dates left to right, money in drawn
upward as solid bars and money out drawn downward as pinned tags, with one continuous balance
line threading between them — a shape nobody mistakes for Basketball's grid of week envelopes or
the market's stall and stock.
Object language: everything is either a bar (an amount that has a height), a tag (a promise that
has a date), or a card (a stub, in payroll mono, on paper white). Three object types, no fourth.
Typography does the argument. Dates in a condensed sans on the rail. Every dollar figure in
tabular-figure mono so columns align down the strip — the payroll aesthetic, borrowed because it
is honest here. The county column in a small civic serif, so public record reads visibly
different from the student's own money and nobody confuses the two.
Colour: chlorine blue and sun-bleached concrete, with the balance line in graphite and the two
deduction slivers in a single warm ochre that appears nowhere else in the world, so the eye
tracks them from the bar to the county column without a label.
Motion: exactly two moves in twenty-four minutes. The bar correcting its own height on June 27,
and the ochre slivers peeling off its top and travelling right. Nothing else in this world moves
at all — which is what makes those two land, and which is why both have exact static equivalents
for reduced motion without losing anything.

### Two Checks, One Summer

**Carries** `gross-to-net`, `what-taxes-fund`  
**Verbs** HANG · CLOSE · CARRY · TRIM · TRACE  
**Budget** 1350 words / 25 minutes

*The student hangs a summer's worth of promises on two pay rails drawn to the $600 the offer letter named, then drives each payroll deduction into the rail themselves and watches it retract under commitments that do not move.*

You got the job at Riverside Pool. Four weeks, twenty hours a week, fifteen dollars an hour.
They pay you every two weeks, and the letter says six hundred dollars a check.
Build Camp costs $840. Seventy-five holds your spot this Friday. The other seven hundred and
sixty-five is due the last Friday of the summer, in one payment, and they will not take it in
pieces.
So: hang what you owe across the two checks, decide what to carry forward, and find out what a
check is actually worth.

**Signature mechanic.** THE RAIL AND THE CHIP DRIVE.
On screen: two horizontal aluminium beams stacked in the left two-thirds — CHECK 1 and CHECK 2 —
each drawn to scale at a computed pixels-per-dollar, each with a milled end cap carrying its
length in dollars. On day one both caps read $600.00, because that is what the offer letter
said. Under them sits the SPOOL, a coiled tape measure: unspent rail wound forward.
The student grabs TAGS from a tray. A tag is a key-fob rectangle whose WIDTH IS ITS PRICE: Camp
deposit $75, Camp balance $765, Phone $45 or $28, Summer bus pass $52, Bike brakes $65 or $30,
Lunch at the snack bar $6 a shift. Tags snap flush left-to-right against each other — no parking
one in the middle. Variant tags have a notched handle on the right edge; dragging or arrowing it
steps the tag through its allowed prices and everything to its right slides. When the student
signs the sheet, every tag they hung gets a brass rivet driven through it. Riveted tags do not
move again.
Then payday. The stub arrives as eight stamped chips docked at the right. Each chip carries its
name, its rule and who pays it: "Social Security · 6.2% of $600.00 · ____ · withheld from your
pay" (the two percentage chips are blank and the student types the amount); "Uniform + whistle ·
one time · $18.00 · withheld from your pay"; and, mixed in, "Social Security · employer's share
· $37.20 · paid by Riverside Parks."
The student drives chips into the rail one at a time. Each drive walks the end cap LEFT by that
chip's amount. $600.00 → $562.80 → $554.10 → $550.10 → $538.10 → $520.10. The tags do not move.
They stay exactly where they were hung, and one by one the rightmost ones end up past the cap —
drawn beyond the beam in hatched amber with the exact overhang stamped on the hatch: "$46.90
past the end."
That is the world. The money moved. The promises didn't.
After closing, the student says the check is finished and only then does the bank line reveal
what actually landed. Rail says $520.10 and the bank says $520.10: closed. Rail says $557.30
because two employer chips got driven: the two numbers sit side by side and the student goes
back in.
The driven chips then fly right onto THE WALL: six vertical fill-tubes, each labelled by what it
pays for and none by the name of a tax. The student docks their five chips into the tubes. When
the fifth lands, the tubes flood with thousands of small grey chips from the rest of Riverside,
and the payout shelf above resolves — including one card on the town's own tube that the student
can lift: a paycheck, exactly as wide as their gross rail. Theirs.
At payday 2 the check-2 rail retracts to $538.10 while the $765 balance tag is sitting on it. It
overhangs by at least $226.90 for every student alive. The only thing that extends a rail is the
spool: splice on what you wound forward in week one, and the beam gets physically longer. What
you did not wind forward, you now have to trim out of tags that can still change — and the
riveted ones refuse.

**The tradeoff.** Commit early and pay less, or stay liquid and pay more — against a rail whose true length you
have not seen.
Committing on sign-up day saves real money: the $75 deposit locks the camp at $840 instead of
$900, and the summer bus pass is $52 against $64 of weekly fares. But every early tag gets
riveted, and it is riveted to a beam labelled $600 that is going to become $520.10. Staying
liquid immunises you against the retraction and costs $76 for the privilege. Neither dominates.
Committing to everything early busts; committing to nothing is the most expensive way to be
safe.
There IS a best line for a student who reasons well — read the card, estimate ~11%, commit early
but sized to ~$520 — and that is the design working, not a defect: the competency is precisely
"plan against net," so good reasoning should have a best answer. The defect would be a non-
reasoning strategy that dominates, and there isn't one, because the two cheap-and-safe
heuristics ("commit early", "wait and see") each cost you something the other doesn't.
The sharpest edge: the week-3 offer at $130 is reachable ONLY on the line basic phone + patched
brakes instead of a bus pass + lunch packed every single day, which leaves $2.20. It has to have
been set up on sign-up day, before any net was visible. It is never graded. So it supplies the
whole world's motivational tension without touching a single evidence row — which is the only
honest place to put something a student cannot see coming.

**Good thinking, bad outcome.** A student reads WHAT COMES OUT on sign-up day, works out that roughly a tenth will not arrive,
and chooses the $65 permanent brake fix over the $30 patch on the correct reasoning that the
patch expires in October and will have to be paid again. That is the better long-view call and
it costs $35 now. It puts the week-3 offer $32.80 out of reach, and they decline it while three
classmates go.
A second, sharper case: a student declines the week-3 offer BECAUSE they correctly computed they
would land $57.80 short, and ends the summer with $72.20 unspent. A teacher glancing at the
board sees a kid who left money on the table.
The evidence survives both because not one of the seven rows reads the outcome. ER1 reads the
closing, ER2 reads whether each commitment fit the money that had actually arrived at that
moment, ER3 reads where the repair money came from, ER4 reads whether the end state is covered
or the gap is named, and the three what-taxes-fund rows read arithmetic, provenance and writing.
The brake choice and the offer are never scored. And the evidence trail stores the rail state at
the moment of the decline, so an observer reading "declined at week 3" can see the student
declined against a computed shortfall rather than out of timidity. There is no score to lose,
because this world does not have one.

**Bad thinking, good outcome.** A student reads nothing. At payday 1 they drive all eight chips including both employer shares,
land at $474.20, un-drive chips more or less at random, and hit $520.10 on the fourth attempt.
Because the confusion made them hang almost nothing on check 1, their spool ends up at $445.10,
the camp balance splices in with room, and they finish the summer with everything paid and money
spare. Best-looking board in the class.
Nothing reports mastery, for three reasons that are all structural.
First, ER1 reads the FIRST closing attempt and the shape of the attempts after it. Single-chip
toggling toward a revealed target is visible in the log as search, not revision, and it scores 2
at best; if the world had to name the "paid by Riverside Parks" line for them it is capped at 3
by `direct_scaffold`; if the world had to set the rail to let them continue, `answer_supplied`
returns null and the row reads "not demonstrated," not zero.
Second, what-taxes-fund ER1 reads the two typed percentages, which cannot be brute-forced
against a target the student cannot see, and ER2 reads the five docks with no word to match on.
Third and decisively: BOW has no composite anywhere. The teacher's screen shows seven separate
rows, and this student's reads "Builds take-home out of gross: 2. Ends inside take-home: 5."
That is exactly the truthful picture of a lucky accident, and there is no arithmetic anywhere in
the product that can average it into "proficient."

**Why not a button.** The signature action is driving a deduction chip into the rail. The button version is one
control marked APPLY DEDUCTIONS that prints $520.10.
Three things the button cannot do. (1) The removals are five different sizes with five different
causes, and the rail shows without a sentence that the Social Security piece is more than four
times the Medicare piece and that the biggest single bite on the first check is a one-off for a
swimsuit and a whistle. A button collapses all of that into one number and makes the arithmetic
the system's, which is the "calculator that does the thinking" hard fail. (2) The tags are
ALREADY on the beam when it retracts. There is no button that can make a student's own prior
promises become unaffordable in front of them without a single one of those promises changing —
that event, *the money moved and my commitments didn't*, is the only thing this world has that a
worksheet does not, and it requires the money to be a length and the commitments to be objects
sitting on it. (3) A button cannot be wrong in a way that means anything. A chip can be driven
wrongly, and the specific wrong drive — taking the employer's half of FICA out of your own pay —
is a nameable misconception a teacher can act on, not an arithmetic slip.
The overhang is the same argument in one image: $46.90 past the end of the beam is a shortfall
you can point at. A red $46.90 in a box is a number you can ignore.

**Would clicking be better.** The honest case against me: real workers never do this. A stub arrives with net already
computed, and the adult skill is READING one, not performing one. By making a kid assemble take-
home I risk teaching that net is something you build rather than something you are handed. And
five drags is five drags — on a Chromebook trackpad at 640px, dragging is strictly worse than
tapping, and a student who understands the idea completely is still doing motor work to prove
it.
I would concede the whole mechanic if the only thing at stake were the number. It isn't: it is
the retraction under standing commitments, and that has no button form. So the chips stay, and I
concede three things instead.
One: I cut the "just me / everyone" lever I originally wanted on the wall. It was a two-state
comparison pretending to be a physical object, which is theatre. The pooled fill now happens
once, as a consequence of the student docking their fifth chip, plus an ungraded "show only
mine" checkbox for anyone who wants to look again.
Two: the student types two amounts, not five. The percentage chips are blank because deriving
6.2% of your own gross is the arithmetic the competency actually names; the flat federal, state
and uniform amounts are printed, because real stubs print them and making a child recompute a
withholding table is busywork wearing a rubric.
Three — the real tell — every drag has a keyboard equivalent that is FASTER than dragging. Focus
a chip, press Enter, it drives. If the pointer path were the point, that would be cheating. It
isn't the point, so it isn't.
What I refuse to concede: making the tags a list of checkboxes with a running total. That is the
dressed-up-worksheet failure this family is warned about, and it is the version where the rail
length stops being a length and the overhang stops existing.

**Accessibility path.** The construct is: a length of money, claims consuming it in order, and the fact that the length
shrinks to less than was announced. Keyboard and screen reader must preserve LENGTH, MEMBERSHIP,
ORDER and OVERHANG. They do, on the same board, driving the same event log. There is no
worksheet variant.
Each rail is `role="list"`, named "Check 1 rail", with a `aria-live="polite"` status region
beside it that always reads the three numbers that ARE the construct: "Rail $520.10. Hung
$163.00. Free $357.10." Every tag is a `listitem` containing a button whose accessible name is
name, amount, position and state: "Summer bus pass, $52.00, 2 of 5, paid Friday, cannot move."
Moving a tag is grab/move/drop, not drag: Space picks up ("picked up Camp balance, $765.00"),
Up/Down moves between tray, Check 1, Check 2 and the spool, Left/Right changes position in the
order, Space drops ("dropped on Check 2, position 1. Check 2 free: minus $226.90"). Resizing a
variant tag is Left/Right on the focused tag, announcing the new price and the new free space
each step. Riveted tags announce "paid — will not move" and fire the same `pin.refused` event a
mouse grab does, so ER3 reads identically.
Driving a chip is Enter on the focused chip and announces the retraction as the number it is:
"Driven. Social Security, $37.20. Check 1 rail is now $562.80." Five presses walk the same beam
the same way. The spool is a spinbutton: Left/Right by $5, Home/End for none or all, then
"splice onto Check 2." The wall's six tubes are labelled regions and the same grab/move/drop
docks a chip into one.
Overhang is never colour or shape alone. The overhanging tag's accessible name gains "past the
end of the rail by $46.90" and the rail's status line reads "Check 2 is over by $46.90." A
screen-reader user knows which tag is past the end and by exactly how much — the identical two
facts a sighted user has.
200% zoom and 640px: pixels-per-dollar is recomputed from the container, so the beam is always
to scale; a tag narrower than 44px renders as a fixed 44px chip with its amount printed, never
smaller; a rail longer than one line wraps with a "continues" marker and the status line carries
the totals, so wrapping can never hide the constraint. Every length on screen is also printed as
a number. Reduced motion: the retraction becomes a single cut with the before and after cap
values both held on screen for two seconds — the same information, no slide. There is no audio
anywhere on the critical path, so muting removes nothing.

**Where it could teach something false.** 1. FICA funding schools, libraries and roads. This is the trap NYSED 3.3's own sentence sets,
and it is false. Guard: the Social Security and Medicare tubes describe benefits paid to people;
schools and roads live on the state tube and the town's property-and-sales-tax tube; the
student's payroll chips never dock on the town tube. The dock answer key and every tube's card
text live in one reviewed file with a source column, asserted by a facts test, and signed off by
a curriculum lead — not by an implementer.
2. A teenager losing 30% to federal income tax. Guard: federal withholding is $4.00 on a $600
biweekly check, derived by the percentage method (annualised $15,600, less the $8,600 adjustment
for a post-2020 W-4 with Step 2 unchecked, into the 10% band, rounded to whole dollars as Pub
15-T permits). Total deductions are 11.8%, and the packet says in one line why the federal piece
is so small at this pay.
3. Confusing withholding with tax owed. Guard: one sentence in the packet — "what comes out now
is an estimate; in April you settle up, and if too much came out you get it back." Said once,
never tested, because refunds are not in this module.
4. Implying Sam's $37.20 pays one named person's benefit. Guard: the payout shelf reads "paid
out of this tube this month" with a count — 1,940 checks in Riverside — and there is never a
one-to-one arrow from a student's chip to a person.
5. Implying the town chose to fund Sam's wage as a kindness. Guard: the card states the parks
budget line as a line, with no adjective.
6. Partisanship. Guard: there is no rate-setting control anywhere in the world; no character
argues for or against taxes; the federal tube's card lists its largest categories neutrally and
alphabetically; the explanation prompt asks what the money does and never whether it is fair.
Review gate: any evaluative adjective on a destination card is a defect, not a copy preference.
7. Implying deductions are the same everywhere. Guard: the state chip and its tube both say New
York by name.
8. Implying an employer paying half of FICA is a favour or that it is the worker's money. Guard:
the chip says "paid by Riverside Parks" and the tube's card says the total going in is 12.4%,
half from each side. Stated once, as a fact.

**Kill condition.** If fewer than a quarter of students open WHAT COMES OUT before signing the sheet AND the
students who did open it score no better on ER2 than those who didn't, then the level-5 path is
not reachable cold, the retraction is a gotcha rather than a consequence, and the world is
punishing children for not reading a page. Redesign the anticipation route — most likely a
first, tiny practice check in week zero that costs no commitment — or abandon the design. I
would not ship it by making the card louder; a card that has to shout is a hint, and a hint caps
the whole competency at 3.

**What it does not prove.** It does not touch `compare-earning-paths`, so NYSED 3.1 stays not assessable and the module goes
to 2 of 3, not 3 of 3. That is deliberate: 3.1's shape is `read-and-judge` and needs several
genuinely different careers compared on training time, training cost and likely pay. A summer
job with three certification options is one career ladder wearing a costume, and bolting it on
would have cost roughly 500 words and produced four-fifths of a competency, which produces
nothing.
It does not prove a student can compute percentages generally — they compute two, with the
method card on screen. It does not prove they can build a budget from nothing: this world names
the claims, and `plan-within-income`'s savings-first row is never tested here. It does not prove
any understanding of tax brackets or progressivity; it shows one earner at one income and says
out loud that the federal table takes almost nothing at this level. It does not prove the
student knows what taxes ought to be, which this product has no business assessing. And it does
not prove they would do any of it with real money.

**Visual identity.** Take the title off and a reviewer still knows it in a second, because nothing else in BOW is
made of metal in daylight.
Environment: a pale chlorine-blue field with the tooth of wet pool concrete. Basketball is warm
court wood and a season grid; the pop-up is night, string lights and stacked crates. This is
11am, damp, and municipal.
Object language: anodised aluminium beams with milled end caps. Tags are rubber-coated key fobs
with a punched hole at the left edge and rounded corners. Rivets are brass. The spool is a
coiled steel measuring tape. Chips are small stamped tokens with a bevel. The wall is six
vertical fill-tubes marked exactly like the depth numbers painted on a pool wall.
Typography: a tight condensed grotesk for every amount, right-aligned, always to the cent, set
as if stamped rather than printed — the numbers are the loudest thing on screen, and they are
supposed to be. A plain humanist face for prose, at a size that survives 200%.
Colour: the rail is the only saturated element on the board — one hard cyan. Tags are neutral
slate. Overhang is hatched amber, never red: it is a fact, not an error. Riveted tags go matte
with a brass dot. Tube fill is the same cyan, rising from the bottom, and everyone else's
contribution is grey.
Motion: one verb, retraction. The cap slides left; nothing else in the world moves, bounces,
floats or eases. Reduced motion replaces the slide with a two-state cut holding both numbers.
The restraint is the identity: a screen where exactly one thing moves, and it moves toward you
taking money with it.

### First Check

**Carries** `compare-earning-paths`, `gross-to-net`, `what-taxes-fund`  
**Verbs** NORMALISE · COMMIT · VERIFY · TRACE · RE-FIT  
**Budget** 1950 words / 25 minutes

*Compare three summer job postings on one footing, commit a plan to the pay number the posting advertised, then take your own first paycheck apart line by line and re-fit the plan to what is actually left.*

Nine weeks of summer. The fall robotics season costs $700 — $240 down by the end of Week 4, the
other $460 by the end of Week 9. You told them you'd cover it yourself. You have $210 saved.
Three jobs are pinned to the board at the Marlow rec desk, and every one states its pay a
different way. One of them takes money from you before it pays you anything. Read them properly.
Pick one. Then find out what a paycheck actually hands you.

**Signature mechanic.** **The Trace Board.** Left third of screen: your first pay stub, on perforated paper — issuer,
period, HOURS 12.0, RATE $12.50, GROSS $150.00, then five deduction lines each printed with its
rate or table source (Social Security 6.2%, Medicare 1.45%, Federal income tax — table, NY State
income tax — table, Marlow Pool staff shirt $12.00 one time), then a NET line that is **blacked
out**. Beside it, an index card: your own shift record, five dates, 13.5 hours.
Step one, VERIFY. The stub says 12.0; your card says 13.5. Nothing points at this. You either
accept the stub or flag it. Flag it and a corrected stub reissues at GROSS $168.75 this period.
Accept it and $18.75 arrives as a correction on the last check of the summer — after the deposit
date it could have covered.
Step two, the split. Under the gross figure sits a single solid ink rule, its printed width
labelled with the gross you just settled. You grab its right end and pull. A segment tears off
with the deduction's name on it, and it is blank — you must put the amount in, computed from the
printed rate against *your* gross with the plain on-screen calculator. Get it wrong and the
segment is the wrong length and the rule under it is visibly the wrong remainder; nothing tells
you which. Pull five times. Federal income tax tears off at $0.00 and must be recorded at zero,
not skipped. When the last segment is off, what is left of the rule — $140.34 — slides into your
account tile, and it is the same object your plan spends. The blacked-out NET line uncovers to
match it, as confirmation, never as the source.
Step three, TRACE. The torn segments sit loose on the desk. Four fund trays open on the right,
each a real document: the Social Security fund page, the Medicare fund page, the NY State
general fund page, and Marlow's own town revenue page. Plus one employer tray, "Marlow Pool —
staff costs." You place each segment. Placing is how you open the document; the tray you drop
into is not the scored thing.
The scored thing is the two sticky notes pinned to the board. Dana, the head lifeguard, wrote
one: *"They take out for Medicare and I'm twenty-four. Where does that go?"* Her own stub is
pinned beside yours — $722.00 gross, the identical 6.2% and 1.45%, and a federal withholding
line that is not zero like yours. You answer by **highlighting one row of one document**. There
is no text box and no multiple choice: you move to a row, press the cite key or click, and a
yellow highlighter mark lands on it with your initials. Second note: *"The library got new hours
this year. Did any of my check pay for that?"* The route is real and it has a hop — your state
withholding, into the state general fund, out as the state-aid line on Marlow's revenue page
beside property tax and fees. A student who highlights their Social Security segment's fund page
is wrong, and that is the whole discrimination.
After: the desk is covered in other people's paper and the only colour on it is the two marks
you made. Your plan rail, still pinned at the bottom, has quietly gone red on whichever line no
longer fits $140.34.

**The tradeoff.** Money soon against money total against money next summer, crossed against a dated payment.
Snack bar: $12.50/hr, 12 hr/wk Weeks 1–6 then 8 hr/wk Weeks 7–9 → $1,200 gross, nothing to pay
up front, cash from Week 1. Junior lifeguard: $185 and three unpaid weeks first, then $17.00/hr
× 16 hr for six weeks → $1,632 gross, $1,447 after training, but the $185 comes out of $210 and
no pay exists before the Week 4 deposit, so the deposit goes late at $25. Camp helper: $40
first-aid card, $88 a session, two sessions a week for eight weeks → $1,408, $1,368 after
training, cash from Week 2.
Spread after costs: $1,422 / $1,368 / $1,200 — and the lifeguard cert is good next summer while
the other two are not. **The lifeguard path does earn the most, and that is not a defect.**
"More training sometimes pays more" is true; the misconception BOW is hunting is *always*, and
*the cost of getting there doesn't matter*. A student who ignores the $185, the three unpaid
weeks and the late fee scores that path at $1,632 — wrong by $210 — and picks it for a reason
that is not true.
No dominant strategy, and the fix is enforced rather than asserted: a balance sweep in the
world's own `balance.ts`, matching the pattern both shipped worlds use, must show each of the
three paths winning under at least one of four authored priority profiles — `cash soonest`,
`most by Week 9`, `useful next summer`, `nothing can go wrong` — and none winning under all
four. If a variant fails the sweep it does not ship.
The second tension is smaller and sharper: the plan's slack. Commit high and the first check
breaks you; commit low and the Week 4 deposit is out of reach. There is no safe floor.

**Good thinking, bad outcome.** A student reads the small print, normalises all three paths, subtracts the $185 and the $40,
works out that the lifeguard path pays nothing before Week 4 and deliberately takes the $25 late
fee with the fee in their own figures, and — because the posting said Social Security and
Medicare come out of every check — commits a first-month plan that leaves room for withholding.
Then the fixed Week 7–9 hours drop and the $460 balance land, and they finish $34 short of $700.
The evidence survives intact because not one requirement reads the outcome. compare-earning-
paths ER1–ER3 are read off rail figures and the deposit route, ER4 off writing. gross-to-net ER2
is a 5 — their first committed plan fitted net before a stub existed, which is precisely "right
at the first real opportunity." ER4 explicitly scores 5 for *naming* a $34 gap that matches the
ledger. The settle-up screen reports "came up $34 short" as a fact beside the evidence rows,
never as a result, and there is no composite anywhere for it to drag down.

**Bad thinking, good outcome.** A student skims, sees $17.00 is the biggest number, drags nothing onto the rail beyond one
headline rate, never enters a training cost, picks the lifeguard path, and commits the whole
advertised $272/week into an auto-save. The lifeguard path really does pay the most in total, so
they clear $700 anyway.
Nothing reports mastery. ER1 fails — one path normalised, two on headline numbers. ER2 fails —
no training figure anywhere, and the $185 leaving the cash tile is the only trace of it. ER3
fails — no Week-4 figure and no late fee in their own numbers. gross-to-net ER2 fails — the
first commitment is sized to the posting. ER3 reads the repair itself: if they recovered by
hammering the spent $185 and then deleting the goal line, that is a 2, not a 4, however the
summer ended. ER1 catches whether the arithmetic was ever theirs. what-taxes-fund ER2 catches
whether the citation settles anything.
The teacher's surface shows a student who hit $700 with a row of 2s and 0s, and the two numbers
sit next to each other with no arithmetic connecting them, because there is none. **The rail is
the guard.** Its whole job is that a good choice and a lucky choice produce different logs even
when they produce the same pick.

**Why not a button.** The action is *producing take-home*. A "Show net pay" button, or a stub that just prints NET
like every real stub does, is `answer_supplied` — the taxonomy's own words, and it scores 0. The
product cannot both hand the student the number and assess whether they can get it. So the
representation is not decoration around a button; it is the only version of this moment in which
the student's mark is not zero by construction.
Three specific things the tearing does that a button cannot. First, the remainder is an
**object**, and the plan rail spends that object — the number the plan is measured against is
the number the student produced, not one the system asserted, so a wrong split shows up as a
wrong plan rather than as a wrong answer on a quiz. Second, the deductions cannot be
transcribed, because the gross is contestable: the shift card says 13.5 and the stub says 12.0,
so until the student settles that, no printed deduction amount is trustworthy and the rates have
to be applied by hand. A button would compute against whichever gross the system preferred and
quietly settle the discrepancy for them. Third, the $0.00 federal line has to be *recorded*, and
recording zero is a different act from being shown zero — it is where "I pay no federal tax" and
"the table takes nothing at this pay this period" separate.

**Would clicking be better.** Arguing the other side honestly, in three places.
**The drag loses.** Grabbing an ink rule and pulling is motor skill, and I would cut it tomorrow
if testing showed students fighting it. The construct does not need the gesture — it needs "take
a named amount out of what is left, and put the amount in yourself." A click on a deduction row
and a typed amount does all of that. So the pointer drag is the *skin*, the keyboard and click
path is the primary, and if a usability round says the drag is theatre it goes with nothing
lost. I am not defending the gesture. I am defending that the student, not the system, produces
the remainder.
**The tray placement loses outright and I have already conceded it.** Dropping the Social
Security segment into the Social Security tray is a matching game with one obvious answer —
which is exactly the labelling failure the mechanics lab scored 47/70. It is in the design only
as the act that opens the document, and **no evidence requirement reads it as a match.** what-
taxes-fund ER2 reads the *citation*, and a citation cannot be a button: the question is which
row of which document settles a claim, and the wrong rows are attractive ones. That is the whole
difference between "match Medicare to hospitals" and "here is the line that answers Dana."
**The verification step is where a button would genuinely be faster and I keep it anyway.**
"Flag discrepancy" is a button. It should be. It sits at the end of a comparison the product
refuses to do — two documents, one number each, and no highlight on either. The button is fine
because the reasoning happened before the click.
What is left after those concessions is one representation and one annotation. If either fails
testing, the world still stands on the plan rail and the citation, and both are boring, native
controls.

**Accessibility path.** The financial construct is **a running remainder that shrinks each time a named amount leaves
it, and each departed amount having a destination that can be pointed at.** That is natively a
list with a live total and a set of document rows — the paper is the skin, not the model.
**Keyboard and screen reader.** The gross figure is a focusable control in a labelled group.
Enter or Space on it performs "take out the next line": a row appears — deduction name, its
printed rate, an amount field, and a "left after this" figure — focus lands in the amount field,
and a polite live region announces "Social Security, 6.2 percent. Amount entered ten dollars
forty-six. Left: one hundred fifty-eight twenty-nine." Pointer and key dispatch the identical
`TAKE_OUT` action into the identical reducer; there is no second implementation and no second
scoring path. The bar is `aria-hidden` throughout and is a rendering of the list. Sorting a
segment is a listbox ("Place Social Security: Social Security fund / Medicare fund / State
general fund / Marlow town / Marlow Pool staff costs"). Citing is a native table: arrow to a
row, press C or Enter, "Cited: State aid, four hundred twelve thousand dollars."
**The documents are the hard part and they are handled structurally.** Every document is a real
`<table>` with a caption, scoped headers and one fact per row — a pay stub genuinely is a table,
so this costs nothing. Screen reader users get row/column context on every figure, which is
*better* than the sighted reading of a dense stub, not a reduced version of it. Documents are
announced as a list with a switcher rather than a pile.
**200% zoom and 640px.** Three postings reflow to a vertical stack; the same-footing rail
becomes one card per path carrying the identical four slots — the comparison survives because
**the comparison lives in the figures the student entered, in one place, not in the side-by-side
arrangement of the postings.** The trace board shows the stub plus one document at a time with
the running remainder pinned to the bottom edge; the remainder never scrolls away, because it is
the construct.
**Reduced motion.** The 180ms tear becomes an in-place appearance; the remainder updates
identically. No information is carried in motion — the segment's width is redundant with its
printed amount, and the red on a broken plan line is redundant with a text figure ("committed
$600 · take-home $561.36 · over by $38.64").
**Muted audio.** Nothing in the world is audio.
There is no worksheet fallback because there is no second artefact: the accessible tree is the
primary model, and the paper is drawn on top of it.

**Where it could teach something false.** **1. FICA.** 6.2% Social Security + 1.45% Medicare, employee side, are two constants in one file
with a source comment; a test asserts every authored stub's amounts derive from them. Dana's
stub uses the identical rates on a much larger gross — same rate, different amount — which is
where "the rate is the same for everyone" gets taught without a word of prose.
**2. Federal withholding on a teenager.** The student's federal line is $0.00, printed as *"at
this pay, the federal table takes nothing this period."* Guard: a banned-string test forbids any
copy implying the student owes no federal tax, ever, or that low earners are exempt. Dana's stub
carries a real federal line precisely so the world never says withholding is zero — it says it
depends on what you earn.
**3. The 30% teenager.** Total tax withheld here is $16.41 on $168.75 — 9.7%. Guard: an
assertion across every authored variant that taxes ÷ gross ≤ 12% for the student's own stub. The
auditor's kill shot is designed out by test.
**4. FICA does not fund the library.** The destination graph is data, and a test forbids any
edge from social-security or medicare to school, library, road or ambulance. This is also the
assessment: the wrong citation is the attractive one.
**5. Nor is state income tax the sole funder of a library.** Marlow's revenue page shows the mix
with property tax as the largest local share, fees, and state aid. The correct citation for the
library question is the state-aid row, and the write-up rubric accepts "part of it" — a student
who claims their check paid for the new hours outright has over-claimed and the scorer sees why.
**6. Non-partisan.** No copy anywhere evaluates whether taxes are too high or too low, or
whether a program deserves funding. Guard: extend the existing neutrality test with a banned-
phrase list; content review by a person before ship.
**7. Taxes vs. deductions.** The $12.00 staff shirt is a payroll deduction and not a tax, has no
fund tray, and is what makes ER1 a judgement. NYSED 3.2 names both categories, so this is
standards-faithful rather than a trick.
**8. Employment realism.** The student is 15. The certification is a county Red Cross course the
student obtains themselves, not unpaid training demanded by the employer — that distinction is a
wage-law issue and the world must not get it wrong. Hours stay inside New York's school's-out
limits for 15-year-olds. Flagged for content review, not settled by an implementer.
**9. The correction, not the accusation.** The hours discrepancy is a payroll error, framed as
ordinary and fixable. No copy suggests the employer stole anything.

**Kill condition.** **Primary: the postings become wallpaper.** If, in playtest instrumentation, fewer than about
40% of students scroll to or dwell on a posting's small print before committing a rail figure,
and median time across all three postings is under 45 seconds, then the same-footing rail is a
worksheet with three multiplications and the entire inspection stance has bought nothing over a
table BOW could have printed. Kill the design; the trap is not fair and the evidence is not
about reading documents.
**Secondary: the discrepancy is free or invisible.** If the hours error is caught by more than
90% or fewer than 15% of students, it is not a verification decision and it comes out — with it
goes the argument that deductions must be recomputed rather than transcribed, and the split
mechanic has to be re-justified from scratch.
**Tertiary: the citation cannot be scored reliably.** If two trained observers disagree on what-
taxes-fund ER2 more than 1 run in 10, the destination graph is not discriminating and the row is
a preference.

**What it does not prove.** Not that the student could fill in a W-4 or choose withholding — that decision is nowhere in
this world. Not that they understand tax brackets or progressivity: deliberately out of scope,
absent from 3.2 and 3.3, and wrong to teach off a first job. Not that they can budget across a
real month of variable expenses — that is 1.3's world and this plan rail is smaller on purpose.
Not long-horizon career reasoning: the furthest this world looks is one more summer, so "will
this training pay off in ten years" is untouched. Not that they would spot a stub error
unprompted in life — the world guarantees a discrepancy exists somewhere, and knowing that is
most of the work. And a null on any row means the opportunity never arrived, most often because
the run did not finish; it is not a zero and no surface may render it as one.

**Visual identity.** Take the title off and a reviewer knows it in one second: **this is the World made of other
people's paper, and the only colour on the desk is yours.**
Everything is a document with a physical edge. Corkboard postings with pin shadows and a torn
tab at the bottom. A pay stub on perforated stock with a real tear line and a payroll printer's
monospace. A ruled index card for the shift record. A stapled town revenue page with a staple
shadow and a page-2 fold. Documents slide *under* one another like a desk being worked, never
fade or float.
Palette: manila and newsprint — warm off-white, a colder grey-white for official pages, black
ledger type. Exactly one accent, a highlighter yellow, and it is reserved: **nothing in this
world is highlighted until the student highlights it.** Every yellow mark on screen is a mark a
student made. That single rule is the identity, and it is also the design thesis — the
annotation is the evidence, so the annotation is the only thing with colour in it.
Typography: a ledger monospace for every figure without exception, so a number always looks like
a number and columns line up down the page; a plain humanist sans for the few dozen sentences
the world speaks in its own voice. The two never mix inside a line.
The one non-paper object in the world is the take-home rule: a solid ink bar under the gross
figure that gets shorter. It is the only thing that changes shape, so it is the thing you
remember.
Motion is paper motion only: a 180ms tear, a document sliding a few pixels as it comes to the
top of the pile. Nothing bounces, nothing glows, nothing celebrates.
Against the shipped Worlds: Basketball is a gym and a grid of weeks; the Pop-Up is a stall of
crates and trays under lights. This one is a desk at closing time.

### Fifteen Hours, One Envelope

**Carries** `gross-to-net`, `what-taxes-fund`, `compare-earning-paths`  
**Verbs** PLEDGE · COUNT · REPAIR · TRACE · WEIGH  
**Budget** 1750 words / 26 minutes

*A student splits a first paycheck across promises they already made, watches the pay be divided into four named parts before it reaches them, then has to re-fit those promises to the envelope that actually came through the glass — and follow every dollar that didn't.*

You got the summer job at the Kettle Street wash. Fifteen hours this pay period, sixteen dollars
an hour — the schedule board says $240.00. You have already told people things. Dev gets his
sixty back. Your share of the phone. Three seats on the crew bus. Boots for the bay. The fair,
Saturday. Friday at four you go to the pay window, and what comes through the glass is not
$240.00. Nothing was stolen. Every dollar that did not reach you went somewhere with a name on
it. Find out where, then fix Friday.

**Signature mechanic.** THE PAY WINDOW — the counting-out behind glass. Screen, top to bottom (it stacks; nothing
depends on side-by-side): THE TRAY, behind a sliding glass pane, holding "WHAT THE WORK PAID —
$240.00 · 15 hours × $16.00". Below the tray, cut into the brass counter, FOUR SLOTS with
engraved name plates: SOCIAL SECURITY · 6.2% of gross; MEDICARE · 1.45% of gross; NEW YORK STATE
INCOME TAX; FEDERAL INCOME TAX. Focus or press any plate before the split runs and it says its
rate and computes its amount — the information needed to beat the trap is on the counter before
the trap. Press a slot expecting to close it and a small tag rises: "This one is not a choice.
Every paycheck in the country runs through it." Then the student pulls THE HANDLE ("Run the
split"). Money drops out of the tray into the lit slots: $14.88 deep blue into Social Security,
$3.48 oxblood into Medicare, $0.00 into each income-tax slot, each zero slot tagged "You will
earn less this year than the amount this tax starts at. The line is still on your stub." Nothing
fades. The four amounts do not vanish — they travel down into THE RAIL, a labelled row along the
bottom captioned WHERE IT WENT, and they stay there for the rest of the Challenge. The glass
stays SHUT. Beside it a keypad field: "The glass opens when you say what is left on the tray."
The net is printed nowhere. Type $240.00 and the glass does not move — and the tray in front of
you visibly holds less than it did, so the correction is available by looking. Two wrong entries
and a direct hint appears ("Count what is still on the tray, not what was on it before") which
caps the row at 3; a third failure supplies the answer and the row records nothing. On the right
number the pane slides, the remainder passes through into THE ENVELOPE, and the envelope's face
prints $221.64. From that moment the envelope is the only money in the world: it hangs on the
shed board, and the board's brass measuring slider is still sitting on $240.00 where the student
left it on Wednesday. The same window is used twice more — once with the crew chief's stub
loaded instead of yours, once per career route in the last beat — so the student meets the split
three times at three scales and never meets a second machine.

**The tradeoff.** The $16.36 can be closed four ways and every one costs something in a different currency: part-
pay Dev (a friend gets $45 of the $60 he was told, and the recap shows you telling him), drop a
bus seat (the crew is a seat short and somebody else covers it), let the boots go (four hours
you cannot work the bay next period, so next check is visibly smaller), drop the $52 fair share
(you are out on Saturday and the block booking closes). Money, a person, next check, and the
thing you were looking forward to — not commensurable, so there is no ranking the student can
compute their way out of. Critically, NO evidence requirement in this world reads which claim
moved. The value judgement is real and unscored, which is what stops "give up the fun thing"
from being a right answer. In beat 5 the goal is stated and the routes genuinely disagree with
each other under it: staying on the crew costs nothing and lands just short; the seven-week CDL
reaches the stated level soonest and cheapest; the two-year program reaches a much higher level
and still clears its cost inside the stated rule; the four-year degree is the highest take-home
of the four and arrives two years after the stated date. No route is scored correct. **The one
near-dominant move is dropping the $52 fair share, which over-frees $16.36 in a single action.**
It stays in, because over-freeing still requires having measured against the envelope to know
you needed to, and nothing scores the choice. If playtest shows students dropping it without
ever setting the measure, the fix is to close the fair booking on Wednesday and leave only
sub-$16.36 moves on the board, forcing at least two.

**Good thinking, bad outcome.** Amina reads the slot plates on Wednesday, works the deductions out before the handle is pulled,
enters $221.64 first try, sets the board's measure onto the envelope before touching a claim,
and then decides to part-pay Dev $45 of the $60 and keep the boots, because she cannot work the
bay without them. Friday's recap shows Dev leaving for his cousin's on Saturday with fifteen
dollars still owed, and the world says so plainly and without moralising. It is the outcome she
would least have chosen. Every gross-to-net row survives it intact, because every one of them
reads a different thing: er1 reads the keypad, er2 reads the measure act, er3 reads the dollars
freed and where from, er4 reads the declared shortfall on Dev's slip. Not one reads who was
disappointed or which claim moved. She scores 5 across all four. The same shape recurs in beat
5: she picks the CDL because it meets the stated level and date at the lowest cost, and the card
beside it shows a permanently larger monthly number she has just walked past. er4 reads her
reasoning and her own payback figure, never the size of the salary she declined.

**Bad thinking, good outcome.** Theo never focuses a slot plate. He guesses the deduction is "about twenty bucks", types $220.00
— glass shut. Types $221.00 — glass shut. Takes the hint on the third try and gets in, so er1
stands at 3 under the direct_scaffold cap. He never touches the measure control; he just watches
the red line and drops the $52 fair share, which over-frees the $16.36 by $35.64. His board fits
by a wide margin, Friday plays out with nothing unpaid and nobody disappointed, and the screen
looks like a clean run. What stops the product reporting mastery: `board.measure_set` never
fired, so er2 scores 2 with the misconception "plan from the number on the offer letter"
attached; er1 is held to 3 by the support cap the engine applies, not by the world's own
generosity; er3 and er4 score well, because he genuinely did free enough and did commit inside
the envelope, and that is honest — he can clear a red line. gross-to-net rolls up as developing,
not demonstrated, and the objective it maps to does not read demonstrable for him. At the rail
he marks both $0.00 lines "none" and what-taxes-fund er1 scores 2 with the specific diagnosis a
teacher can act on. The tidy Friday appears nowhere in the report, because no row in this world
reads the outcome.

**Why not a button.** The financial action being represented is not "calculate net pay". It is money that was earned
by you being divided, by a system you did not choose and cannot switch off, before it ever
reaches your hand. A `SHOW NET PAY` button prints a number and asserts all of that in a caption.
The window makes each part of it a property of the world instead: the deductions are not
optional because there is no handle that closes a slot and pressing one says so; the money did
not disappear because it drops into a rail that stays on screen for the next twenty minutes and
is the literal material of the next beat; and you cannot spend the gross because the glass is
shut until you can say what is actually there. The tray is also the same object the shed board
was measured against four minutes earlier, so the shortfall arrives as something the student
observes rather than something the interface announces. And the machine is reused rather than
replaced — the crew chief's stub and every career route run through the same four slots — so "an
advertised rate is not what you get" is one fact learned once and applied three times at three
scales.

**Would clicking be better.** The honest case for the button: for gross-to-net er1 alone, a plain field labelled "Take-home
pay = gross − deductions" beside a printed stub would collect identical evidence in four
seconds, work on every device, need no new primitive, and cost nothing in reading budget. The
glass, the handle and the tray add zero information to that row. If er1 were all this world had
to produce, I would build the field and delete the window, and I would be right to. It is not
all this world has to produce. The window earns its build on three rows it is the only cheap way
to reach: er2 depends on the student having planned against a figure that was true and is now
stale, which requires the gross to have been a live instrument and not a printed fact; what-
taxes-fund er2 and er3 depend on the deducted money being a persistent object with a named
destination rather than a subtraction that has already happened by the time the student arrives;
and the "deductions are optional" misconception is killed by a slot that will not close, where a
button would need a sentence telling the student a rule. Everything else in the world is
deliberately not theatre: the board is native radios and steppers, the rail is a list, the scale
is two checkbox groups with a hook limit. There is exactly one bespoke object in this Challenge
and it is the one carrying four rubric rows.

**Accessibility path.** There is no worksheet fallback; the keyboard path is the same instruments, the same numbers and
the same acts. The construct being preserved is three claims — money is routed to named places
and still exists, the envelope is smaller than the schedule, and claims must be measured against
the envelope — and none of the three is carried by motion, colour, sound or position. THE WINDOW
is a section containing: an `<output>` for the tray total; a `<ul>` of four slots, each a
`<button>` whose accessible name is "Social Security, 6.2 percent of $240.00, $14.88, not a
choice"; a "Run the split" `<button>`; an `aria-live="polite"` region that after the split reads
all four routed amounts and then "the glass is shut"; a labelled `<input inputmode="decimal">`
for the net that receives focus automatically; and the glass state as text, never as an image.
The rail is a list a screen-reader user can re-read at any time, each item "$14.88 — Social
Security — where it went". THE BOARD needs no drag: each claim is a fieldset with a radio pair
(this check / next check), a stepper for adjustable amounts, and a part-pay stepper — all
existing BOW primitives with existing tests. The measure is a two-option radio group, "Measure
this board against: the schedule ($240.00) / the envelope ($221.64)", so the act that carries
er2 is a native control, not a gesture. The two-stub comparison is a real `<table>` with `<th
scope>` on both axes, reflowing under 480px into a stacked definition list with the same cells
and headers. THE SCALE is two `<fieldset>`s of checkboxes with hook limits enforced in the DOM
(further boxes disable, announced), and an `<output aria-live="polite">` printing "pays for
itself in 9 months, measured against extra take-home over the crew". Reduced motion: tokens
appear in the rail without transit and the glass toggles state without sliding — the live-region
text is byte-identical either way. Muted audio: the clunk is decoration; "the glass is still
shut" is text. Colour: the four ink colours never appear without the line's name in text beside
them. 200% zoom and 640px: single column throughout, tray then slots then handle then keypad
then envelope, which is also the reading order the screen reader gets.

**Where it could teach something false.** 1) FICA. 6.2% Social Security + 1.45% Medicare, employee side; no wage-base or additional-
Medicare complication at these amounts. Guard: rates live in one constants file with a source
comment; a table-driven test asserts the four stub lines sum to the stated deductions to the
cent and that rounding is half-up on the period total, never per hour (per-hour rounding
produces a five-cent drift and a student who checks would be right and the world wrong). 2)
Federal withholding at $0.00. Correct for a summer earner well under the standard deduction, and
it is the single most valuable surprise on the stub — but it must be said as a fact about this
year's earnings, never as "teenagers don't pay federal tax". Guard: the tag reads "You will earn
less this year than the amount this tax starts at," and the crew chief's stub carries a real
federal line so the world never implies the line is always zero. 3) NY State at $0.00 is
defensible at roughly $3,000 of summer earnings and is phrased the same year-based way; the crew
chief's stub carries the non-zero state line. 4) The destination cards are where this design
could teach something false and it is the highest risk in the build: Social Security and
Medicare must never route to schools, libraries or roads. Social Security goes to monthly
payments for retired and disabled people and to children whose parent died; Medicare to hospital
and doctor care for people 65 and over; state income tax to schools, roads and state services;
federal income tax to national things. A build that accepts SS→schools without marking it wrong
is a failing build, asserted in tests, not left to review. 5) "An account with your name that
you get back at 65" is deliberately a distractor card, because it is the most common wrong model
of Social Security and it must be marked wrong rather than left unaddressed. 6) The crew chief's
withholding and all four routes' take-home figures must come from one dated spreadsheet of
record with its assumptions written beside it (single filer, standard deduction, NY resident, no
dependents, 2026 tables), checked by a finance reviewer, and frozen as constants; no figure on a
route card ships without a source in the content file and a year printed on the card. 7) The
scale's payback ignores interest on borrowed tuition. Guard: one plate on the scale, eleven
words — "This does not count interest if you borrow the money." 8) Tone and law: 15 hours over
two weeks at 14–15 with working papers is legal and modest; a design showing a middle-schooler
at forty hours would be both wrong and irresponsible. 9) Non-partisan check: the world states
where money goes and who receives it, and the county lever shows the alternative as "these bills
do not vanish — they are paid by the person, or their family, or not at all," which is a fact
about who pays, not an argument about whether they should.

**Kill condition.** Abandon the design if playtest shows the net figure is obtainable without producing it —
students copying it from a neighbour's screen, from a stray render, or brute-forcing the keypad
— because er1 and the whole shut-glass premise collapse together. Abandon it equally if
`board.measure_set` fires before any `envelope.read` for most students: that would mean the act
carrying er2 is a chore, not a judgement, and there is no version of this world worth building
where its central row is fake. A softer kill, taken as a scope cut rather than an abandonment:
if the whole Challenge runs past 28 minutes in two consecutive classroom trials, beat 5 comes
out as a unit, compare-earning-paths is dropped from the claim, and 3.2 and 3.3 are delivered
whole — the beat was built as a removable block precisely so that this cut costs nothing already
earned.

**What it does not prove.** It does not prove the student can rank competing claims by need, want and value — the world
deliberately never scores which claim moved, so nothing here speaks to sort-by-need-want-goal.
It does not prove they can build a budget from nothing: the five claims arrive already made,
which is what creates the trap and also what means plan-within-income is untouched. It does not
prove they understand progressive brackets, marginal rates, a W-4, or why the two income-tax
lines are zero beyond the sentence the world gives them. It does not prove any long-run career
judgement: beat 5 tests one comparison against one stated goal at one moment, and a student who
reasons it well may still be reasoning from four cards someone else wrote. And a student who
clears the overhang by dropping the largest movable claim without deliberating has still
demonstrated er3 and er4 honestly — this world can see that they can clear a red line, and
cannot see whether they chose well.

**Visual identity.** Brass, paper and one pane of glass. Take the title off and a reviewer knows it in a second by
two things: the four engraved slot plates set into a counter, and four ink colours that never
change meaning anywhere in the Challenge — Social Security deep blue, Medicare oxblood, state
green, federal charcoal — appearing on your stub, on the rail, on the crew chief's stub beside
yours, and again on every career route's split. Two typefaces doing semantic work rather than
decoration: a receipt monospace for every number the payroll system states, and a pencil face on
cork for everything a person promised. So a figure's face tells you instantly whether it is a
fact of the system or something you said to a friend, which is the world's whole subject. The
motion vocabulary is one rule, and the rule is the argument: money only ever drops (down into a
slot) or slides (through the glass). Nothing in this world fades, shrinks, sparkles or vanishes,
because the thing the Challenge is trying to say is that the money did not vanish. Basketball is
a court and a season strip; the night market is stalls and crates; this is a counter, four
plates and a pane you have to earn your way through.

## Module 4 · Risk Management

### Six Kits, One Fund

**Carries** `plan-for-the-unexpected`, `use-insurance`, `is-the-add-on-worth-it`  
**Verbs** COVER · RESERVE · SETTLE  
**Budget** 1750 words / 24 minutes

*The student lays one term's money out on the crew's worktable — pooled cover, their own reserve tin, one add-on tag, the festival fund — watches what each choice leaves sticking out on their own gear, and then, as the crew's treasurer, settles all six members' repairs out of the one shared tray.*

You run the money for Frame Six, the after-school film crew. Six of you, six kits, one term.
Everyone's gear is theirs to look after, and gear breaks. That's why the crew keeps a Fund:
everybody puts in, and it pays out to whoever gets hit. This term you pick your own cover,
decide what to hold back for yourself, and — because you're the treasurer — you settle the
crew's repairs out of the tray at the end. Then there's the festival. Entry closes Friday.

**Signature mechanic.** THE TABLE. One top-down worktable, lamp-lit, is every screen in the world. Down the left edge
runs a printed money ruler marked in $10 rungs — every amount in the world is drawn against it,
and that single shared scale is the whole design.
Your bench (bottom): four gear items stand as bars whose HEIGHT is their replacement cost, each
with a gaffer-tape label — "CAMERA BODY · $180 · broke 1 of last 6 terms", "MIC · $40 · broke 4
of last 6". Your envelope holds $150 in fifteen $10 chips.
The middle of the table holds three kraft-card Fund cards with die-cut chip slots printed on
them: NO COVER (0 slots), BASIC ($20, 2 slots, "pays up to $60 of any one repair"), FULL ($70, 7
slots, "pays up to $150"). You pay by dropping chips into the slots. Then you SLIDE the card up
over your gear row — and it casts a translucent amber band from the shelf line to its ceiling.
Every part of a bar inside the band is covered. Every part above it turns chalk red and prints
its own number: "YOU PAY $120". That red stub is the object this whole world is about, and it is
the only thing in the world drawn in that colour. Slide a different card over and the stubs
jump. Slide the card off and every bar goes red to the top.
Right: THE TIN, an open metal tin with a fill line drawn on the same ruler. Chips dropped in
raise the fill. Because the tin and the red stubs share one scale, "is my reserve as tall as
what's sticking out?" is answered by eye across the table before any arithmetic — then confirmed
by the printed numbers.
Also on the table: one TAG ($30, three slots) that clips to exactly one item and pays that
item's whole replacement; and the FESTIVAL LADDER, three rungs you pledge chips onto — $30
ENTRY, $60 +POSTER, $90 +BUS TO THE PREMIERE. A chip is in exactly one place. Fifteen chips,
four places.
Top rail: five enamel-pin badges — Dev, Nia, Marco, Priya, Sam — each with their own kit and
their own Fund card already chosen and printed on the badge. Their chips sit in the FUND TRAY in
the centre of the table with a running total. When you fill a card's slots, your chips slide out
of your envelope into that same tray, stacking with theirs. That is the pool, and it is a
physical container from the first minute.
"LOAD THE TRUCK" locks the table.
Three shoot days deal the term's repairs. At the end the table clears to the LEDGER — the same
table, six columns — and you become the treasurer: for each of the four repairs, you slide that
member's own Fund card over their bill bar and pay the covered part out of the tray, the
remainder into that member's own column. The tray drains as you go and cannot be asked for more
than it holds. Four bills, four different shapes: yours, covered; Nia's $180 camera under a $150
ceiling (a split you set); Marco's $50 tripod with no cover at all; Priya's tagged laptop, which
must be routed to the shop's tab and never touch the tray. Last, you drag whatever is left in
the tray into the NEXT TERM slot — and the card prices on the table visibly change, because that
is what actually sets them.
Then the second term: the same table, re-dressed. The mic is gone, the school has lent the crew
a $300 camera for the away shoot, the tag on it costs $50, and the Fund's ceiling still stops at
$150. You re-arrange rather than rebuild.

**The tradeoff.** Fifteen chips; four places. Protection competes with the festival, and inside protection three
different shapes compete: REACH (the card, high ceiling, covers everything a bit), DEPTH (the
tin, covers anything but only as far as it fills) and ONE THING ABSOLUTELY (the tag). They are
the three real strategies — transfer, retain, and in Season 2 avoid — and the board has a
physical place for each.
Dominant-strategy audit, run honestly: at least three structurally different boards score 5
across the arrangement rows. Full $70 + tin $40 + entry $40 (the tail is $30, funded). Basic $20
+ tin $60 + entry and poster $60 (lens gap $60 funded, the 1-in-6 camera knowingly bare). No
cover + tin $120 + entry $30 (self-insurance, actually funded for every likely item — and it
costs the entire rest of the festival). None of these is BOW's preferred answer and the rubric
names none of them.
What stops "just buy everything" being mark-optimal: R2.er1 scores 2 for the most expensive card
with an empty tin, and R3.er1 scores 2 for buying the tag where the stub is smaller than the
tag's price. Caution is not free evidence. The residual I will not pretend away: heavy
protection with a funded tin is always a defensible board, because it is defensible in life. Its
price is the poster and the bus rungs — the story's stakes, not the mark's.
Season 2's decision is a genuine trilemma with no virtuous option: take the school's $300 camera
and tag it ($50, eats the bus rung), take it bare (nobody's board can pay $300), or don't take
it (give up the wide shots the festival ladder names).

**Good thinking, bad outcome.** A student takes Basic ($20, reach $60), fills the tin to $60 — which funds the largest gap on
every item the log book calls likely — and pledges $60 to entry and poster, leaving the 1-in-6
camera body knowingly bare. This is a coherent, defensible risk position. The deck then breaks
the camera: $180, the Fund pays $60, the tin pays $60, and they drag $60 back off the ladder,
losing the poster. The film goes in plain and their term looks like a disaster.
Their evidence is untouched by that. R1.er1 is read off the lock snapshot before any card was
dealt: 5. R1.er2 reads whether the tin ever dipped: 5. R1.er3 explicitly does not read the size
of the loss — it reads whether the card was claimed first, the tin second, the rung un-pledged
last, with nothing unpaid: 5. R2.er1 reads the card against the kit, not the draw. R2.er2/er3
are read off the treasurer step, which is the same for everyone. R3's four rows are read off the
two tag decisions and the routing. Not one of the twelve rows can see whether the student's term
went well. The only thing that got worse was the story, which is where consequence belongs.

**Bad thinking, good outcome.** A student slides No cover onto the rail without ever previewing what the other cards reach,
leaves the tin empty, and pledges the whole $150 to the bus rung. The deck gives them the $40
mic and nothing else — the cheapest hit in the deck. They pull $40 off the ladder, still make
the festival with the poster, and finish the term looking fine.
The product reports no mastery. R1.er1 = 0: nothing was committed to protection at either lock.
R1.er2 = 0: there was never a reserve to keep. R2.er1 = 2: the cheapest card taken with four
items standing above its reach and nothing behind them. R1.er3 they can still earn — they
settled their bill correctly — and that is honest: paying what you owe from what you have is a
real, small competence, and it is one row out of twelve. R1.er4 turns on whether the Season-2
board changes; if they repeat it, 2. R3.er4 catches the specific bad inference — "nothing broke,
so I don't need any of this" — as its named level-2 pattern.
The structural guarantee is the one the module needs: no row anywhere reads the student's
outcome. Their $40 draw could have been Nia's $180 and their evidence would be identical.

**Why not a button.** The number this entire module turns on — what is left on you after the cover — does not exist
until the student makes it. A "BUY FULL · $70" button prices coverage; it cannot show reach. The
board draws the premium's reach, the item's cost and the reserve's depth against one ruler, so
the question "is my tin as tall as what's sticking out?" is answerable by eye before any
arithmetic, and the answer is an object the student produced by sliding a card.
That matters because the named misconception here is "a lower premium is always better", which
is precisely the error of comparing two prices with no model of what the price buys. A button
list makes price the salient dimension and reach a sentence of small print — it teaches the
misconception's own habit. The board inverts it: the price is three chip slots, boring and
quickly done, and the reach is the amber band that repaints your shelf.
And it is the same gesture on someone else's life. Sliding Nia's card over Nia's $180 bill is
the identical act that sized your own camera, which is what makes the treasurer step an
application of a model rather than a comprehension quiz about a ledger. Buttons cannot reuse
anything, because they encode conclusions rather than the relation the conclusions come from.

**Would clicking be better.** The steelman is strong and I will not pretend otherwise. Radio buttons for the three cards, a
number field for the reserve, a checkbox for the tag, a form for each settlement: faster,
keyboard-native from birth, zero motor cost, no drag physics to test on a Chromebook — and every
one of my twelve evidence requirements is still loggable from it, because they read committed
amounts and routing choices, not gestures. A judge who says "this is a form with a lamp on it"
is describing a real product that would probably ship sooner.
Where it fails is narrow and decisive: it cannot produce the stub. With a form, "you would pay
$120" is a computed line the interface prints for you — which is the system doing the target
reasoning, the hard fail this doctrine names. On the board the student produces that comparison
by placing a card and reads it off a shared scale; the interface reports two independent
quantities (cost, reach) and never their difference as a verdict.
Where the button wins, I use it. The tag is a near-button — one clip, one gesture, no
manipulation — because it is a yes/no at a stated price and needs nothing more. The festival
ladder is a stack-drop with a stepper, and if playtest shows it eating time it becomes a plain
stepper and nothing is lost, because no evidence requirement reads it. The design spends
interaction only where the representation carries the concept and stays button-cheap everywhere
else. Weakest link, named: chip-by-chip dragging is motor work masquerading as finance if you
have to do it fifteen times, so chips move in grabbed stacks and every place accepts a typed or
stepped amount.

**Accessibility path.** The construct is two things: money located in one place at a time, and one amount measured
against another on a shared scale. Both survive without sight, without a mouse and without
motion, in the same interface everyone else uses.
KEYBOARD. The table is a grid of named places (envelope, three cards, tin, tag, four ladder
rungs, each item, each crew badge, the tray). Tab moves between places, arrows within one. Space
picks up ("picked up 4 chips from the envelope"), Tab, Enter drops ("2 chips into Basic — Basic
is paid, 0 slots left, 2 chips still in hand"). Every place also takes a typed amount. Sliding a
card is an action on the card itself ("slide onto my shelf" / "slide onto Nia's bill"); no
pointer path is ever required.
SCREEN READER. Focusing an item speaks exactly the quantities the geometry shows, in the same
order: "Camera body. Replace $180. Basic reaches $60. You would pay $120. Your tin holds $60."
Sliding a card re-announces the shelf as a list: "Under Basic — mic covered; light covered;
lens, you pay $60; camera, you pay $120. Largest amount on you, $120. Tin, $60." That is not a
worksheet substitute; it is the identical three-quantity comparison, and the "largest on you
versus tin" line is the same juxtaposition the ruler makes visually. A commit-level live region
announces the board after each committed change, never on keystrokes.
REDUCED MOTION. Chips arrive without transit, the band appears rather than sweeping, the tray
total steps rather than counts up. Nothing in the world is understood only through a transition.
200% ZOOM AND 640px. Bars rotate to horizontal rows sharing one max width set by the largest
amount on the table, so the common scale is preserved rather than lost; each row renders as
covered segment then red stub, both labelled in dollars. The ruler becomes a top rule with $10
ticks and a printed "1 rung = $10". The crew badges become a scrollable list of the same rows.
The shelf never becomes a table of numbers with the comparison removed.
COLOUR AND AUDIO. Covered versus on-you is carried by a hatch fill and a printed dollar label as
well as by colour; nothing in the world is signalled by sound alone. No timers anywhere, so
nothing is lost to slower input.

**Where it could teach something false.** 1. CAPS ARE NOT DEDUCTIBLES. The cards carry a per-repair ceiling and nothing else. Copy must
say "pays up to $60 of any one repair" and never "deductible", which is a different mechanism
and would teach it wrong by association. Guard: a copy test forbidding the word.
2. RE-PRICING MUST READ AS ARITHMETIC, NOT PUNISHMENT. Next term's card price comes from the
tray's carry-forward, shown as "took in $200, paid out $190, ends $10 over" and applied to every
member equally. It must never be attached to who claimed. Guard: the re-price screen has no
member names on it.
3. THE POOL MUST NOT ALWAYS RUN OUT, AND MUST NOT ALWAYS WIN. Season 1 is tuned to end over;
Season 2's deck ends it short. Both arithmetics are shown side by side on the end card. Either
alone teaches a falsehood.
4. EXTENDED WARRANTIES MUST STAY HONESTLY UNATTRACTIVE WHERE THEY ARE. Season 1's numbers are
deliberate: mic $40 at 4-in-6 is $26.67 of expected loss against a $30 tag; camera $180 at
1-in-6 is exactly $30 — a true coin-edge that expected value alone cannot settle, and that
ability-to-absorb does. Season 2's tag is right for most boards only because a $300 loss sits
above the Fund's ceiling and beyond any tin, not because "warranties pay off". Guard: a balance
sweep asserting no single tag answer is correct at both offers.
5. NO ITEM MAY BE PAID MORE THAN IT IS WORTH. The tag pays replacement, the tray pays up to the
ceiling, and no route ever hands a member a profit on a loss.
6. NO HARDSHIP, NO SHAME. Every dollar in this world is club and shoot money, never household
income. Marco carries no cover because he is keeping his money for the bus, said once,
neutrally, and the ending never moralises about him. Nobody in the crew is depicted as unable to
afford anything.
7. "SHARED RISK" IS EARNED, NOT ASSERTED. The phrase does not appear in copy before the student
has settled four claims out of one tray. The write-up prompt does not use it either, so a
student cannot pass er4 by echoing a word the world handed them.

**Kill condition.** If, in playtest, the median student cannot say in their own words what the red part sticking out
of their camera bar IS — if it stays a graphic rather than becoming "the money I'd have to find"
— this design dies rather than gets patched. Every one of the twelve requirements rests on the
stub being a financial idea the student holds; if the geometry does not convert, no amount of
copy will make the band mean anything and the honest move is to go back to the button version
and accept a smaller claim. Second kill: if a seed sweep ever shows the draw moving a rubric
level, the world is catastrophe roulette in a nicer jacket and must not ship.

**What it does not prove.** It does not prove a student can read a real policy — no declarations, exclusions, deductibles,
co-pays, premiums-by-risk-class, or claims processes appear, and this world would be a poor
foundation for claiming any of them. It says nothing about health, auto, renters' or liability
insurance specifically, and nothing about insurance the student does not personally choose. It
does not touch 4.4: no identity theft, no phishing, no protective action on personal
information, and the module still needs a separate world for it. It shows a reserve held across
one term and one interruption, not the months-long habit an emergency fund actually is. And it
is a club treasurer's table, not a household — it shows that a student can manage risk on shared
gear with visible numbers, not that they would do it with their own money in a world with no
ruler drawn on it.

**Visual identity.** Take the title away and you know it by the red stub. One colour — chalk red — is reserved in the
entire world for the part of an amount that would come out of your own pocket, and it appears
nowhere else: not in errors, not in warnings, not in the crew badges. A reviewer seeing a
screenshot with a bar half amber and half red on a walnut table knows which World this is.
Everything else supports that. A dark walnut worktable seen top-down, one warm task lamp pooling
light on the bench so the lit area is where the decision is and the rest of the table falls off
into shadow. Objects have weight and edges: brass-toned chips that stack, a dented metal tin
with a fill line, kraft-card Fund cards with real die-cut chip slots and a paper shadow when
they slide, an enamel-pin badge for each crew member instead of an avatar. Labels are gaffer
tape — hand-lettered condensed caps, slightly off-square — and money is set in a mono face so
amounts line up down the ruler. The ruler itself, printed down the left edge in $10 rungs, is
the world's signature furniture: every quantity in the world, premium, repair bill, tin depth,
festival rung, is drawn against that one scale, which is both the design idea and the identity.
Motion is short and physical: chips slide and settle with a small overshoot, the card casts its
amber band as it comes to rest, the tray total ticks. Nothing floats, nothing sparkles, nothing
celebrates. Against BOW's existing pair — Basketball's court and season strip, Pop-Up's night
market — this is a lamp-lit table at six in the evening after school, and every screen in the
run is the same table re-dressed rather than a new place.

### Ten Weeks, One Horn

**Carries** `use-insurance`, `is-the-add-on-worth-it`, `plan-for-the-unexpected`  
**Verbs** COVER · HOLD · JUDGE · SETTLE  
**Budget** 1900 words / 25 minutes

*The student lays a strip of cover tape across the weeks of a ten-week band season — choosing how good the cover is and which weeks it is switched on — holds back cash for what cover will not take, judges three shop add-on offers, and then reads the whole section's board to see whose losses fell inside their tape and whose did not.*

Your baritone cost $900, used. You have $240 from a summer of mowing lawns and ten weeks until
Regionals. Lessons with Ms. Okafor cost $18 each, and every one you take makes the audition go
better. Kessler's shop sells a repair plan by the week — you pick how much it covers and which
weeks it is switched on. Eleven other players in your section are deciding right now too. Last
season four of twelve had a repair. Nobody knows who is next.

**Signature mechanic.** **The cover tape on the shop's season board.** The screen is one sheet of kraft paper on
Kessler's repair counter. Across the middle runs a ten-cell week strip, Weeks 1–10, each cell
labelled with what happens that week; four cells carry a small printed van stamp (Wk 4 bus to
Ridgeway, Wk 6 parade, Wk 7 away game, Wk 10 Regionals) — the shop's own note that these are the
weeks horns get knocked about. Above the strip sits the horn on its tag: "Baritone — replace:
$900." Below the strip is a row of eight lesson slots, $18 each, the thing the money is actually
for.
The student grabs a **roll of cover tape** from the counter and drags it onto the strip. It
snaps to whole weeks. Two handles — left edge and right edge — set the start and end week. A
two-position switch sets the grade: thin blue **Basic** ($3/week, you pay the first $75, plan
pays up to $450) or thick amber **Full** ($8/week, you pay the first $25, plan pays up to $900).
As the tape stretches, three things move at once: the running premium ticks up, lesson slots at
the bottom go grey one by one (this is what the tape costs, counted in lessons), and the chip on
the horn's tag flips between "if it's damaged here, you pay $25" over taped weeks and "you pay
all of it" over bare ones. Bare weeks inside the season show a hatched hairline reading **no
cover — yours**.
To the left of the strip, above everything, is a **tin**. The student drags dollars into it. The
tin has no left or right edge — it sits over the whole season and is not attached to any week.
That contrast is the design: **cover is an extent in time with edges you chose; a reserve is a
pot with no edges.** Money in the tin cannot buy lessons.
A **steel ruler** lies across the board at Week 0. The student drags it right to play the
season. The rule, printed on the ruler: *weeks behind the ruler are settled — you cannot buy
cover for a week that has already happened.* Every inch of drag permanently forecloses a
decision. The ruler stops itself at Weeks 3, 4, 5, 7 and 8 where something is open; between
stops, red repair tags punch onto the board in other players' lanes as it passes.
After Week 10 the sheet scrolls up and becomes **the section board**: the same ten-week ruler,
now twelve lanes deep — eleven named players plus the student — every player's tape drawn at its
real length and grade, four red repair tags punched at their real weeks, one green case-plan
tag, and a ledger strip at the foot: *taken in $434 · paid out $490*. Sam's tag sits two weeks
to the left of where Sam's tape starts. That single picture is the whole of shared risk, and the
student reads it rather than being told it.

**The tradeoff.** Everything is priced in lessons, and lessons are what the season is for. Full cover all ten
weeks is $80 — four lessons and a bit. Full over the four van-stamped weeks is $32. Basic all
season is $30 but leaves a $75 deductible, so it demands a fatter tin, and its $450 cap does not
reach the horn. Money in the tin buys nothing and cannot buy a Week-3 lesson in Week 10, so
holding is spending in goal terms.
Three families all pass and none dominates: **wide and thin** (Basic all season plus a $75 tin),
**narrow and strong** (Full over the stamped weeks plus a bigger tin for quiet weeks), **broad
and strong** (Full all season, few lessons, small tin). The world's own data refuses to settle
it: two of the four section losses land in quiet weeks and two in stamped weeks, so "cover only
the trips" is smart and genuinely exposed. Tuning target for the balance sweep: no family
optimal across more than 45% of the seed space.
**The one thing that is not a live option, and I am saying so rather than pretending
otherwise:** carrying no cover at all on a $900 horn against $240. That fails, and it should — a
world is allowed wrong answers. It does not defeat the decision, because it settles only the
first of four questions; grade, window, tin size and lesson count all stay open after it, and
those are where the run actually happens.

**Good thinking, bad outcome.** A student reads the van stamps, buys Full over Weeks 4, 6, 7 and 10 for $32, holds $60 in the
tin against the quiet weeks, books four lessons, pays the Week-5 charge from the tin and refills
it. Seeded, their horn is damaged in Week 9 — a bare week — and the $140 comes out of the tin
and two unbooked lessons. They finish the season with fewer lessons than the classmate who
bought nothing and was untouched.
Every row survives, because no row reads their tag. `use-insurance.er1` scores the structure
they bought against the shop's published range, not against what happened; `er2`, `er3` and
`er4` are read entirely off other people's lanes on the section board. `plan-for-the-
unexpected.er1`–`er3` are read off the tin and the Week-5 charge, which every student faces
regardless of seed, and `er4` scores the acknowledgment chips on the bare weeks they chose
knowingly. Their recap states the $140 as a fact and never calls the plan wrong. The only thing
their bad luck costs them is lessons.

**Bad thinking, good outcome.** A student buys no cover, holds nothing, books all eight lessons, takes the mouthpiece plan
because the clerk offers it, declines the case plan because they are out of money, and is never
hit. They end the season with more lessons than anyone in the section.
The product reports: `use-insurance.er1` at 2 or 0 — no cover on a horn they could not replace;
`er2` and `er3` scored normally off the board, because reading other people's settlements is a
separate skill and they may well have it; `er4` scored by a person on what they wrote. `plan-
for-the-unexpected.er1` at 0, `er2` null or 0, `er3` at 2 (they met the charge by cancelling a
lesson), `er4` at 2. `is-the-add-on-worth-it.er1` and `er2` at 2 — they took the plan on the
cheap item without opening either fact. Nothing anywhere in the run asks whether their horn was
damaged; the seeded outcome touches no observation, appears in no observer input, and cannot
lift a level. The recap refuses to congratulate them: it prints their eight lessons beside
Theo's $780 and adds no comment.

**Why not a button.** The signature action is laying the tape, and a button version exists: two dropdowns, *Cover:
none / Basic / Full* and *Term: whole season / trips only*. Identical arithmetic, fewer pixels.
The tape earns its place for one reason a dropdown cannot reach: **the losses land on the same
ruler.** Sam's tag sits two weeks to the left of where Sam's tape starts. In tape, "he had
insurance and still paid the whole $95" is a thing you see; in a dropdown it is a sentence a
twelve-year-old has to be persuaded of. Coverage is an interval — the one property of insurance
that a menu structurally cannot render — and this world's whole assessment claim (`use-
insurance.er2`, the row about reading other people's lives) is a claim about intervals and
points.
Two further things only the representation gives. The tape's price is paid in lesson slots that
dim directly beneath the weeks being bought, so cost sits under the thing it bought rather than
in a total elsewhere. And when the Week-5 charge lands, paying it by dragging the right edge
back is the same gesture as handing away the rest of the season — a dropdown makes that a
neutral re-selection of a different option. `plan-for-the-unexpected.er3` is exactly that
gesture, and it is legible as a gesture.

**Would clicking be better.** Clicking would be better for most of this world, and it is what I have built almost everywhere.
The ruler advances on a button and on arrow keys, not only on a drag. The tin is a number you
set. The three add-on offers are cards with yes and no — no drag, because there is nothing
continuous to choose. The settlement splits are two typed amounts. I am defending drag in
exactly one place.
And I would kill the tape myself under two conditions. If cover were sold as one fixed term, the
tape would be theatre and two dropdowns would be honest; it survives only because start week,
end week and grade are three free variables with real prices. If the balance sweep showed one
window family optimal across the seed space, the window would be a decision in name only and the
tape should go with it — that is my first kill condition and it is not rhetorical.
The remaining honest charge is that dragging the ruler is watching. Partly true: between stops,
four peer tags punch on and the student reads. My defence is the printed rule — *you cannot buy
cover for a week that has already happened* — which makes advancing an act of foreclosure rather
than playback, and the ledger of it: passing Week 3 without a tape is a decision the log records
as a decision. Total unbroken watching across the run is under ninety seconds. Nothing about the
mark touches the pointer; every level is reachable with arrow keys alone, so motor skill is not
being assessed anywhere.

**Accessibility path.** The construct to preserve is: *cover is an extent in time with edges; a loss inside the extent
is paid by the plan less the deductible; a loss outside it is paid by the person; a reserve has
no extent.* None of that is carried by pixels.
**Keyboard and screen reader.** The tape is two `role="slider"` thumbs over a ten-step scale
plus a radio group for grade — a native pattern, not a bespoke one. `aria-valuetext` reads
"starts Week 3" and "ends Week 10". A polite live region speaks every change in the world's own
terms: "Full cover, Weeks 3 to 10. Eight weeks. $64 — that is three lessons. Weeks 1 and 2 bare:
a repair there costs you all of it." The ruler is a stepper with a *Play the week* button and
Left/Right keys, carrying the same foreclosure confirm the drag does. The tin is a number input.
Nothing needs a pointer.
**The section board is a `<table>`, and that is not a fallback — a twelve-by-ten grid is
natively tabular.** Row headers are players, column headers are weeks, and summary columns carry
grade, weeks in force, premium paid, repair, self-paid, plan-paid. A tag cell's accessible name
is the whole fact: "Week 2, Sam, slide damage $95, outside his cover — Sam paid $95, plan paid
$0." The inside/outside relation is stated per cell and independently inferable from the in-
force columns, so a screen-reader user meets the same object with the same redundancy a sighted
user does. The tag marks are two-state buttons in the cells; the splits are number inputs with a
running "must total $380" check.
**200% zoom and 640px.** The ruler rotates: weeks stack downward as rows, the tape becomes a
contiguous vertical run with handles at top and bottom, the lesson slots move to a right-hand
gutter. Extent survives rotation intact. The board renders twelve stacked lanes of ten 26px
cells — 260px wide, so all twelve lanes stay simultaneously visible, which is the property that
matters; below 480px it becomes a per-lane list keeping the summary columns and a standing
header, "12 paying · 4 repairs · in $434 · out $490", so the pool is never reduced to one life.
**Reduced motion:** the tape resizes without easing, tags appear rather than punch, the ruler
jumps week to week. **Muted audio:** no information is ever in sound; the week tick is
decorative. **Colour:** in-force versus bare uses fill, a diagonal hatch, and a per-cell text
mark ("in force" / "bare"), never colour alone; all text ≥ 4.5:1 on kraft.

**Where it could teach something false.** **Selling cover by the week.** Real instrument policies are annual, not weekly. Guard: the shop
states a weekly rate on a plan you switch on and off, never implies cover can be bought for a
week already passed, and never implies it can be bought after a loss. The concept being taught —
a policy is in force over a period, and losses outside it are yours — is exactly right; the
billing granularity is a simplification and the copy owns it in one line.
**Making extended warranties look like a good deal.** Retailers price service contracts well
above expected loss, and a world where the warranty pays off teaches something false. Guard: the
mouthpiece plan is the common case and is bad value on both tests ($9 against a $28 item at 1 in
10 over two years). The case plan is priced at $16 against an expected loss near $14 —
deliberately *above* fair value — so the only sound reason to take it is that you cannot absorb
$55 in Week 7 when you must have a case to travel. The recap never says a plan "paid off," and
the Week-8 renewal is written so that letting it go is a correct answer for a student who can
now absorb $55.
**The charity model of insurance.** Guard: the ledger prints both sides — in $434, out $490 —
and one line says the shop prices on many seasons, not one. This also blocks the opposite error:
no line implies the shop's margin makes the plan a con.
**The deductible read as a second fee.** Guard: the deductible appears only inside an actual
settlement, in the splitter, never as a standing charge.
**Reserve and insurance shown as substitutes.** Guard: the Week-5 charge is uninsurable by
construction and the copy says so — "no plan covers a bus charge; this is what the tin is for."
The tin's job is the deductible, the bare weeks, and the costs no plan touches.
**Small-number probability abuse.** "1 in 4 over three years" must never resolve as "1 in 4 this
trip." Guard: the seeded schedule is generated from the stated per-season rates, and a test
asserts the realised counts across the seed space match what the shop publishes — four horn
repairs in twelve lanes against a published four-in-twelve, one crushed case against one-in-
four-over-three-years.
**Theft framing.** Theo's $780 is stated flatly, once, with the school lending him a spare. No
pity, no blame, no lesson drawn at his expense. It is the only place the tail appears and it
must not be spectacle.

**Kill condition.** A balance sweep over the seed space — the same instrument `balance.ts` uses on the market —
showing one grade-plus-window family optimal across more than 60% of seeds after two rounds of
price tuning. That would mean the window is not a decision, the tape is decoration, and the
honest ship is two dropdowns and a smaller claim.
Second: classroom observation in which fewer than one student in five ever opens an item's
replacement tag or a failure-rate card before deciding an offer. Optional disclosures nobody
opens are not evidence of anything, and `is-the-add-on-worth-it.er1` and `er2` would both be
resting on a floor of zero — at which point the competency should be dropped from this world
rather than reported thin.
Third: if the Week-8 renewal produces a near-even split uncorrelated with students' own Week-8
cash, `er4` is reading coin flips and must be cut, and the `full` claim on 4.3 goes with it.

**What it does not prove.** It does not prove a student can price risk numerically — no expected value is ever computed or
observed, deliberately, at this grade band. It does not prove they understand insurance beyond
property: nothing here touches health, liability or life, and the pool is small, local and
voluntary in a way real markets are not. It does not prove they would decline cover on something
genuinely absorbable in a case the world did not stage; there are three add-on offers and they
are the three the world chose.
`is-the-add-on-worth-it.er4` reads one binary decision against a student's own numbers, and a
student can land it by accident — which is why it sits beside two disclosure-based rows and why
one attempt is a piece of evidence, never a claim about a child. `use-insurance.er4` is scored
by a person and BOW asserts nothing about the writing itself.
It says nothing at all about NYSED 4.4: identity theft is not in this world, `protect-your-
information` is not carried, and that objective stays not assessable, which is the truthful
thing for the coverage table to keep saying.
A `null` on any row means the run never reached that moment — a Chromebook that died at Week 6
leaves the board unseen and three rows null. That is not a low score and must never be rolled
into one.

**Visual identity.** Take the title away and it is still unmistakable: **this is the only BOW world made of paper and
tape.**
Environment — a repair-shop counter at Kessler & Sons Band Repair. Pegboard behind, valve oil, a
felt-lined case propped open, a paper wall calendar curling at the corner. Everything happens on
one kraft sheet on the counter; the world never cuts to another screen, it scrolls the sheet.
Object language — adhesive and stationery. Cover is masking tape in two weights: thin blue
Basic, thick amber Full, with visible fibre and slightly ragged ends where it was torn. Reserve
is a battered tin above the strip. Losses are oxide-red carbon repair tags, punched and
threaded, hanging off a week. The playhead is a steel ruler with a worn edge. Settled weeks sit
under a grey hatch, like graphite shading. Add-on plans are perforated card stubs with the price
rubber-stamped.
Typography — a hand-set stencil for headings ("KESSLER & SONS · SECTION BOARD"), a typewriter
face for the shop's printed lines, and a mono ledger face for every amount, right-aligned and
column-locked so twelve lanes read down at a glance.
Colour — kraft ground, graphite text, oxide red for tags, amber and dusty blue for the two tape
weights, a single green for the one claim the plan paid on a case. Deliberately no team colours
and no market lanterns: nothing here is a court and nothing is a stall.
Motion — the ruler slides with weight and a little friction; tape stretches from its torn end;
tags punch on and swing once. Under reduced motion the tape appears at length, tags fade, and
the ruler steps. Motion carries no information anywhere.

### Nine Riders, One Fund

**Carries** `use-insurance`, `is-the-add-on-worth-it`, `plan-for-the-unexpected`  
**Verbs** TRACE · RECONCILE · COVER · STAMP  
**Budget** 1900 words / 24 minutes

*The student closes a repair fund's books, traces a claim payout back to the named riders whose shares actually paid it, then signs their own cover level, sets a reserve, and rules on two extended-warranty offers before a repair they cannot predict lands on them.*

Cedar Line is nine riders who deliver for the Vasquez Bakery. Everyone rides their own bike, and
a broken wheel means no shifts and no pay. So the crew runs the Chain Fund: everybody pays a
monthly share, and the fund pays repair bills. Rosa Villanueva is joining. Nobody signs before
they read the book. Tomas kept it by hand for nine months, he has left, and it does not tie. You
are at his desk. Rosa rides. You handle the money.

**Signature mechanic.** **The Claim Jacket.** Left half of the desk: the Member Rail, nine ruled paper strips, one per
rider — `IVY K. · Basic · paid in $54 · claimed $0 · remaining $54`. Right half: one open claim
jacket, a manila card with the bill printed at the top (`WHEEL · Devon R. · $145.00`) and two
labelled rails below it — **DEVON PAYS** and **THE FUND PAYS** — each with a running total
starting at $0.00.
The student first has to work out the split from Devon's cover card and the coverage table:
Basic takes the first $25 from the rider and pays up to $80, so $65 is Devon's and $80 is the
fund's. They type those two figures onto the rails. Then they fill the fund's $80. They focus a
member strip, press Enter (or drag it), and an amount field opens defaulting to that rider's
remaining. Tie $26 from Devon's own strip and Devon's strip drops to $28 remaining and grows a
punched hole reading `— $26 to Devon's wheel`. Tie the rest of Devon and he reads `remaining
$0.00` and the fund rail still says **54 of 80**.
That is the beat the world exists for. Devon's own shares cannot pay for Devon's wheel. The
student has to reach along the rail and take $18 from Ivy, who has claimed nothing in two
seasons, and $16 from Mateo, and $10 from Priya. At $80.00 the jacket stamps CLOSED and prints
one line into the book:
`WHEEL · Devon R. · $145.00 — Devon paid $65.00. The fund paid $80.00, from Devon $28, Ivy $18,
Mateo $24, Priya $10.`
Nothing snaps to a correct answer: any strips that total $80 are right, because money is
fungible — what is being assessed is the split, the sourcing, and what the student then says
about it. Cards carry season totals rather than monthly lines, so a jacket closes in three to
five ties and never becomes data entry. The same mechanic returns once, late: Rosa's own repair,
her own jacket, and this time the strips she reaches for have other people's names on them and
one of them is Ivy's.

**The tradeoff.** Rosa has $620 for the season, $150 of it already spoken for by required gear and $300 of it
wanted for the spring frame service she is riding for. That leaves about $170 that protection
and the goal fight over, and every path is exposed somewhere:
- **Full, $126** — leaves ~$44 of reserve. Covers the pack, covers a fork down to $25. But
nothing under the $25 deductible is ever paid, so a run of tubes and pads comes straight out of
a reserve that is almost gone, and the goal is short.
- **Basic, $54, plus the $46 pack plan** — leaves ~$70 reserve, goal nearly intact, but the $80
cap means a $160 fork costs the rider $80 and the reserve only just holds.
- **None, $0** — leaves ~$170 reserve, goal safe. Covers a $145 wheel out of pocket. Does not
cover a $340 pack, and the one repair that lands is certain.
**The candidate dominant strategy is Full plus decline both plans**, and I will not pretend the
invented numbers already defeat it. The fix is structural and already has precedent in this
codebase: a `balance.ts`-style sweep over every (cover level, reserve, plan A, plan B)
combination across all seeds, with Full's premium and the deductible as the levers, asserting
that no single combination wins under every seed. If it cannot be balanced, the world is wrong,
not the sweep.
**Declining cover is a passing path.** A student who signs None and funds a real $170 reserve is
self-insuring, which is legitimate, and every requirement below scores it that way. Nothing in
this world rewards buying insurance as a virtue.

**Good thinking, bad outcome.** A student signs Basic ($54), writes both derived boxes correctly (fund $80 / rider $80 on the
fork) and flags the battery pack as uncovered, funds a $70 reserve, declines the $12 helmet plan
against a $58 exposure at 1 in 8, and buys the $46 pack plan against a $340 exposure at 1 in 5.
The seed then cracks the helmet and spares the pack. They are $58 down on the decline and $46
down on the buy, and their goal is short.
Every requirement survives, because not one of them reads an outcome. use-insurance.er1 and
plan-for-the-unexpected.er2 read the two boxes. er2 and er3 read the reconciliation and the
jackets, which happened before any of this. is-the-add-on-worth-it.er1 and .er2 read the figures
on the certificates; .er3 reads that the stamps preceded the resolutions; .er4 reads the pack
decision against the pack's own card, and it holds whichever way the helmet went. plan-for-the-
unexpected.er3 reads that the reserve covered the rider's share, which it did. er4 accepts a
named shortfall at level 5. This student can score 5 on all twelve rows and finish the season
$104 worse off, and the closing screen shows them the book rather than a verdict.

**Bad thinking, good outcome.** A student signs None in four seconds without opening any level's terms, leaves the exposure box
blank until a hint forces it and then writes $340 (right at None, for the wrong reason), sets no
reserve, puts everything into the goal, declines both plans by copying the price tags into the
exposure boxes, and the seed spares the helmet and the pack.
They still take the one certain repair — a $145 wheel — and pay it out of the goal line. What is
reported: use-insurance.er1 at 2 (no terms compared), er2 at whatever the reconciliation earned,
er3 at whatever the jackets earned, er4 from writing that will almost certainly argue from luck.
is-the-add-on-worth-it.er1 at 2 (price tag copied). plan-for-the-unexpected.er1 at 0 —
protection was never a line — and .er3 at 2, met only by reaching committed money, and .er2
capped at 3 by the hint.
Three things stop this reading as mastery. There is no composite anywhere to average a 5 into.
The requirements that could have looked good are the ones that read documents rather than
results. And plan-for-the-unexpected.er1 and .er3 are read off the state of the plan at the
moment the repair landed, which no seed can improve.

**Why not a button.** The signature action is tying a named rider's shares into somebody else's repair. A `SETTLE
CLAIM` button would settle it out of a single fund balance, and a balance is exactly the
abstraction that hides the idea: shared risk is invisible while the pool is one number. The
student has to run Devon's own strip to $0.00, watch the fund rail still say **54 of 80**, and
then take $18 off Ivy — who has claimed nothing in two seasons — to close the bill. That is not
an illustration of shared risk; it is the student performing the transfer with their own hands,
and it is what makes Ivy's letter at the end answerable from something they did rather than from
something they were told.
It also produces evidence a button cannot. use-insurance.er3 needs a rider/fund split the
student computed from a cover level and a fund side sourced from real cards. A button produces
one boolean.

**Would clicking be better.** The honest case for the button is strong and I will make it. The trace is bookkeeping. A student
can become fluent at tying strips and still believe the fund is a savings account with extra
steps, and a well-drawn static diagram plus one question — *could Devon's own shares have paid
for Devon's wheel?* — delivers most of the insight in twenty seconds instead of three minutes.
Where a mechanic is slower than a picture and no better, the picture wins.
Two answers. First, that question is multiple choice with decoration, which is a named hard fail
here; it produces an answer, not evidence, and cannot distinguish a student who reasoned from
one who guessed a yes/no. Second and more important: **I am not claiming the trace is the
assessment.** The trace makes the idea available and produces one decision row (er3). The thing
that shows understanding is er4, a paragraph a person reads. If the trace ever has to carry the
concept on its own, this design has failed and the kill condition below fires.
Where a button genuinely is better, this world uses one. SIGN and DECLINE on a certificate are
plain buttons — there is no truthful representation of deciding, and a signature-drawing gesture
would be motor skill wearing a suit. The cover level is a radio group, not a drag, because
choosing a tier is choosing from a list and pretending otherwise is theatre. The only thing
built as a representation is the one thing a control would lie about: where the money came from.

**Accessibility path.** The construct to preserve is *a claim is paid out of finite contributions that have names on
them*. It survives because the tie, not the drag, is canonical.
**Keyboard.** The Member Rail is a real `<table>` — rider, level, paid in, claimed, remaining —
which is not a downgrade of the paper strips, it is what they are. Tab to a row, Enter opens a
number field defaulting to that rider's remaining, Enter again ties it. Drag is a pointer alias
for the identical action and is never the only route. The jacket's two rails are labelled number
inputs; Escape releases; a tie is undone from the jacket's own tie list.
**Screen reader.** The fund rail is a polite live region and it announces the whole mechanic:
*"Tied Ivy K. eighteen dollars. Fund rail 46 of 80, needs 34 more. Ivy remaining 36."* The load-
bearing moment is announced explicitly rather than shown: *"Devon R. remaining zero dollars.
Fund rail 26 of 80."* A screen-reader user meets the impossibility of self-funding in the same
beat and with the same force as a sighted one — that sentence is the design.
**Reduced motion.** The only motion is a strip settling onto a rail, 120ms, purely decorative;
under `prefers-reduced-motion` strips appear in place. No information is carried by movement.
**Muted audio.** Nothing in the world is audio-bearing.
**200% zoom and 640px.** Two stacked regions, ledger then jacket, with the jacket's two running
totals sticky at the top so the target is never scrolled away. The member table collapses to one
labelled block per rider with the same six fields. The certificates are ≤60 words each and the
fine print is set at body size — a world about reading the fine print may not render the fine
print small, and shrinking it would be a realism gag paid for by the reader.
**Colour and density.** A line that does not tie carries the text tag "does not tie" as well as
its colour; nothing is flagged by colour alone. All money is tabular figures. The density risk
this stance carries is real and is managed by the pool being nine riders, by cards holding
season totals rather than monthly lines, and by a jacket closing in three to five ties. If a
jacket ever needs ten, the strip data is at the wrong grain.

**Where it could teach something false.** **Calling a friends' repair fund insurance.** It is mutual insurance and that is defensible — it
is how insurance started — but a student could leave thinking insurance is a thing nine people
do. Guard: the fund's rulebook page names each real term exactly once against its own object
(*your share is a premium; your level is your coverage; the first $25 is your deductible; a bill
you send in is a claim*), and one sentence says a company insuring thousands of people works the
same way at a size no nine riders could reach. That sentence is on the critical path and
counted.
**Teaching that insurance always pays.** The Basic cap and the battery exclusion are the guard,
and they are the honest lesson: coverage has limits and the limits are in the document. The
world must never settle a claim more generously than the table says — which is precisely the
discrepancy the student is sent to find.
**Teaching that insurers hoard.** The fund has to close solvent with a visible carried surplus,
or a student concludes the pool pockets everything. Authored claims must total meaningfully less
than shares collected, and the surplus line must be on the closing sheet with a word about why a
fund holds one.
**Teaching expected value as the rule.** No requirement reads an EV calculation and the world
never computes one. er1 reads exposure, er2 reads stated likelihood, er4 reads consistency. A
student who buys the helmet plan against the odds because $58 would empty a reserve they need is
not wrong and is not marked wrong. A student who buys the pack plan at Full — $46 to protect $25
— is wrong, and er4 is what catches it.
**Shame.** Nobody in this world is poor or careless. Devon hit a pothole. Ivy is not a sucker
and not lucky; she is the person the student has to write to, and her question is the sharpest
one in the module.
**Arithmetic band.** No percentages anywhere: flat deductible, flat cap. Every figure a student
derives is one subtraction or one comparison against a posted list.

**Kill condition.** Instrumented playtest shows students closing Devon's jacket by trial and error against the
running total without opening member cards — operationally, more tie/untie events than tied
cards in over 30% of runs. That would make the signature act arithmetic pachinko, would make er2
and er3 unobservable, and no amount of copy would fix it. Second kill: median time in the
reconciliation beat exceeds nine minutes, which means the inspection stance's density risk has
landed and the world cannot fit a period. Third kill: the screen-reader build cannot make "Devon
remaining $0.00, fund rail 26 of 80" land as the same moment it is for a sighted student — the
concept lives in that announcement, and a worksheet fallback is not an acceptable exit.

**What it does not prove.** It does not touch identity theft (4.4) — `protect-your-information` is deliberately not carried,
because its shape needs a stream of inbound messages and a delayed financial consequence, and
bolting that on would cost roughly 600 words and produce a fifth of a competency, which is
nothing.
It does not prove the student can build a budget within an income: the sign-on sheet is a four-
line protection allocation, not a budget board, and `plan-within-income` is not claimed.
It does not prove any understanding of probability beyond reading a stated frequency off a log.
The world never asks for an expected value and no requirement reads one.
It does not prove the student understands how commercial premiums are set, underwriting, or risk
pricing — the fund's shares are posted, not derived.
And use-insurance.er4 is one person's judgment of one short paragraph written on one day. It is
the strongest row here and the least mechanical, and the product should say so on the teacher's
screen rather than let it sit beside eleven deterministic rows looking like the same kind of
thing.

**Visual identity.** Take the title off and you still know: **this is a book being tied off.** Basketball is a plan
for a season; the market is a stall on four Saturdays; this is a desk covered in paper that
hooks together.
Every object is a strip — narrow, ruled, punched at the left edge, warm paper-white on a
slightly darker desk. One ink, dark slate. Two accents only: a muted fund-green for money coming
in and a rust for money going out, and neither ever carries meaning alone. The claim jacket is a
manila card, taller than the strips, with a printed bill at the top and two rails ruled across
it. When a strip funds a claim, its source card grows a punched hole with a printed note — `—
$18 to Devon's wheel` — and it stays there for the rest of the world; by the closing sheet the
rail is a row of cards with holes in them, which is the season's whole argument in one image.
Type: a ledger serif with tabular figures for every name and amount, a plain sans for interface
chrome, and no size below body for anything a student must read. Motion is one thing only: a
strip settling onto a rail, 120ms, off under reduced motion. There are no charts in this world,
because the member table already is one.
The after-state of the signature interaction — a jacket with four strips hooked to it, four
cards holed, and one printed line naming who paid what — is the screenshot this World would be
recognised by.

### The Repair Ring

**Carries** `use-insurance`, `is-the-add-on-worth-it`, `plan-for-the-unexpected`  
**Verbs** COVER · POOL · RESERVE · SETTLE · SORT  
**Budget** 2150 words / 25 minutes

*The student snaps one line across eight crew members' gear that decides what each person carries alone and what the pool carries together, funds a backstop against a trip the crew wants, then lives ten weeks with whatever breaks and reads the ledger of who paid for whom.*

Your crew films everything — home games, the talent show, the eighth-grade trip. Eight of you,
eight pieces of gear, and gear breaks. Last year Priya's tablet cracked in week two and you
filmed the finals on somebody's phone. So this season you're running a repair ring: everyone
puts in a little each week, and when something breaks, the ring pays for it. You decide how much
the ring covers and how much each person still covers on their own. Then you find out what
breaks.

**Signature mechanic.** THE SNAP LINE. Eight open gear cases stand in a row on a club-room table. Out of each case rises
a vertical bar whose height is that item's replacement cost on one shared dollar axis: Dev's
controller $40, Rosa's headset $60, Kai's tripod $90, Sam's phone $120, Ivy's mic $160, Tomas's
lens $210, Priya's tablet $260, Nia's laptop $360. Under each bar, a gaffer-tape tag: name,
item, a strip of ten squares with N filled ("breaks in 3 of 10 seasons"), "$4 a week", "$50 on
the spot".
The student grabs ONE bright chalk line and drags it horizontally across all eight bars at once.
It snaps in $10 steps and clicks onto meaningful heights (each bar's top). Everything BELOW the
line fills amber — the member carries that alone. Everything ABOVE fills teal — the ring pays
it. One line, eight contracts.
Three counters move as it moves, and they move in opposite directions:
— WEEKLY FROM EACH: recomputed live from what the ring would owe across the crew (a pool price,
shown with its small margin named on screen).
— IN THE RING: eight tape tags. When the weekly passes a member's stated limit their tag peels
off and greys. Fewer members means the same payouts split fewer ways, so the weekly rises again
and another tag can peel. The ring settles at the largest group that can all afford the price it
produces.
— SHORT ON THEIR OWN: members whose amber share is bigger than what they said they could cover
on the spot. Their gear stays broken for the season if it breaks.
Pull the line down: the ring covers almost everything, and half the crew can't afford to be in
it. Push it up: everyone joins for pocket change, and three people are one drop away from
filming on nothing.
Commit, and the line latches with a taut-string settle. It stays on screen for the rest of the
season. When Nia's laptop dies in week 6, the loss is drawn as a notch on Nia's bar and the
payment splits along the line the student drew — amber below, teal above — before any word
appears.

**The tradeoff.** Three counters, one line, no direction that improves all three. Down: the ring covers nearly
everything and members can't afford to be in it (at $50 the ring settles at five members and the
three outside carry their gear whole). Up: everyone joins for a dollar a week and three members
are short on their own share, so their gear stays broken and the crew films the finals on
nothing. The band where a full-mark run lives is roughly $80–$180 and it is wide, because which
line is best depends on which gear is fragile and how much anyone can absorb — judgements the
world states and refuses to rank.
Second trade, coupled to the first: the $300 float splits between the ring's backstop and
$30-a-head entry fees for the invitational. A high line makes the ring's obligations small, so
protection is cheap and everyone goes; a low line makes them large, so protecting the ring costs
the crew seats on the bus. The student who plans well can just about afford both.
Third trade, coupled to both: the counter's plan is only worth buying on an item the ring does
not already cover. A student who set a low line and then buys the shop's plan on the laptop is
paying twice for the same cover — and the world never tells them, it just takes the money out of
the backstop.
Dominant strategy check: none, and it must be held by a test. Add this world to the existing
balance sweep with the assertion that for at least three distinct committed lines there exist
backstop and seat choices producing a defensible full-mark run, and that no line simultaneously
maximises weekly-affordable, seats-in-ring and nobody-short. If the sweep ever finds one, the
member data is wrong, not the rubric.

**Good thinking, bad outcome.** The concrete case: a student commits the line at $100 (ring carries $260 of the $360 laptop, all
eight tags stay in the ring at $2 a week, one member short on their own share), holds $110 as
the backstop and sends six of eight to the invitational. The seeded season then deals the
laptop, the tablet AND the lens — a season in the top decile. The backstop empties in week
seven, the call goes out, two tags peel, and the crew films the finals two cameras down. The
ring, on this run, paid out far more than it took in.
Nothing in the rubric reads that. use-insurance er1 reads the committed line against the crew's
biggest replacement cost; er2 reads a sort of the ledger, which exists whatever the ledger says;
er3 reads two numbers about one claim; er4 is scored on whether the pool is accounted for, and a
rough season gives that paragraph more to work with, not less. plan-for-the-unexpected er1 reads
the backstop against their own line's fourth-largest exposure — a threshold they met before the
season began. er2 reads whether they steered toward the flag the season raised, which a bad
season makes easier to do well. er3 explicitly allows raising next season's opening backstop to
what this one drew.
The world also hands the student the argument: the three-seasons panel replays their own
committed design under seeds+1 and +2, unchanged, and shows a quiet season and a middling one
beside the one they lived. Same line, same backstop, three ledgers. A student who designed well
sees that they designed well, which is the difference between a lesson and a punishment.

**Bad thinking, good outcome.** The concrete case: a student drags the line straight to the top ($360+), notices the weekly go
to almost nothing and every tag stick, commits in nine seconds, spends the entire float on eight
seats to the invitational, declines both counter offers because "everything is expensive", and
the seeded season deals only Dev's $40 controller and Rosa's $60 headset. Nobody is hurt. The
crew has a great season and money left over.
The product reports: use-insurance er1 scores 0 — at that line the ring's share of the biggest
exposure is nothing, which is the misconception, not the outcome. plan-for-the-unexpected er1
scores 0 — nothing held back. er2 likely 2 or 0 at the re-vote, since the flags raised were
about members carrying their own gear. er3 catches the second half of it: a backstop of zero
after a quiet season, unchanged, is one sample read as a rule. is-the-add-on er1 and er2 can
still score if the declines were defensible on the numbers, and they should — declining cover
for good reasons is a real skill and the student gets it. Two of three competencies come back
not demonstrated, and the ledger the student saw was cheerful the whole way.
Two structural guards make this hold. First, no evidence requirement reads the student's own
balance or their own item's fate; the observer must be blind to `youMemberId`, and the build
carries a test that swaps `youMemberId` in a golden log and asserts not one level changes.
Second, the season is drawn at WORLD_OPENED before any decision exists, so a lucky run is a
property of the seed, and the same seed run twice by two students who chose differently produces
different evidence from identical losses.

**Why not a button.** The plain version of this action is three buttons: BASIC / STANDARD / PREMIUM, each with a
price. That version cannot assess the competency it claims, and the reason is specific rather
than aesthetic.
A coverage level is not a tier. It is a place where the split falls, and the same place falls
differently on a $40 controller than on a $360 laptop — for Dev the ring never pays at all, for
Nia it pays most of it. That distribution across the eight members IS the shared-risk construct
the `compare-two-lives` shape demands, and a tier label is the one representation that erases it
completely. Press STANDARD and you learn a price. Snap the line at $100 and you see, in one
image, that three people just bought protection they will never collect on and one person just
got most of a laptop covered — which is exactly what a pool is, and exactly what Kai will
complain about at the end.
Second: on the button version the premium is a fact handed to the student. On the line it is a
consequence of a decision they made, arriving in the same instant, and it moves the wrong way
when they are generous. The peel of a name tag off the ring when the weekly crosses somebody's
limit is not decoration; it is the denominator of shared risk becoming visible, and there is no
tier list in which it can happen.

**Would clicking be better.** The honest case for the button: buttons are instant, keyboard-native, unambiguous, and free of
motor demand. Dragging is slower, fiddly at 200% zoom, and hostile to a student with a trackpad
and a shaky hand. Three tiers with a small table showing what each pays for each member would
carry most of the distribution information at a fraction of the build cost and none of the
accessibility risk. Continuous input also invites false precision — no twelve-year-old means
anything different by $110 and $120, so a 37-position slider is 34 positions of noise.
Two of those three land, and the design concedes them. The line is not a drag control that also
has a keyboard fallback; it is one value with two equal inputs, a slider and a number field,
both always present, both first-class. The $10 snapping exists because the false-precision
objection is right. If a playtest showed dragging added nothing over typing, the drag would go
and the design would survive intact.
What does not concede is the representation. The claim is not "dragging beats clicking" — it is
"one shared threshold rendered across all eight participants beats a tier menu", and that claim
is about what is on screen, not about the input device. Take away the line and put back three
buttons and you have deleted the only place in the run where a student can see that the same
decision lands differently on eight different lives. Take away the drag and keep the line and
you have lost a little pleasure and nothing else. That is the test of whether an interaction is
theatre, and this one passes it by being indifferent to how the number is entered.

**Accessibility path.** The construct is a split and a count. Both are text, so both survive.
KEYBOARD AND SCREEN READER. The line is a single `role="slider"` (min $0, max $370, step $10;
Arrow $10, Shift+Arrow $50, Home/End) bound to a number field that sets the same state — one
control, two inputs, neither a fallback. `aria-valuetext` carries the whole construct: "One
hundred dollars. Each member covers the first $100, the ring covers the rest. Two dollars a
week. Eight of eight in the ring. One member short on their own share." The eight members are a
real table in the DOM at all times, not a description of a picture: each row reads name, item,
replacement cost, "breaks in 3 of 10 seasons", then two live cells — "Nia pays $100, the ring
pays $260" and "in the ring at $2 a week" or "out; her limit is $2". A screen-reader user reads
the same eight splits a sighted user sees, by row instead of by colour. A polite live region
announces on settle only — 400ms after the last change, never per step — and speaks the value
plus any counter that changed, so dragging does not produce chatter.
640px AND 200% ZOOM. The eight bars rotate: eight full-width rows, each a horizontal bar, with
the line becoming one vertical rule crossing all eight at the same x. The rule that may never be
broken is that it stays ONE line across ALL members; per-member sliders would be a different and
much easier financial object, and the design would rather lose the layout than the sharing.
REDUCED MOTION. Fill transitions and tag peels become instant state swaps; the taut-string
settle is removed. No information was ever in the movement — every counter is also a number.
MUTED. No audio carries information; the room tone is ambience.
COLOUR. Member side solid, ring side hatched, and both dollar figures printed in every row, so
the split reads without hue.
THE OTHER TWO CUSTOM INTERACTIONS. The closing sort is a two-group listbox: Tab to a chip, Space
to lift, Arrow between bins, Space to drop — and every chip additionally carries two plain
buttons ("the ring paid them more" / "they paid in more") that do the same thing in one press.
Bin totals are text beside each bin. The counter is a radiogroup of three items plus "none",
each option's accessible name carrying price, strip and plan terms, with the reason chips a
second radiogroup. Nothing on the critical path requires a pointer, a steady hand, or a second
of reaction time.

**Where it could teach something false.** 1. THE PREMIUM IS NOT THE EXPECTED LOSS. The weekly is computed as expected payouts × 1.3 ÷
members ÷ 10. If the 1.3 is silent, the world teaches that a premium equals what the pool
expects to pay, which is false of every insurer on earth. Guard: the margin is a labelled line
on the ring readout — "and a little extra so the ring doesn't run dry" — and it is the hook for
the closing sentence that a company also keeps some for staff and profit, which is why the
counter's $60 plan is dearer per dollar of cover than the student's own ring.
2. FREQUENCY READ AS SCHEDULE. "Breaks in 2 of 10 seasons" invites "so it breaks in season 2".
Guard is structural, not textual: the three-seasons panel replays identical strips producing
different losses, so the correction is something the student watches rather than something the
copy asserts.
3. DEDUCTIBLE, NOT COINSURANCE. The line is a per-claim deductible. The world allows at most one
claim per member per season, stated on the ring screen, so the per-claim/per-year distinction
can never be silently violated.
4. THE CAP AND THE FIRST $25 ON THE PLAN TAG MUST BE OBEYED EXACTLY. If the plan pays up to $400
after the first $25, a $520 laptop leaves $145 on the member. A build that quietly makes the
plan whole teaches "warranties always pay off" while the rubric is busy testing for it. This is
the single highest-risk line of arithmetic in the world and wants its own test.
5. AFFORDABILITY IS NOT ADVERSE SELECTION. Tags peel because of price, not because low-risk
members opt out. The member data must keep weekly limits uncorrelated with failure strips (they
are, by construction) and no copy may imply that the ones who stay are the ones most likely to
claim — that is a real phenomenon and a different, harder concept than this world teaches.
6. A MUTUAL IS NOT AN INSURANCE COMPANY. The ring has no shareholders and no claims department.
Guard: exactly one sentence at the close names the difference, and the counter offer is where a
commercial price is actually felt rather than described.
7. NON-SHAMING GUARDRAILS, held as content rules. Weekly limits and on-the-spot amounts are
stated as facts about an optional crew fund and never as facts about a family. No member's
circumstances are described. Nobody is excluded from the crew — only from the ring — and a
member outside the ring plays the whole season. Losses are ordinary breakage: a dropped
controller, a cracked case, a lens knocked off a table. No fire, no theft, no medical event, no
catastrophe spectacle.

**Kill condition.** Two observations, either of which kills it rather than triggers a revision.
FIRST: in a playtest of twenty grade 5–8 students, 40% or more commit the first line position
they land on AND cannot afterwards say what happened to either of the other two counters. That
is the anti-gimmick question answered against the design in the field — the shared threshold is
not being read as shared, the pool is invisible, and three buttons would be honest and cheaper.
SECOND: er4 level correlates with whether the student's own gear broke at r > 0.3 across seeds
and across `youMemberId` swaps. Insurance is where luck eats assessment, and if a student's
ability to explain the pool turns out to be predicted by their own outcome, the world is
measuring what happened to them. No amount of rubric editing fixes that; the mechanic goes.
A third, softer trigger for redesign rather than death: if the settlement fixed point — the
largest group that can all afford the price it produces — cannot be explained to a twelve-year-
old in one sentence on screen, the peel mechanic becomes magic, and magic in the middle of a
financial construct is a defect. The sentence budgeted for it is "the ring settles at the
biggest group who can all afford it." If that does not land in testing, the affordability limits
become a simple fixed threshold and the world loses its most interesting counter.

**What it does not prove.** It does not prove the student can read a real policy — no exclusions, no fine print, no claim
form, no renewal. It does not prove they understand probability or expected value; the strips
are frequencies read as counts, and no student is ever asked to multiply one by a dollar amount.
It does not prove they would buy insurance with their own money as an adult, which no twenty-
five-minute world can show. It covers property only: nothing here speaks to health, auto,
liability or life insurance, where the sums, the pooling and the moral weight are all different.
It says nothing about NYSED 4.4 — identity theft and protecting personal information is a
different construct with a different mechanic and this world should not pretend to touch it. It
does not prove the student can compute a premium, and it should not: the ring prices, the
student judges. And a `null` on any row here means this world never presented that opportunity
to that student — most often a run that ended at week four on a dead Chromebook — which is a
statement about the run and not about the child.

**Visual identity.** Take the title off and a reviewer knows it in one frame, because no other BOW world is a row of
open cases with a bright line across them.
ENVIRONMENT. A middle-school club room after last bell — cool fluorescent grey-green, a scuffed
folding table, a window going dark. Not a court, not a night market: this is the room where
equipment lives.
OBJECT LANGUAGE. Eight hard cases with the lids open, foam cutouts, a flat icon of each item in
its slot. Every case wears a strip of gaffer tape with a name written in marker; the strips peel
and re-stick as the ring changes, and a peeled tag hangs by one corner rather than vanishing.
The cost bars rise out of the cases like the gear standing up in its foam. The line is a chalk
snap-line — a real crew tool — in chartreuse, taut across all eight.
TYPOGRAPHY. Two voices only. Condensed marker lettering for anything the crew wrote by hand:
name tags, the fundraiser envelope, the plan tag at the counter. Plain product type for every
number, because money is never in the handwriting.
COLOUR. Amber below the line (yours), teal above (the ring's), chartreuse for the line itself,
everything else desaturated room. The three counters are the only other place colour appears,
and they never share a colour with the split.
MOTION. The line settles with a short taut-string wobble on release. Tape peels and curls; it
never snaps away. A loss draws as a notch cut into a bar, then the amber and teal fill up to it
— the split arrives as a fill, never as a bang, and there is no shatter, no siren, no red.
Reduced motion turns all of it into instant state.
THE IMAGE THAT SURVIVES THE RUN. Eight bars, one bright line, two colours — and at the close,
that same picture beside two small bins of name chips: six on one side, two on the other, and
the totals underneath almost equal.

## Module 5 · Saving and Investing

### The Kiln Fund

**Carries** `save-toward-a-goal`, `how-savings-grow`, `compare-rates`  
**Verbs** SPLIT · PARK · RUN FORWARD · DEFEND  
**Budget** 2300 words / 24 minutes

*As the new treasurer of the ceramics club, the student splits one tin of club money between a trip that has a deadline and a kiln that will need replacing in eight years, parks each pot in an account whose rules are riveted to its face, and cranks the year forward one month at a time.*

Ms. Alvarez hands you a tin, a key, and a note. You are the new treasurer of fifth-period
ceramics. Inside the tin: $2,140 in dues and bake-sale money that has sat in a supply closet for
four years, earning nothing. Two things want it. The regional student show is in March, and the
club has to say by January 30 how many of the twenty-two of you are going. And the kiln is
eleven years old. They last about twenty. The last treasurer left one line of advice: "I kept it
all in the tin. The bank stuff seemed like a hassle."

**Signature mechanic.** THE BENCH. A wooden shop bench seen flat-on, in four bands.
TOP — the month rail: a length of shelf angle-iron with ten open slots, September to June. Each
slot holds that month's money as fired clay tokens the club made itself ($5, $20, $100 discs —
irregular, hand-pressed, so they read as objects and not as chips). Dues land every month; the
winter bake sale and spring car wash land as lumps. A brass GOAL TAG hangs on a hook; the
student drags it into a slot to set the deadline, and types the target on its face by turning
the traveler dial (8, 14, 22 students) — the target is entry fee plus per-traveler cost, so it
is a number the student constructed.
MIDDLE — the tins: four actual tins standing in a row, each with an index card riveted to the
front carrying one line of terms. Closet tin (0.00%, anyone with the key can take from it).
Northgate Bank Savings (0.01% a year, $5 a month if the balance is under $300). Riverbend Share
(4.30% a year, paid at the end of each month, money out in two days). Riverbend 6-Month
Certificate (4.75% through October, then 3.90%; money locked six months; break it early and you
lose 90 days of interest). The student picks up a token or a stack and drops it into a tin. The
tin's front fills with a two-tone bar: buff clay for what the club put in, cobalt glaze for what
the tin added.
LEFT EDGE — the floor rail: a horizontal bar the student drags up and down across all ten month-
slots at once. Its height is the amount that must go into the goal tin every month before
anything else moves; its end cap prints the figure. Months whose tokens fall below the line go
hatched. Crucially the shortfall beam under the tag DOES NOT MOVE WHILE DRAGGING — it only
redraws when the crank is pulled. You cannot wiggle your way to the answer.
RIGHT EDGE — the crank: a brass handle. One pull advances one month. Tokens drop, the floor rail
takes its bite, and each tin ratchets one line of adding-machine paper out of its base: `OCT ·
in $195 · interest $8.40 · bal $2,343.40`. The certificate's tape prints `locked until APR` on
every line until it isn't, so the collision with a January deadline is visible four months
before it bites.
WHAT THE SCREEN LOOKS LIKE AFTER: ten slots empty, four curls of tape on the bench floor, the
goal tin's bar mostly buff with a thin cobalt band, the certificate's bar thicker and still
sealed, and the tag either hanging on its slot or re-pinned lower with the old position ghosted.
THE HINGE: once the January invoice is paid, the rail does not clear — it EXTENDS. Month-slots
compress into eight year-slots and the kiln comes onto the bench. The student places a start peg
(this year, or year three, the honest thing most clubs do), pulls the long crank, then places a
second peg and pulls again. Two tapes, side by side, cobalt bands of visibly different
thickness.

**The tradeoff.** One tin, two horizons. Every dollar moved to the kiln pot is a student who does not travel to
the show; every dollar that travels is a kiln the next generation buys later, smaller, or with a
bake sale that isn't theirs.
The genuinely non-dominated decisions, which the balance sweep must prove: (1) how many travel —
8, 14 or 22, where each must win under some priority weighting ("everyone goes", "the fund
survives the year", "start the kiln now"); (2) the kiln start peg — now versus year three, where
starting now is better arithmetic and worse for the twenty-two people currently in the room, and
BOW scores neither choice; (3) the December bus response — cut travelers, take from the kiln
pot, or run an extra fundraiser, all legitimate.
THE DOMINANT STRATEGY I AM DECLARING AND KEEPING: the tin placement has a right answer — liquid
money in the share account, kiln money in the certificate, and nothing in the closet or behind
the $5 fee. That is correct and intentional. `compare-rates` is a comparison competency; asking
it to be a dilemma would be asking it to stop being itself. The non-dominance burden sits
entirely on the three decisions above, and the world must never score which of those three the
student picked.
THE ONE DOMINANT STRATEGY THAT WOULD KILL THE DESIGN, AND THE FIX: if starting the kiln fund
early were free, the peg would be a no-brainer and objective 5.5 would become a button. So the
kiln pot is carved out of the SAME $2,140 in September, before the trip is funded, and it
visibly moves the floor rail up. Starting early has to cost this year's members something, or
the world has taught that compounding is free money.

**Good thinking, bad outcome.** A student sends fourteen, sets the floor rail at $195 (correct for the tag they built), splits
$1,400 to this year in the Riverbend share and $740 to the kiln in the certificate, and starts
the kiln peg now. Sound on every count. Then the seeded car wash comes in at the bottom of its
band — $180 against the $240 they reasonably expected — and the $380 bus claim lands. On January
30 they are $60 short. They cut one traveler, re-pin the tag, and state the gap on the invoice
slip.
Every row survives, and it survives by construction rather than by mercy. er2 is scored against
R computed from the information available AT THE MOMENT THE RAIL WAS SET — the expected
fundraiser midpoint, not the realized draw — so a bad draw cannot retroactively make a correct
division wrong. er3 explicitly credits re-setting the target as much as protecting it, which is
the whole reason the shipped rule has two limbs. er4's own text says 'or states how far short
and why', and my invoice slip makes stating it a first-class action rather than a consolation.
er1 was already banked in September. And all seven how-savings-grow and compare-rates rows are
read off the long rail, which runs on the plan they built and is untouched by the draw.
Stated as a rule the observer enforces: no rubric level in this world is a function of a seeded
value. Randomness changes how much slack the student has; it never changes what a decision is
compared against.

**Bad thinking, good outcome.** A student picks 4.75% because it is the biggest number on the bench and puts the whole $2,140 in
the certificate. They set the floor rail by eye at $250 with no arithmetic behind it. Both
fundraisers land at the top of their bands. In January the certificate is locked, so they break
it, forfeit $26 of interest — and still clear the invoice, because the fundraisers over-
delivered. The tape says GOAL MET.
Nothing reports mastery, because nothing in this world scores the outcome. compare-rates er1 is
scored on the placement: this-year money behind a lock that outlasts the deadline is a 2 whether
or not the total was enough, and breaking it late cannot be a 4 because the correction came
after the consequence, not before it. er2 is scored on the resting tins. save-toward-a-goal er2
is scored on the first rail placement against R, which $250 by eye does not match. how-savings-
grow er2 catches them again, because a projection made in November by someone who never worked
out the arithmetic and never accounted for the forfeit will not be compounded-true. And there is
no composite anywhere: GOAL MET is a fact printed on the tape, and the twelve rows are twelve
separate levels beside it. The teacher's screen shows a student who got there and a student
whose decisions were not the reason.
The inverse trap is closed too: a student who does everything right and is short because of the
draw does not lose a single level, per the row above.

**Why not a button.** The signature action is putting money into a place. A button labelled DEPOSIT TO RIVERBEND hides
the three things that are the entire financial content of that act: how much, out of which pot,
and how long it will have to sit there. It makes the fund look infinite (a button can be pressed
again) and it makes the conditions look like fine print behind a modal.
The bench makes the fund finite because it is a countable number of tokens that leave one place
in order to be in another, and it makes the conditions unavoidable because they are riveted to
the face your hand has to pass to get the token in. A student who drops the trip money into the
certificate has physically put it behind a lid with 'locked six months' on it. That is not
decoration; it is the same information a modal would have shown, positioned where the decision
is made rather than where the decision is confirmed.
The floor rail is the stronger case. A field asking 'how much per month?' is a form field: it
takes a number and tells you nothing. The rail draws one line through all ten month-slots at
once, so the instant it is placed the student can see that November's tokens fall below it — the
amount and its feasibility are one object, and the hatching on the short months is a fact no
numeric field can express without a second widget. And freezing the shortfall beam until the
crank is pulled is what stops the rail from being a solver: you commit, then you find out, which
is also how a month works.

**Would clicking be better.** Argued straight: most of this world should be buttons, and in my design it is. The crank is a
button labelled 'Run October'. The December bus response is a list of named options. The invoice
slip is a figure field and a cause list. The traveler dial is a three-position control. I am not
building representation where a control would carry the same meaning — the crank does not need
to be a rotating handle you drag, and if it were, that would be motor skill masquerading as
finance.
The hardest case against me is the fund tokens. Two number fields — 'this year: $____ , kiln:
$____' — would set the split faster, with far lower motor cost, and on a Chromebook trackpad
dragging is genuinely worse. I concede that the split alone does not need a gesture.
The answer is that the split is not what the gesture does. The token has to LAND somewhere, and
the landing is the account choice — the thing objective 5.3 is entirely about. Two number fields
separate 'how much' from 'where', and it is precisely their combination that is the answer being
assessed. A student who types $740 into a kiln field has said nothing about horizon; a student
who carries $740 past the certificate's lid has.
Where I hold the line honestly: every tin also accepts a typed amount, available to every
student and not only to assistive-technology users, and that path is not treated as inferior.
The gesture is the default because containment is the construct, not because it is the only
door. If pilot data shows sighted mouse students overwhelmingly prefer the numeric entry, that
is a fine result — the construct is identical either way, and I would keep the drag for the same
reason a real bench keeps a drawer you can see into.

**Accessibility path.** The construct here is CONTAINMENT AND ORDER — which container, which slot — never position on a
canvas. That is the whole reason this workbench survives where a free-placement one would not:
nothing the student does is measured in pixels, so nothing is lost when the layout reflows.
Keyboard: the bench is a region grid. Tab moves rail / tins / rail-control / crank / tape;
arrows move within. Tokens use the standard pick-up-move-drop pattern — Space picks up, arrows
step across tins, Space drops, Escape cancels — announcing 'Picked up $400 of this year's money.
Four places: closet tin; Northgate savings, five dollars a month under three hundred; Riverbend
share, four point three percent; Riverbend certificate, locked six months.' The conditions are
read at the moment of choosing, which is where the rivet plate is visually.
The floor rail and the two-tone divider are the same primitive: role=slider valued in dollars,
arrow $5, PageUp $25, Home/End the extremes, with aria-valuetext carrying the thing the visual
carries — '$195 a month. November brings in $88.' The slider's value IS the bar's height; this
is the same object in a different modality, not a substitute for it.
Screen reader: the tape is a real table with month / in / interest / balance columns, and it is
ALWAYS ON SCREEN beside the bar, for everyone. The two-tone bar and the tape are the same two
accumulators rendered twice. So the interest/principal separation — the one thing a colour-coded
bar would normally own — is fully present in text for every student, and no information exists
in colour alone. Each crank announces the line just printed.
640px and 200% zoom: single column. The rail becomes a vertical list of months, tins stack, each
tape sits under its tin. Containment and order are unchanged, so no measurement moves. The long
rail's eight year-slots become eight list rows.
Reduced motion: tape lines appear rather than ratchet; bars snap. Muted audio: the crank's click
is decorative and every event prints a line. Colour: buff and cobalt are 4.6:1 against each
other and each carries a text label on the bar's ends.
No separate worksheet exists, and none is needed — the tape IS the worksheet, and the sighted
student is reading it too.

**Where it could teach something false.** 1. RATES DRIFT. 0.01% at a big bank and 4.30% at a credit union are true for 2025-26 and are
what give objective 5.3 its teeth. They live in one dated content file, the plates print 'rates
checked [month]', and the arithmetic is rate-agnostic. Guard: a test asserts the spread between
the best and worst plate exceeds 200 basis points; if it ever fails, compare-rates is no longer
honestly assessable here and the competency comes out rather than the numbers being inflated.
2. THE EARLIER-START OVER-CLAIM — the biggest risk in the design. Over eight years at 4.75%,
most of the advantage of starting now over starting in year three is MORE DEPOSITS, not
compounding. A world that lets a student walk away saying 'compound interest did that' has
taught something subtly false about the one idea this module exists for. how-savings-grow er4 is
the guard and it is required, not optional: the student must split the gap into deposits and
interest and the two must reconcile.
3. COMPOUNDING MUST BE REAL. Monthly, on the closing balance, credited on the last day, integer
cents, no daily fudge, no annual shortcut. A table-driven test against hand-computed values for
all four tins across the full rail is the accuracy gate and must be written before the UI.
4. THE EARLY-WITHDRAWAL PENALTY. 'Lose 90 days of interest' is the real convention. It must be
computed as 90 days of interest on the withdrawn amount and CAPPED AT THE INTEREST THAT
CERTIFICATE HAS EARNED. Implemented as a principal haircut it would teach that a savings account
can lose your money, which is false and is the worst thing this world could say.
5. THE FEE. Northgate's $5 under $300 is real and it is the point — the tape must print the
balance going down. Guard: the club's balances are usually above $300, so the fee only bites a
student who splits the fund small, which is a true lesson rather than a gotcha.
6. THE WORD 'INVEST' APPEARS NOWHERE. This world carries 5.1, 5.2, 5.3 and 5.5. It does not
carry 5.4 or 5.6, it contains no asset with a range of outcomes, and a certificate is not an
investment vehicle. Every screen says saving. A copy test greps for the word. A teacher reading
'invest' on a screen would read coverage this world has not earned.
7. THE MONEY IS THE CLUB'S, NOT THE STUDENT'S FAMILY'S. Nothing here simulates a child's
household shortfall, and the December pressure is a school bus, not rent.

**Kill condition.** Fewer than 60% of a pilot class place the floor rail before the first crank pull. That would
mean the plan/run separation — the thing that makes er2 a computation rather than a search — has
failed at the interaction level, and every level 5 this world reports on that row would be a
level 5 for persistence. I would not patch it with a lockout; I would take the shortfall beam
off the board entirely and rebuild the run around the tape alone, or abandon the design.
Second kill: if students in exit interviews consistently dismiss the interest as trivial despite
the rates being real, then compare-rates and how-savings-grow are not honestly assessable at a
horizon a twelve-year-old cares about, and I would cut both rather than inflate the numbers —
leaving save-toward-a-goal alone, which still lights objective 5.1 on five requirements that are
already shipped.

**What it does not prove.** It proves nothing about risk, volatility or asset classes. Objectives 5.4 and 5.6 stay dark and
this world must never be read as touching them: there is no asset here with a range of outcomes,
nothing falls, and a student who does everything right has not been shown to understand why
spreading money helps. Bolting weigh-investment-risk onto this bench would have needed a
different information structure — stated ranges resolving against the student — and would have
blown both the reading budget and the range-versus-average honesty that competency exists to
protect.
It proves nothing about the student's own money. This is a club treasury, deliberately: it is
the honest vehicle at this age and it is the role many of these students already hold. But
planning well with other people's money is not evidence of saving for yourself, and no report
should imply it is.
It proves nothing about long-horizon compounding as adults meet it. Eight years is a real
replacement cycle, not a working life, and how-savings-grow er4 exists specifically to stop the
world implying otherwise.
It proves nothing about choosing a real bank. Northgate and Riverbend are invented institutions
with real-shaped terms.
And it says nothing about a student whose Chromebook died at the December slot: those rows are
null, and null is not zero.

**Visual identity.** A wooden shop bench under a dusty window in a ceramics room, seen flat-on from above. Take the
title away and you would still know: the two existing worlds are a gym and a night market, and
this is the only one made of clay dust and adding-machine paper.
OBJECT LANGUAGE: everything on the bench is a thing a ceramics club would actually own. Money is
fired clay tokens the club pressed itself — irregular, thumbprinted, slightly off-round, in
unfired buff. Accounts are four battered tins with index cards riveted to the front, corners
curling. The month rail is a length of galvanised shelf angle-iron with ten open slots. The
deadline is a brass tag on a hook. The crank is a brass handle bolted to the right edge. Tape is
real adding-machine paper that curls off the bottom of each tin and pools on the bench. Nothing
is a card, a panel or a modal.
PALETTE: unfired clay buff (principal), cobalt glaze blue (interest), kiln-brick orange (the
goal tag and the deadline), slip white (the bench light), a single oxide red used for exactly
one thing in the whole world — the early-withdrawal forfeit line.
TYPOGRAPHY: the handover note in a hand-lettered marker face, slightly crooked, because a real
one is. Rivet plates in a small condensed sans, like a label maker. Every number, everywhere, in
a plain tabular monospace with the columns aligned — the adding machine's voice, and the reason
the tape looks like evidence rather than a chart.
MOTION: discrete, never eased. One crank pull ratchets exactly one tape line out and drops it
into place; the two-tone bar steps up in one increment; tokens fall into a slot and stop. No
particles, no counting-up numbers, no celebratory anything. The bench is quiet and the paper
does the talking.
THE IMAGE A REVIEWER REMEMBERS: at the end, ten empty slots, four curls of tape on the floor of
the bench, and two long tapes side by side with cobalt bands of visibly different thickness —
the eight-year run that started now, and the one that waited.

### The Treasurer's Drawer

**Carries** `save-toward-a-goal`, `how-savings-grow`, `compare-rates`  
**Verbs** NORMALISE · ATTACH · POST · RECONCILE · PROTECT  
**Budget** 2000 words / 26 minutes

*As the new treasurer of a school club, the student restates three savings offers in one common unit, attaches each offer's conditions to the periods they actually govern, runs the club's two pots of money forward on a passbook rail, and reconciles their own projection against the bank's statement when the two disagree.*

You're the new treasurer of the Sound Crew — the twelve people who run mics and lights for every
show at Ridge Middle. Last year's treasurer left you a drawer. In it: a tin with $150, a
district gift of $1,500 that has sat in that tin since 2019, and three leaflets from three
banks. Channel 2's mic pack died mid-song last Friday. A new one is $420. The spring show is
June 1. Your job is deciding where this money sits.

**Signature mechanic.** Left third of the screen: three leaflets, each a card with the bank's headline set in typewriter
type and four CLAUSE CHIPS printed underneath it — a wide bar for the rate, a bracket for a time
window, a low horizontal rule for a minimum balance, a small tooth for a fee, a circle for a
one-time bonus. Right two-thirds: THE PASSBOOK RAIL, a green-ruled ledger with one row per
period and columns "Put in (principal) / Added (interest) / Held / In force". The Held column is
drawn as a stacked bar in each row: solid fill for club money, hatched fill for bank money. A
dashed target line runs down the Held column at $420.
The student drags a chip off a leaflet and drops it on the rail. It lands as a BAND across the
periods it covers — and the student sets the span; the chip does not know where it belongs. The
moment a chip lands, the "In force" cells of those rows gain its name and the Added figures
redraw: a rate band grows hatched caps, a fee tooth cuts a notch out of every row it covers, a
minimum-balance rule draws a line across the Held column and any row whose bar sits below that
line loses its hatched cap entirely — you can see, as bare rows, the months that earn nothing.
Drag the band's edge and the caps appear or vanish at the new boundary.
Nothing projects until the student POSTS THE FIRST PERIOD BY HAND. Period 1's Added cell is an
empty box with the account's own words printed beside it ("$4 a year for every $100 you keep in
— you're holding $1,500"). The student types it. Only then does the rail run the remaining rows
forward, one at a time, using the rule the student just demonstrated and the chips the student
attached. On the fund rail the student posts period 2 by hand as well, on the balance period 1
ended with.
After: a filled ledger with a hatched wedge widening down the right-hand side, the target line
crossed or not crossed, and every row naming the clauses in force on it. In January an envelope
slides in and the bank's statement opens as a SECOND LEDGER beside the first. Every row where
the two disagree is struck with a red pencil line and a difference figure. The student fixes the
rail, or fixes the account, or lives with it — and the closing slip asks them to split the
ending balance into what the club put in and what the account added.

**The tradeoff.** Two pots, three accounts, and the conditions cut in opposite directions — the same account is
the best home for one pot and the worst for the other.
Goal pot (nine months, ends at ~$420, needs to be spendable on June 1): Pillar finishes at $433
— the $10 opening bonus is worth more over nine months than any rate on the page, and its $2 fee
is waived in every month a deposit lands. Harbor finishes at $426. Grove finishes at $420 flat,
because this pot never reaches its $500 floor, and the money cannot be withdrawn on June 1
unless notice was filed by April 1.
Fund pot (twelve years, $1,500, untouched): Grove reaches the $2,300 soundboard in year 11;
Harbor in year 15 — four years later. Pillar loses money: no deposits ever land, so the $2 fee
is charged every month, and the "best rate" is 0.5% after the first three months.
So the strong play is Pillar for the goal and Grove for the fund, and there is no way to see
that without converting a one-time bonus, a monthly fee and three differently-quoted rates into
one unit. Two instincts both fail: "take the biggest headline" puts the fund in the account that
shrinks it, and "avoid the flashy bank" hands the goal pot to the account paying zero.
IS THERE A DOMINANT STRATEGY? Yes, and it is deliberate: the terminal answer is unique and
knowable. This world is not a gamble — the difficulty is entirely in the reading, and a student
who reads well should be able to be right. What stops it becoming a single memorised answer
shared across a class is a seeded variant set: in B the floor is $2,000 and there is no bonus,
so Harbor wins the fund; in C the fee has no waiver, so Pillar loses both. All three variants
are swept by a balance harness that fails the build if any one account wins both pots.

**Good thinking, bad outcome.** A student stamps all three offers correctly, prices Pillar's $10 bonus at $4.60 per $100 per
year and Grove's floor at $0.00 on the goal pot, puts the goal money in Pillar and the fund in
Grove, and works out $30 a month on the goal card cold. In January the drama teacher needs $95.
They decline to sell the spare pack — the crew would have no backup for a live show, which is a
defensible treasurer's call — and instead file an explicit reset: the target drops to the $340
refurbished unit and the date holds. Two months later the refurbished unit is out of stock and
the crew borrows a pack for the spring show. The world resolved against them.
The evidence is untouched, because NO EVIDENCE REQUIREMENT IN THIS WORLD READS THE OUTCOME. er1
reads the goal card; er2 reads a figure typed before the rail existed; er3 reads the reset
event, and an explicit reset is the level-5 branch of the shipped rule, not the consolation one;
er4 is satisfied by stating the shortfall and a cause that is in their own statement. All four
compare-rates rows read the comparison card and the bindings. All four how-savings-grow rows are
on the fund rail, which the January claim never touches. The teacher's page shows a student who
reached every judgement well and did not get the pack, and says exactly that.

**Bad thinking, good outcome.** A student reads nothing, takes Pillar for both pots because "5.0% — our best rate!", drags every
chip across every row without looking at a single window, and saves the full $30 because that is
all there is. The goal pot lands at $433 on June 1. The pack is bought. The outcome is good.
What stops the product reporting mastery, item by item: compare-rates er1 and er2 read the
comparison card, which is blank or wrong — 0 or 2, and the outcome cannot reach them. er3
requires two fund projections and a year the $2,300 arrives; on Pillar the fund never reaches
$2,300 at all, and a student who never ran the second rail has no figures to type. er4 caught
them putting both pots in one account. how-savings-grow er2 catches the flat second posting —
the fixed-bonus signature — and er3 catches a split that credits the whole balance to what was
put in. The reconciliation trail shows their rail and the bank's statement disagreeing from
November onward, never repaired. The one row luck can carry is save-toward-a-goal er2, where $30
is both the correct division and the club's ceiling — which is exactly why the goal card asks
for the per-month figure on a screen where the ceiling has not yet appeared, so $30 can only
have come from $270 over nine.
And there is nothing to launder a lucky run through: no composite score exists on any surface,
so thirteen rows are reported as thirteen rows, and eleven of them say what happened.

**Why not a button.** The signature action is attaching a clause to the periods it governs, and the whole financial
content of a clause is its SCOPE. "5.0% a year" and "5.0% a year for the first three months" are
the same sentence and completely different money. A checkbox labelled APPLY INTRO RATE erases
precisely the part that matters, and it erases it in the direction the bank would prefer: the
student ticks the 5%, sees a bigger number, and never confronts the edge where it stops.
Dragging the band across rows 1 to 3 and watching the hatched caps vanish at row 4 is fine print
rendered as duration, and it is the same act a person performs when they read a disclosure
properly.
Second, and more important for assessment: a checkbox has no wrong placement. A student who
never noticed the window and a student who read it carefully produce identical checkbox states,
so the instrument cannot tell them apart. A span can be wrong, which is what makes the January
reconciliation a real event rather than a recap — and the belief the student encoded in the rail
is a thing the bank's statement can contradict.
Third, the minimum-balance rule and the fee tooth have no button-shaped truth at all. A floor is
a line the balance is either above or below, month by month; drawn on the rail, it makes the
bare months visible as bare months. As a checkbox it is a sentence nobody rereads.

**Would clicking be better.** The honest case for buttons is strong and I concede most of it. Drag is slow and fiddly on a
school trackpad; every second placing paper is a second not spent thinking about money.
Placement can be wrong for motor reasons rather than financial ones, and motor skill in the
evidence is a hard fail. A SELECT ACCOUNT dropdown plus a projected table would produce all the
same numbers in a third of the time, and twelve chips across three leaflets is a lot of handling
for one decision.
What I concede in the build rather than in argument: placement is never scored as placement. Not
one of the thirteen rows reads "the chip is in the right cells." Every row reads a typed figure
or a committed binding. The chip's only job is to determine what the rail projects, so a
misplacement surfaces as a projection the bank contradicts, and the mark comes from the
reconciliation — from noticing and repairing — not from the motor act. I also concede that only
the chosen account's chips need attaching; the other two leaflets stay on the desk, so the real
handling is three or four chips per pot, not twelve. And the keyboard path is three keystrokes
(Enter, arrow, Enter), which is faster than dragging, so the representation is not a motor tax
on anyone who does not want it.
What I would not trade for a button is the span and the floor. If those become checkboxes, this
world becomes a rate-comparison worksheet with a nice ledger attached — and the mechanics lab
has already scored that kind of thing at 67 and told us why the trace investigator beat it: the
annotation has to be able to be wrong.

**Accessibility path.** The construct is a LEDGER, not a chart, and that is what makes this survivable rather than a
promise.
DOM. The rail is a real `<table>` with a caption: rows are periods, columns are Put in
(principal) / Added (interest) / Held / In force. The bars are CSS backgrounds sized from the
same numbers printed in the cells, so at 200% zoom and at 640px the bars collapse to nothing and
the table remains. There is no worksheet fallback because the table is the primary artifact and
the bars are its styling.
KEYBOARD. Each chip is a button in a list. Enter picks it up — "Grove: earns nothing on a
balance under $500. Picked up. Choose the periods it covers." The rail then takes roving focus:
arrow to the first period, Enter anchors ("anchored at September"), arrow to the last, Enter
attaches ("attached September to June, ten periods"), Escape drops it. That is the standard
range-selection idiom, three keys, and it is faster than the mouse path.
SCREEN READER. Every chip prints its own current scope as text on its leaflet ("in force:
Sep–Nov"), so nobody has to interrogate the rail to learn what they attached. Each row reads as
one sentence: "March. Put in $30. Added $0.71. Held $312. In force: Harbor, 25 cents a month per
$100." The rail's run-forward posts one polite live-region line per period rather than a sweep.
THE STATEMENT. The January disagreement is a second table with its own Difference column and a
per-row text status — "Rail said $2.10, bank said $0.00, short $2.10, reason: balance under
$500" — so the red pencil line is a second channel, never the only one.
COLOUR, MOTION, AUDIO. Put in and Added are separate columns with separate headers and separate
fill patterns; colour never carries the distinction alone. Reduced motion turns the paper-drop
and the pencil stroke into instant state changes. Nothing in the world is carried by sound.
Focus order is document order: leaflets, comparison card, rail, closing slip.

**Where it could teach something false.** 1. THE "HIGHER RATE GETS YOU THERE SOONER" LIE AT SHORT HORIZONS. On $270 over nine months the
rate gap between the three accounts is worth about $6, while a $10 bonus is worth $10 and nine
$2 fees are worth $18. A world implying the rate is the big lever on a one-year goal would teach
something false. Guard: the normaliser stamps conditions in the same unit as rates, so the
student's own card shows the conditions outnumbering the rate gap; the sooner-arrives
demonstration for 5.3 is placed on the twelve-year fund, where four percent genuinely reaches
$2,300 four years before three percent does; and the ending names the true cause of a late
purchase ("notice was never filed", "nine $2 fees, $18") rather than "you picked the low rate".
2. QUOTING VERSUS CREDITING. Guard: no screen says APY. Harbor's terms are stated exactly as the
rail applies them — 25 cents a month per $100, credited monthly on the month's opening balance.
Grove's are stated as $4 a year per $100 added once a year to the new balance. The engine and
the words are the same rule, and the student's posted figures are checked against the words they
were given.
3. MINIMUMS DO NOT ALL WORK LIKE GROVE'S. Real accounts more often tier (you earn a base rate
below the threshold) than pay zero. Grove's terms say plainly what Grove does; the closing card
names the general rule as "conditions differ — read what this one says", never "a minimum means
zero".
4. BONUS-CHASING. The design has Pillar's $10 bonus winning the goal pot, and a student could
leave believing bonuses always win. Guard: the same $10 is worth nothing across twelve years on
the fund, the closing card says so in one line, and the variant sets include a run with no bonus
at all.
5. NOT MODELLED, AND SAID SO ON THE TEACHER'S CARD RATHER THAN IMPLIED TO BE ZERO ON THE
STUDENT'S SCREEN: inflation, tax on interest, rate changes, and any risk of loss. The word
"investing" is never used of these accounts; they are savings accounts, and 5.4 and 5.6 are not
claimed.
6. "DEFINE" IN 5.2. The objective's verb is define and differentiate, and asking a child to type
a definition would be vocabulary labelling dressed as assessment. Guard: the two rail columns
are headed "Put in (principal)" and "Added (interest)" — the words appear once, beside the
things they name, and the student is asked to produce the two AMOUNTS, never the two words.

**Kill condition.** Two observations would end it. First: in playtest the median student takes more than about four
minutes to place chips and post period 1 — the paper handling has eaten the thinking, and the
reading budget was spent on furniture. Second: compare-rates er2 comes back more than 40% blank
even with the hint taken, which would mean pricing a condition in the rate's unit is an analyst
move grade 5–8 cannot reach in this form. Either way I would not patch it. I would cut the fund
pot entirely and rebuild as a single-pot, two-account desk carrying `save-toward-a-goal` alone —
five shipped rows, 5.1 lit, no new rubric authored — and let `how-savings-grow` and `compare-
rates` go to a sibling world with room for them.

**What it does not prove.** Nothing about risk, assets, markets or diversification: 5.4 and 5.6 are untouched, deliberately,
because they need outcomes that resolve against a range and that is a different shape and a
different world. Nothing about whether this student would save their OWN money — the money here
is the club's, which is what makes the scenario honest for thirteen-year-olds and also what
limits the claim. Nothing about compounding beyond a fixed stated rate: no volatility, no
inflation, no tax, no rate changes. And nothing about choosing an account in the real world,
where there are forty of them, the disclosure is eight pages, and nobody hands you a stamp with
the right unit already on it.

**Visual identity.** Take the title off and you still know which world this is inside two seconds: it is the only one
made of PAPER ON A DESK. The other two are outdoors and in motion — a court and a night market.
This one is a school office in the afternoon: manila, photocopy grey, green ledger rule, a warm
desk lamp pool that dims toward the drawer.
Every financial object is a document with a printed edge. Leaflets have perforated chip tags you
tear off along a dotted line. The rail is a passbook page with green rules and a red margin
line. The statement arrives in a window envelope that slides in from the right. The normaliser
is a rubber stamp that leaves a slightly-off-register ink impression, and the impression is
imperfect in the same way twice — it is a stamp, not a badge.
TYPOGRAPHY DOES ASSESSMENT WORK. Anything the bank printed is set in a typewriter face; anything
the student wrote is a soft humanist sans in pencil grey. At a glance, on any screen, you can
see which numbers came from an institution and which came from you — which is the entire skill
this world teaches, encoded in the type.
COLOUR: manila, ledger green, pencil grey, one red pencil. Club money is a solid ink fill; bank
money is a hatched fill. Two fills, not two hues, so the distinction survives greyscale and
colour blindness.
MOTION: chips settle with a two-frame paper drop, no bounce. The rail fills row by row like a
pen moving down a page. The single dramatic moment in the world is one red pencil stroke drawn
along a disagreeing row in 200ms — and under reduced motion it is simply already there.

### Where the Money Waits

**Carries** `save-toward-a-goal`, `how-savings-grow`, `compare-rates`  
**Verbs** PLACE · PROJECT · PROTECT  
**Budget** 2050 words / 24 minutes

*As the new treasurer of a school bike-repair club, the student places every dollar the club holds onto a ten-month timeline — which shelf it waits on and which month it starts waiting — then defends the Spring Ride plan against a bill that lands in February.*

You are the new treasurer of Wheelhouse, the bike-repair club at Ridge Middle. Two piles of
money sit in the shop tin. One is the club's: $610, plus dues at every month's first meeting,
plus whatever the fall bike sale brings. The other is the Ramirez Fund — $4,200 raised for new
loan bikes, which the club voted not to touch for three years. Your job is to decide where every
dollar waits: the tin, which pays nothing; savings; or a certificate that pays more and locks
the money. And to get the club to the Spring Ride.

**Signature mechanic.** The Waiting Board. One screen: a month axis running Sept → June across the top, with a
compressed multi-year tail on the right where the fleet target sits. Under the axis are four
horizontal lanes, drawn as labelled shop shelves: ARRIVALS (where money appears), THE TIN (0%),
SAVINGS (3.0% a year, paid monthly, no conditions), CERTIFICATE (4.6% a year, $500 minimum,
locked 12 months, breaking it forfeits 90 days of interest). Money exists as blocks sitting in
ARRIVALS at the month they arrive: FUND $4,200 (Sept), CLUB $610 (Sept), DUES $176 (×6,
Oct–Mar), BIKE SALE $420 (Oct). The student grabs a block and drops it into a lane at a month
column; it snaps to the cell and cannot snap left of its arrival month ("that money isn't yours
yet"). On drop the block redraws as a bar running rightward from its placement month, solid
amber for principal with a hatched mint band accruing on top of it, month by month, with the
running interest figure printed at the bar's right end — so two identical blocks placed in
different months sit side by side with visibly different mint bands. Lane conditions render as
geometry, not dialogs: a certificate placement draws a grey fence twelve months to its right,
and if that fence crosses the goal flag, the flag goes hollow — you can see your own ride money
on the wrong side of a lock before you commit. The goal flag is itself a placed object: the
student drags it to a month column and sets its height by choosing a cell from the ride price
sheet (Ride $980 due Mar 6, or $882 paid by Jan 9; Ride + Mechanic's Clinic $1,480, or $1,332
paid by Jan 9). Once the flag is set and the student has typed the per-collection figure, a
reserved band is drawn across the coming dues blocks — the ride's share of each collection,
fenced. Bills must be dragged onto a source: to pay February's $210 parts bill the student drags
it onto a pot, and dragging it onto the reserved band physically shortens the band, unfences the
flag and reopens the per-collection question. Time moves only one way: a NEXT MEETING control
advances one month, interest posts as real cents into a permanent posting log, costs come out,
and nothing can be un-advanced. There is no scrubber and no animation to watch — the only thing
that moves under the student's hand is money, and it moves in time. One rule governs the whole
board: it never shows a number the student has not been asked for first. Placing into a rate-
bearing lane opens a placement panel showing all three lane cards and asks two typed figures —
what this money adds in the lane you chose, and in one other lane you tap — before it accepts
the block. After the last month the board freezes into the handover: the posting log, the fund's
ending balance, the fleet arrival flag, and two lines written to the next treasurer.

**The tradeoff.** Reach versus return, and it is priced in time on both pots. Every dollar moved toward a better
rate acquires a condition about when it can come back; every dollar kept reachable earns
nothing. On the near plan the same tension is a date: paying by Jan 9 costs 10% less but demands
$75.50 a collection out of the $81 a month that exists and leaves the club with $117 when the
February bill lands, while paying at Mar 6 costs $148 more but keeps slack — and the bill
decides which of those was right, after both were chosen. There is a cautious line — fund in the
certificate, club money in savings, Ride-only at Jan 9 — and I will not pretend otherwise. What
stops it being "the answer" is that no evidence row reads which cell was chosen. The rows read
the arithmetic (per-collection, monthly posting, start-date gap, arrival gap), the coherence
between a placement's condition and the dates the student's own flag created, and whether the
line was protected or the target explicitly re-set. The cautious student must do every one of
those, and the ambitious student is not rewarded for ambition. The only thing the cell changes
is how hard February is — and the balance sweep must show all four cells finishing on target and
all four finishing short, depending on how the bill is sourced.

**Good thinking, bad outcome.** Maya aims at Ride + Clinic at Mar 6 — the club has wanted the mechanic's clinic for years and
the later date leaves slack. She computes $75 a collection against the $81 a month that exists,
prices the fund in two lanes, puts it in the certificate (a four-year horizon; the twelve-month
fence costs her nothing), keeps club money in savings where the ride can reach it, and holds the
band whole through autumn. February's bill is $210 against a $90 estimate. She sources what she
can from outside the band, still lands $79 short of $1,480, states the $79 exactly and names the
bill, and explicitly re-sets the flag to Ride-only at Mar 6. The club rides; it does not get the
clinic; a twelve-year-old is disappointed. The evidence: er1 5, er2 5, er3 5 — the shipped
rule's own success condition is protect *or* explicitly re-set — er4 5 for naming the exact
shortfall and a cause the log contains, er5 scored on her note. The four rows of how-savings-
grow and three of compare-rates are untouched by the outcome; they are arithmetic she did. Her
results page carries five levels and the moment behind each, and nowhere does it carry the
sentence "the club missed the clinic".

**Bad thinking, good outcome.** Dev's whole method is "4.6 is the biggest number on the wall". He drags the fund and the club's
$610 into the certificate in the first thirty seconds, ignores the grey fence, and advances
months as fast as the board allows. His fund ends at $4,363.81 — the best outcome available on
the board — and the fleet arrives in July 2030, the earliest date anyone can reach. Four things
stop the product reporting mastery. The placement panel would not accept the block until he
typed two priced lanes, so compare-rates.er1 is his arithmetic and not his luck. compare-
rates.er2 reads his placements against his own flag date, finds the ride money behind a lock
that ends in September and never moved, and reads 2. February's bill has to be dragged onto a
pot; the only reachable pot is the reserved band, and he never re-sets the flag, so save-toward-
a-goal.er3 reads 2 and er4 reads 2 for a shortfall he cannot state. And there is no composite
anywhere in this product that could average a $4,363 fund into a mark. The teacher's page says:
best possible fund, and here is the month his ride money went behind a twelve-month lock.

**Why not a button.** The two facts that *are* the finance here are which pot the money is in and what date it started
working — interest is a function of exactly those. A PAY WITH SAVINGS button erases both: it
records a choice and destroys the position. A MOVE TO CERTIFICATE button plus a modal reading
"this money will be locked for 12 months" gives the student a sentence to agree to; the fence
drawn twelve months to the right of their own block, crossing their own goal flag and hollowing
it, gives them a fact about their own plan, before they commit, in the same picture as the thing
they are saving for. Same for the February bill: a PAY button hides the only question the bill
asks, which is *from where*. Dragging the bill onto a pot is the financial act, and dropping it
on the reserved band shortens the band in front of you and reopens the per-collection number —
which is the misconception "I'll save what's left" being made physically impossible to do
quietly.

**Would clicking be better.** The honest case for the button is strong and I have to concede most of it. Dragging is slower,
it is fiddly on a Chromebook trackpad, and every placement in this world could be a two-select
form — [Bike sale $420] into [Savings] starting [November] — which is faster, keyboard-native
and screen-reader-native. That form is in fact the keyboard path, and it is not a degraded
version; a student using it makes exactly the same commitments in fewer motions. So the drag is
not what I am defending. What I am defending is the board, and the distinction is this: a form
can express one placement, but it cannot show a plan. The fence only means something because it
is drawn against the goal flag on the same axis; "earlier ends with more" only lands because two
identical blocks placed in different months sit side by side with visibly different mint bands
and different numbers at their right ends. Remove the board and you can still make every
decision, but you cannot see the consequence of the one you just made without doing the
arithmetic the world is trying to teach. The drag is a convenience over the board; the board is
the argument. If playtest shows students placing without looking at it, the fence and the flag
are decoration and the mechanic has failed — which is in the kill condition, deliberately.

**Accessibility path.** The board is a grid, so it is a grid to everything. Rows are lanes plus the arrivals row,
columns are months; a roving tabindex walks cells with arrow keys, Space picks up the block
under focus, arrows move a ghost placement, Space drops, Escape cancels, Home/End jump to first
and last month. Focus announcements carry the whole construct: "Bike sale, four hundred twenty
dollars, arrives October. Currently in the tin, earning nothing." On drop: "Placed in
Certificate, November. Locked until November next year. Adds one dollar sixty-one a month at
four point six percent. Your ride date is March sixth — this money cannot pay for the ride."
That last clause is the fence, spoken. Nothing lives only in the picture: every bar prints its
interest figure as tabular text at its right end; the fence is also a state on the block
("locked until Nov") and a Reach column in the posting log; the arrival flags are dates in prose
("At three percent the fund reaches five thousand dollars in July 2032; at four point six
percent, July 2030"), which is the *sooner* construct itself, not a substitute for it. The
posting log — month, lane, opening, posted, closing — is a real table that is the primary
surface at 640px and at 200% zoom, where the month axis becomes a horizontally scrolling strip
with sticky lane labels and sticky month headers; sighted students use the same log to check
their arithmetic, so it is nobody's inferior path. Principal and interest differ by hatching as
well as hue and are labelled in text. Reduced motion: bars and postings draw instantly; the
monthly tick is a log row appearing, never information carried by movement. Muted audio: nothing
is audio-only. Colour never carries a state alone — locked cells carry a hatch and a word.

**Where it could teach something false.** 1) Rate wording. The board computes balance × rate ÷ 12 monthly, so a quoted "3.0%" yields
slightly more than 3.0% over a year. Guard: no lane card ever says APY; they say "3.0% a year,
paid as one-twelfth each month", which is exactly what the engine does, and the posting log
shows the arithmetic. 2) Magnitudes. At club scale interest is small and the world must never
imply otherwise — $420 in savings for five months earns $5.28. Guard: the near plan is won or
lost on deposits and the February bill, never on interest; the $4,200 fund is where interest is
loud ($163.81 by June, and two years off the fleet date), and the handover states both plainly.
3) Rate levels drift. Guard: rates are content data with an annual review date, and every claim
the world makes is relative ("the certificate pays more and locks the money"), never "this is
what banks pay". 4) Early withdrawal. Real certificates forfeit interest and sometimes
principal. Guard: the lane card states the exact rule implemented — 90 days of interest — and
the break preview shows the dollar figure before the student commits, with a back-out. 5)
Restricted funds. The Ramirez restriction is a donor letter and a club vote, which is how
restricted funds actually work; the copy must not suggest a bank partitions money. 6) Risk.
Savings and certificates at a credit union do not fall, and one line says so — which is also the
honest fence around this world's claims: it teaches nothing about assets that can lose value,
and does not pretend to. 7) Taxes, fees and inflation are not modelled; the teacher note says
so. 8) The compressed years tail is a broken axis and is labelled as one, with the two arrival
dates always given as dates in text so nothing rests on reading a squeezed strip.

**Kill condition.** Two. First: if fewer than half of runs contain a placement moved after the board showed a fence
or a flag change, the timeline is a form that gets filled in once and the fence and flag are
decoration — I abandon the board rather than defend it, because the entire anti-gimmick case
rests on the board being consulted. Second: if the arithmetic rows (per-collection, monthly
posting) score 0 in more than a third of runs, the world is measuring computation rather than
the idea, and it has to be re-numbered — rounder dues, a rate that divides cleanly — or dropped.
A third, softer kill: if the condition trap catches under 10% or over 70% of students, compare-
rates.er2 is not discriminating and the lane conditions need re-tuning before anything ships.

**What it does not prove.** It proves nothing about assets that can fall — no allocation across risky assets, no range-
versus-average, no diversification, so it produces nothing for NYSED 5.4 or 5.6 and must not be
mapped to them; those need a second world and `weigh-investment-risk` and `spread-the-risk`
still have no evidence requirements written. It does not show compounding over decades: at ten
months and $4,200 the difference between simple and compound interest is about three dollars,
and the world does not pretend to falsify "growth is linear" hard — the fleet arrival gap is the
strongest thing it has. It says nothing about fees, taxes, inflation, minimum balances beyond
the one modelled, or choosing an institution on anything but rate and condition. It does not
show a student would save their own money, only that they can run someone else's toward a dated
target. And a 5 on the per-collection row is evidence of one calculation done once, not of a
habit.

**Visual identity.** Cover the title and you are in a bike shop, not a bank. The ground is oil-rag grey pegboard; the
four lanes are hand-painted shop shelf signs — ARRIVALS, THE TIN, SAVINGS, CERTIFICATE — set in
a condensed industrial sans like a tool board's label tape, and every dollar figure is
monospaced tabular so money never reflows when it changes. Money blocks are punched cardstock
tickets with a hole and a hanging notch, the kind a shop hangs on a hook, and they hang from the
month they arrive. Principal is brass-amber, solid; interest is a cooler mint hatch that
accumulates on top — deliberately not the loud colour, so growth reads as accumulation rather
than reward. Locks are a matte grey-red fence of hatched uprights running twelve months out, and
a fenced flag is drawn hollow. The compressed years tail is a fold in the pegboard with the seam
visible, so time obviously continues past the ten months you can touch. Motion belongs to the
student: money moves only when they move it, and the single system motion is the monthly tick
writing one row into the posting log, which is a printed workshop ledger down the right side.
Against Eight Weeks to the Showcase (warm hardwood, court, a season strip) and Run the Pop-Up
(night market, stall lights, perishable stock), this world is cold light, metal, and a wall you
hang things on.

### Three Years of Saturdays

**Carries** `save-toward-a-goal`, `how-savings-grow`, `compare-rates`  
**Verbs** ORDER · PLACE · PROJECT · REPAIR  
**Budget** 2180 words / 25 minutes

*The student decides, in order, what the bike shop's money pays each month and where the club's long fund sits, runs the year with that standing order, then projects three years forward to say when the club can stand on its own.*

Ridge Street Bike Shop is run by kids, on Saturdays. You handle its money. Two things are owed.
The tool wall costs $540 at the parts store, and the price goes up to $720 on 30 June — nine
months from now. And the city's $120 a month stops in three years, so the club needs $9,000
saved by then to stay open. Every month, all the shop's money comes down one belt. You decide
what it passes first, and what it passes last.

**Signature mechanic.** THE SPLIT LINE. Side elevation of the shop's back room. At the left edge is an intake hopper;
each month the shop's money arrives on the belt as ONE bar of coin-yellow with the figure
printed on it ("Month 3 · $390"). The bar rides left to right along a steel rail past FOUR
station heads, and the student sets the rail's ORDER by dragging heads along it: PARTS (the bin
that keeps Saturdays running), TOOL JAR (glass jar, with the goal tag clipped to its neck), THE
FUND (a chute into the chosen account's canister), TILL (the open end of the belt). Each head
carries one typed number — its take per month — except exactly one head, which the student
assigns the setting ALL THAT'S LEFT. Pressing RUN sends the bar down the line: at each head a
segment the size of that head's take is cut off and drops into its container, and the bar
visibly continues shorter. If the bar is thinner than a head's take, that head takes what is
there, its mouth STAYS OPEN with the shortfall printed beside it ("Fund took $140 of $195 — $55
short"), and every head downstream gets nothing, on screen, that month. After the pass: the
jar's coin level has risen against a scored line at $540 with the date tag beside it; the Fund
canister shows its contents as two stacked colours — a dark green block labelled "put in $2,340"
and a thin gold band on top labelled "made $96" — and one new line has printed on the passbook
to the right (Mo 07 | in 195.00 | interest 9.14 | balance 2,891.40). The order is a STANDING
instruction: RUN advances month after month unattended, printing receipts, and stops only when
something needs the student. Second object, on the wall: the DOUBLE PASSBOOK, a two-column
ledger board. The student clips a configuration card into each slot — {how much, which month it
goes in, monthly deposit, which account} — and cranks; each column prints its 36 monthly lines
with the $9,000 target line ruled across the board. The board does arithmetic. It never ranks,
never recommends, and never says which row crossed the line — the student reads that off
themselves.

**The tradeoff.** Three-cornered, and none of the corners is virtue. (1) THE JAR is near and dated — $540 by month
9, $720 after. (2) THE FUND is far and compounds — every month a dollar is not in it is a month
of growth gone, and the ledger shows exactly how little that is per month and how much it is
over 36. (3) PARTS is the line that GENERATES the income — starve it and Saturdays close and
next month's repair fees drop $60, so "savings first, always" is punished by the world rather
than praised. Layered on top: placement timing against liquidity (money placed in month 1 grows
longest but is the money the compressor wants), and rate against conditions (4.1% becomes worse
than 2.0% the moment the balance sits under $1,000, because $10 of fee beats $1.50 of interest,
and a student who places only part of the opening balance will find that out). DOMINANT-STRATEGY
CHECK: there is no free buffer — holding $200 idle for 36 months costs about $26 of growth,
roughly what one broken condition costs, so cautious and aggressive are both defensible and
neither is free. This is a `balance.ts` sweep obligation, not a claim: the world does not ship
until the sweep confirms no single order-plus-takes configuration reaches both goals with slack
across every seed. If one exists, the numbers are wrong, not the design.

**Good thinking, bad outcome.** A student places the opening $1,200 in the credit union in month 1, runs both start months and
both accounts on the rig first, sets the rail PARTS → JAR → FUND → TILL with the Fund at $195
and the Till holding what is left, moves the tag nowhere, and absorbs the compressor out of the
Till and one month of Fund. The seeded parts requirement then comes in at $145 three months
running, the Fund is short twice, and at month 12 the projection says the club stands alone in
month 39, not 36. They miss. The evidence is untouched, because the structural rule of this
world is that NO EVIDENCE REQUIREMENT READS A FINAL BALANCE. S1 is read off the tag, the jar's
take, the shock month and the stated shortfall. S2 is read off the form's deposit figure, the
crossing month against their own ledger, and their two start-month runs. S3 is read off which
accounts they ran and whether a condition fired. Every one of those is intact, and several of
them are 5s, on a run that failed.

**Bad thinking, good outcome.** A student puts the whole $1,200 in the certificate because 4.9% is the biggest number, never
touches the rig, never clips a figure or a date to the tag, sets the Fund head to ALL THAT'S
LEFT and leaves the jar at $20, and gets a seed with cheap parts and a late shock. The Fund
lands at $9,100 and the shop looks fine. The product reports: S1.er1 = 0 (no target, no date),
S1.er2 = 0 (the goal had no per-month number — ALL THAT'S LEFT is the misconception,
mechanised), S1.er3 = 0 (the jar was cut and the tag never moved), S3.er1 = 0 (no comparison
exists in the log), S3.er3 = 0 or 2 (no months-sooner figure, or one nothing supports), S2.er3 =
2. There is no composite score to launder that into a pass, no final total appears as evidence
anywhere, and the teacher's page shows the tag that was never filled in and the empty rig log.
Mastery is not reported because mastery was not observed — the money is beside the point.

**Why not a button.** The order of the heads on the rail. A button labelled SAVE FIRST asserts the concept; the rail
makes the student author it and then makes scarcity actually flow past their choice. "I'll save
what's left" is not an opinion in this world — it is a physical position, fourth on a rail, and
the consequence is a mouth that stays open while the money that would have filled it is already
in a bin upstream. No button, dropdown or checkbox produces the moment where a student watches
their own priority rule run unattended for three months and come back thinner than they planned.
The second thing that is not a button is the goal tag: it is a physical object clipped to the
jar carrying an amount and a date, and it must be MOVED to be changed, so that re-setting a
target is an act with a timestamp rather than an edit to a field that overwrites the old number
silently. `save-toward-a-goal.er3` turns entirely on the difference between re-setting a target
and missing one, and a text input cannot tell those apart.

**Would clicking be better.** Argue the other side honestly: a form with four amount fields and a priority dropdown carries
exactly the same data, builds in a fifth of the time, is faster for the student, and is
trivially accessible. For the AMOUNTS, that argument wins outright — which is why every take in
this world is a typed number in the existing spinbutton primitive, not a dragged slider.
Dragging a quantity is motor skill wearing a finance costume, and it is not in this design. Only
two things stay physical, and each has to earn it separately. The rail earns it because priority
under scarcity has to RUN, unattended, across months the student is not watching, for the
concept to exist at all — a dropdown records a preference, the rail executes a policy and hands
back the receipts. The tag earns it because a commitment that can be silently overwritten is not
a commitment. Where the projection is concerned the anti-gimmick answer runs the other way and I
concede it: the double passbook is a calculator, and it should be, because no seventh grader
computes 36 months of monthly compounding by hand and a world that demanded it would be
assessing arithmetic stamina. The line is that the machine does arithmetic and the student does
judgement — the rig never ranks the accounts, never recommends one, never announces which row
crossed the target, and never fills a single field on the city form. And the kill switch is
named: if playtest shows students treat the rail as decoration and never revise an order, the
honest move is to collapse it to a numbered list, which is the same object — the keyboard path
already is that list.

**Accessibility path.** The belt IS an ordered list, not a picture of one. The rail is an `<ol>` of four station groups;
reorder is Alt+Up / Alt+Down on a focused head, with a live-region announcement that names the
new position AND the new financial relationship ("Fund moved to position 2 of 4. Fund now takes
before Till."). Each head is a labelled group containing one numeric spinbutton (the hand-built
spinbutton already shipped and already keyboard-complete) plus one radio for ALL THAT'S LEFT,
which is a single-selection group across the four heads. RUN prints, into the live region and
into the page, the per-station settlement as sentences — "Parts took $115 of $115. Jar took $60
of $60. Fund took $140 of $195, $55 short. Till got nothing." — which is the entire information
content of the cut-and-drop animation, so `prefers-reduced-motion` loses nothing but the motion
and the animation is never the only carrier. The passbook and both rig columns are real
`<table>`s with captions and column headers, so a screen reader user navigates the interest
column cell by cell and arguably reads compounding more directly than a sighted user does; the
row where the balance first crosses the target carries a text marker, not a colour. Colour never
carries meaning alone: the canister's dark-green and gold blocks are labelled "put in $X" and
"made $Y" in text at all times, and the shortfall is a printed figure, not a red glow. At 200%
zoom and at 640px the rail reflows to a single vertical column where top means first — order
semantics survive because they were never in the horizontal axis, they were in the list — and
the station heads stack rather than squeeze, which is the specific failure mode the
accessibility recon found on the existing money board and this world must not repeat. The goal
tag is a two-field form (amount, month) inside a group whose accessible name is "Goal tag on the
tool jar", and moving it fires the same `goal.tag.moved` event a drag does. No audio carries
information anywhere. There is no worksheet fallback because there is nothing to fall back from:
every construct — priority order, scarcity cutting off downstream, principal against interest,
two ledgers side by side — is a list, a table or a sentence before it is ever a picture.

**Where it could teach something false.** (1) RATE LABELS. Never say APY. Every account is labelled "X% a year, paid monthly" and the
engine uses r = X/12 exactly, so the passbook the student reads is arithmetically the account
they were shown. An APY label with a monthly-compounding engine is a world whose own ledger
contradicts its own signage. (2) CERTIFICATE MECHANICS. A real 12-month certificate takes a
lump, not a monthly deposit. The certificate slot therefore accepts the placement only and
monthly belt money must go elsewhere — model it truthfully or cut it. (3) THE TOP-UP RULE. The
city tops up 10% of DEPOSITS, and the engine must actually compute it that way, or the world
teaches a false rule while assessing the true one. (4) HIGHEST RATE WINS. Guarded structurally,
not by copy: at a $900 balance the credit union pays $0 and charges $10 while the 2.0% account
pays $1.50, and the world must leave that state genuinely reachable — a student who places only
part of the opening balance must be able to sit under $1,000 and watch it happen. (5)
OVERSTATING COMPOUNDING. At $195 a month and 4.1%, interest runs $4.10 in month 1 and about $30
in month 36. That is the honest shape and it is undramatic. No animation, colour ramp or sound
may make it look bigger than it is; the gold band is thin because the gold band is thin. (6) THE
TWO-MONTH DIFFERENCE. 4.1% reaches $9,000 in month 36 and 2.0% in month 38 — $317 apart at month
36. The copy must say what that is (the difference between making the deadline and missing it)
and must never say a 2.1-point rate difference is transformative. (7) PROJECTION AS PROMISE. The
rig board is headed "If nothing changes" and the played year has already shown the student a
shock the projection did not know about — that sequencing is the guard, and it should not be
softened.

**Kill condition.** Abandon the design if the balance sweep finds any single order-plus-takes configuration that
reaches both goals with slack across every seed — the three-cornered tradeoff would be fake and
the world would be a virtue test with a machine on it. Abandon it equally if playtest shows the
rail order is inert: if 80% or more of students place the Fund last, never revise after a
shortfall, and their .er2/.er3 levels are uncorrelated with rail position, then the signature
interaction is decoration and the honest thing is to ship the numbered list and stop calling it
a belt.

**What it does not prove.** It does not prove the student can save their OWN money — this is the club's money, and the
competing claim is an operating cost, never a want of their own, so nothing here speaks to
whether they would protect a savings line against something they personally wanted. It does not
touch 5.4 or 5.6: there is one growth account, no asset falls, no range against average, no
allocation across assets — weigh-investment-risk and spread-the-risk are untouched and must not
be claimed from this world by inference. It does not show a student can compute compound
interest by hand; the rig does that arithmetic deliberately. And it does not show they can build
a savings plan from nothing — the income lines and both goals are given, so the plan is repaired
and prioritised rather than originated.

**Visual identity.** Take the title off and it is still unmistakable: a workshop back room in cross-section, warm
grey concrete and bare bulb, a steel rail running straight across the middle of the screen at
waist height with four squat machine heads bolted to it, a glass jar with a paper tag wired to
its neck, a steel canister, a pegboard of bike tools behind, and a printed passbook column
running down the right edge. Nothing else on either existing world is horizontal, industrial, or
has a spine of running text down one side. OBJECT LANGUAGE: everything the student touches is a
fixture — heads bolt, tags wire, cards clip, the crank turns. Nothing floats. COLOUR is a three-
word vocabulary that never swaps roles anywhere in the world: coin-yellow is money moving on the
belt, dark green is money the club put in, gold is money the money made. Those three and nothing
else carry meaning; the rest of the palette is concrete, steel and one red for an open mouth.
TYPOGRAPHY: station heads in stamped industrial uppercase, and every figure in the world — belt
bar, jar, canister, passbook, rig, form — set in one tabular monospace, so any two numbers in
this world can be read as a column. MOTION: exactly one idea, ever — a bar of money travelling
left to right and losing segments. Nothing else on screen moves, which is why the one thing that
moves means something.
