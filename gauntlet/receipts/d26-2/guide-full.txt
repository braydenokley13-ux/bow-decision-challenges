READ THIS FIRST

THE WHOLE RESOURCE IN ONE MINUTE.
What does it assess?
Whether students can apply budgeting under uncertainty — build a workable eight-week plan, keep it working when income and costs change, and justify it.
When do I use it?
After you have taught the skills. It is an application task, not a lesson.
How long?
20–28 minutes for most students. One sitting, one device, no sound.
What do students do?
They handle the money in one of two stories, and they pick which. Eight Weeks to the Showcase: a player’s season, where they choose housing, build a plan, absorb a Week 5 loss and a new required cost, and repair it. Run the Pop-Up: four Saturdays at a night market, where they take a booth, decide how much food to cook against the crowd it draws, and cover a generator that dies with the biggest night still ahead. Every crowd figure is printed before the student orders, so what is assessed is planning against known demand rather than predicting it. Both ask for a plan that fits the money, both break it, and both end with the student explaining in writing what they did.
Do the two give me the same thing back?
Yes, and that is the point of having two. Both stories collect evidence against the same named parts of the work and are judged on the same rubric, so a class where students chose differently still produces one answer about the class. What differs is the story, the decisions and the numbers — never what is being looked for.
What do I get back?
For each student, what the evidence shows against each skill — one line for each thing the work had to show, every judgement traceable to the moment in their own run. Plus what the class decided, and one written explanation you read and score yourself. Counts for your gradebook come with it.
How do I launch it?
Create a class, paste your class list, and print the cards — one per student. Put the class code on the board; a student types that code and the code on their own card. They never type an email address, a password or a birthday. No list to hand? Students type the class code and their own first name instead.

BEFORE STUDENTS BEGIN

Teach these first. This is an application task, not a lesson.

01
Distinguish dependable and conditional income.
02
Combine recurring and one-time costs.
03
Build a budget that does not exceed available money.
04
Separate committed money from money that can change.
05
Revise after income or expenses change.
06
Explain a trade-off with relevant numbers.

Schools may use their own instruction.

A two-day sample mini-unit, if you want one

This sample sequence shows the prerequisite skills students should learn before Plan Under Pressure. It does not pre-teach Avery's answers.

Day 01
Build a plan from dependable money

Example context: Jordan is saving for a robotics camp while earning money from neighborhood jobs.

Dependable vs conditional income

Sort a guaranteed allowance from snow-shoveling money that depends on weather.

Recurring + one-time full cost

Calculate six weeks of bus fare plus a one-time registration fee.

A budget that works

Give every dollar one job across required costs, a goal, a reserve, and flexible cash.

Exit prompt: “Which dollars can Jordan count on before the weather is known?”
Day 02
Revise when conditions change

Example context: Sam is preparing for a school music showcase with a short paid equipment-helper role.

Committed vs adjustable money

Mark the registration and transit already committed; keep future savings adjustable.

Contingency thinking

Construct a lower-income version without telling students which priority to reduce.

Unexpected change

Add a required repair cost, revise the plan, and explain one trade-off with two numbers.

Debrief prompt: “How can two different revised plans both be financially coherent?”
Keep the assessment clean

Teach the skills with different names, amounts, and situations. During the BOW challenge, allow calculators and access tools, but do not tell students which financial strategy to choose.

RUNNING IT
Create a class, paste your class list, print the cards and hand them out. Students type the class code from the board and the code on their card.
Allow 20–28 minutes. Do not coach a financial strategy.
Read and score the written explanations. Nothing a student writes is machine-scored.
Open the class: what the evidence shows, and what to teach next.
Run the debrief with the room.

A high grade reflects the financial skills a student actually showed — not a preference for saving more, spending less, taking a job, or choosing the cheapest option.
