# The External Bar: Middle-School Financial-Literacy Curriculum & Application/Assessment Products

Research pass for the BOW Decision Challenges gauntlet, Phase 1. Compiled 2026-08-18. Every claim below is sourced; where a source could not be verified first-hand (paywalled, sunset, or PDF that would not render), that limitation is flagged explicitly rather than smoothed over.

**Method note:** Common Sense Education's edtech review program — the standard third-party review source for exactly this category — announced it is "taking a break from publishing and updating edtech reviews" as of January 2026, with existing review pages scheduled for removal in February 2026 ([Common Sense Education FAQ](https://www.commonsense.org/education/reviews/everfi-financial-literacy-for-high-school)). This means several of the most-cited teacher reviews in this space (EVERFI, Financial Football, Hands on Banking) are disappearing from the open web during the exact window this research was done. I recovered what I could from live search snippets before the pages fully vanish; I could not reach the Wayback Machine from this environment to archive full review text, so ratings/pros/cons for those three are reported at the fidelity the search snippets gave me, not from the primary page.

## 1. Product-by-product findings

### NGPF (Next Gen Personal Finance) — the incumbent, free, teacher-facing content leader

NGPF is a 501(c)(3) that bills itself as the largest provider of personal-finance curriculum and PD for U.S. educators ([ngpf.org](https://www.ngpf.org/)).

- **Arcade (games).** 11-13 free browser games, each 5-20 minutes, each a single-topic scenario (e.g. *Payback* = paying for college in 20 min, *Money Magic* = budgeting in 15 min, *Shady Sam* = predatory lending in 10 min, *Build Your $TAX* = 20-year investing simulation in 20 min) ([NGPF Arcade](https://www.ngpf.org/arcade/)). Each game ships with a teacher reflection worksheet, but the game itself is the only "evidence" produced — there is no persistent student record inside the Arcade tying gameplay to a rubric.
- **Middle School Course.** 9 units / 9 weeks, ~45 min/lesson, delivered as Google Slides / Nearpod decks a teacher runs live; covers money values, banking, saving, budgeting, credit, investing, digital-safety/insurance, careers, and post-secondary planning ([Middle School Course](https://www.ngpf.org/courses/middle-school-course/)). Assessment = a diagnostic exam, a unit exam per unit (2-9), and a final exam. Concretely, unit review materials are **20 multiple-choice questions** delivered via Kahoot/Wayground, used to prep for a unit test built the same way (verified against a live NGPF Budgeting unit-review study guide: [Docsity](https://www.docsity.com/en/docs/ngpf-budgeting-complete-exam-questions-and-answers/11603442/), [Quizlet study guide](https://quizlet.com/study-guides/ngpf-budgeting-unit-review-8b99c2d3-a8ee-495c-b9bf-bf0e70aa5cb1)). So NGPF's actual assessment artifact for its flagship middle-school course is **vocabulary/recall multiple choice** ("gross pay vs. net pay," "50/30/20 budget" term-matching), not a scored decision under uncertainty.
- **Case Studies** are NGPF's closest analog to "application after instruction": realistic, ambiguous, no-single-right-answer scenarios that put the student in a decision-maker role (peer advisor/counselor), include data exhibits and deliberately-planted misinformation to catch, and require the student to evaluate multiple courses of action ([NGPF Case Studies](https://www.ngpf.org/case-studies/), [Making a Case for Case Studies](https://www.ngpf.org/blog/activities/making-a-case-for-case-studies/)). These are discussion/worksheet artifacts scored by the teacher, not auto-graded.
- **Teacher account model.** A teacher registers, is manually vetted (school email or uploaded proof — ID badge, gradebook screenshot — reviewed within ~1 business day), and unlocks answer keys and assessments ([Unlock NGPF Teacher Access](https://www.ngpf.org/onboarding-step1/), [Teacher Toolkit](https://www.ngpf.org/account/teacher-toolkit/)). **There is no student account, no roster, no class code, no gradebook, and no LMS sync inside NGPF itself.** NGPF's own official guidance is blunt: the blog post titled "Should My Students Create an NGPF Student Account?" answers "The quick answer is **NO NO NO!**" — NGPF is a content-distribution platform; teachers deliver via Slides/Nearpod/Canvas and collect evidence in their own LMS ([NGPF blog](https://www.ngpf.org/blog/pro-tip-from-ngpf/should-my-students-create-an-ngpf-student-account/)). This means NGPF, despite being the category leader, has **weaker** identity/roster infrastructure than BOW's class-code-and-seat-number model — it has none at all.
- **Cost:** free, permanently, funded by donors, not corporate product sponsors in the transactional sense ([Middle School Course page](https://www.ngpf.org/courses/middle-school-course/)).

### iCivics — the model for free, standards-aligned, game-based classroom infrastructure (not a finance-content competitor)

iCivics is civics/government-focused (founded by Sandra Day O'Connor); its one clearly economics-adjacent game is *People's Pie* (federal budget-balancing) — it is **not** a personal-finance content competitor, but its **account/classroom architecture is the strongest structural comparandum found in this research** ([iCivics teacher user guide, PDF](https://www.nhcivics.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/icivics_teacher_user_guide.pdf)):

- Teacher creates a **named class** with a unique **class code**, a default password, and a start/end date.
- Students either self-register with their own email + the class code, **or** the teacher bulk-adds students by first/last name and the system auto-generates a username + shared default password (no student email required) — i.e., iCivics supports exactly the "no-email-needed" case BOW is in, but still produces a **persistent, named student identity** tied across sessions, not a seat number that resets.
- Teachers create **assignments** (a specific game/webquest + due date + a "completion condition" the system tracks automatically), see a roster with green checkmarks for completion, and can click into any student's profile to see **Game Sessions**: how many times they played, and how well they scored.
- Teachers can post **class discussions** and **announcements** students see in an inbox.
- All content is tagged to all 50 states' standards, individually searchable ("Standards Search").
- Cost: free.

Read against BOW: iCivics proves that "no student email required" and "a real, cross-session student identity with a completion/score record" are **not in tension** — a free nonprofit product already ships both. (Caveat: the guide I extracted is an older iCivics UI snapshot hosted by a NH civics affiliate — it references IE9/Firefox 10 compatibility, so the current product has likely been rebuilt, but the class-code + roster + assignment-completion-tracking model is corroborated by iCivics' own current help-center article titles: ["What is a class code?"](https://icivics.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/206154038-What-is-a-class-code), ["Add Students To iCivics Class"](https://icivics.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/230355968-Add-Students-To-iCivics-Class).)

### EverFi (FutureSmart + Vault) — the corporate-sponsored, dashboard-forward incumbent

- **FutureSmart** (grades 6-8): 7 digital lessons, ~25 min each, plus up to 9 hours of offline extension material. Story-driven — students join a "Help Squad" of interns solving fictional characters' money problems (budgeting, payment methods, career planning, insurance/investing), and build a personal "Blueprint" of career/financial goals ([EverFi FutureSmart](https://everfi.com/courses/k-12/financial-literacy-middle-school/)). Independent validation is claimed at **ESSA Level III** (statistically significant gains in knowledge, self-efficacy, behavior) — this is a vendor claim, not independently re-verified here.
- **Vault: Understanding Money** (grades 4-6, upper-elementary/early-middle overlap with BOW's 6-8 band): 5-6 modules, 20-30 min each, ~2-3 hrs total ([Vault course page](https://everfi.com/courses/k-12/financial-literacy-elementary-students/)).
- **Teacher dashboard:** real-time completion rates, pre/post quiz scores per lesson, downloadable grade reports, ability to see exactly where a class is struggling ([EverFi teacher resources](https://support.everfi.com/course-materials-resources)). This is a genuinely stronger reporting layer than anything NGPF or BOW currently ship — auto-graded, exportable, per-student, per-lesson.
- **Cost:** free to schools, funded by corporate/bank sponsors.
- **Criticism found:** Common Sense's now-sunsetting review flagged that "students have vastly different experiences and backgrounds... lessons might not take their specific experiences into account," urging teacher care in discussion ([Common Sense review, via search snippet before sunset](https://www.commonsense.org/education/reviews/everfi-financial-literacy-for-high-school)). Separately, *Rethinking Schools* — a teacher-run pedagogy journal — published a sustained critique naming EverFi and Junior Achievement specifically: that bank/corporate-sponsored curricula frame wage and debt outcomes as purely individual choices, omit labor-rights content, and "obscure the systemic and structural dimensions of debt," while citing PISA-style achievement-gap framing that mirrors corporate-ed-reform deficit narratives ([Rethinking Schools, "You're to Blame"](https://rethinkingschools.org/articles/youre-to-blame-how-financial-literacy-curricula-make-students-financially-illiterate/)). This is an ideological critique, not a UX/assessment-fidelity one, but it is a real, citable line of criticism a reviewer could raise against any bank-sponsored product — and it is one BOW (no visible sponsor) is structurally immune to.

### Zogo — gamified micro-learning app (not classroom-native)

Free consumer/student app; 1,200+ bite-sized modules (5 concepts + a 5-question quiz each), gamified with "pineapple" points redeemable for real rewards, funded by bank/credit-union partners who sponsor specific modules ([Zogo](https://zogo.com/), [thewaystowealth.com review](https://www.thewaystowealth.com/zogo-review/)). Common Sense's **Privacy** evaluation (a separate, still-active arm from the sunsetting review program) gives Zogo a **"Warning"** rating: it collects name, email, school, geolocation, and self-reported financial-interest survey data, allows third parties to track users across sites/apps, and shares data for third-party advertising/marketing, with unclear disclosure on data sale ([Common Sense Privacy Evaluation](https://privacy.commonsense.org/evaluation/Zogo-Finance-Simplified)). No teacher dashboard/classroom layer surfaced in this research — it functions as a self-directed app, not a class tool.

### Banzai — free, credit-union-sponsored middle/high content

Middle-school course: choose-your-own-adventure scenarios (summer-camp job, budgeting, paying a cell-phone bill, unexpected car trouble); teacher toolkit with downloadable worksheets unlocks after a free account; claims adoption by 45,000+ teachers ([Banzai middle school](https://banzai.org/courses/finlit/middle-school), [TeachersFirst review](https://teachersfirst.org/single.cfm?id=17033)). **No independent critical review surfaced** in this research — every source found was vendor or credit-union-partner marketing copy; this is itself worth flagging as a gap (a product this widely adopted with essentially no third-party critical coverage online).

### FoolProof — free, self-grading, explicitly anti-marketing framing

Web-based, self-grading, built around three "habits": healthy skepticism, trustworthiness, personal responsibility; explicit design goal is to inoculate students against manipulative marketing/credit offers rather than teach product mechanics ([FoolProofMe middle school](https://www.foolproofme.org/academy/middleschool)). Claims a **34.3% pre/post test score increase** across 375,000+ students at 10,000+ institutions ([FoolProof Foundation](https://www.foolprooffoundation.org/what-we-do/foolproof-for-schools)) — vendor-reported, not independently re-verified here. One parent-facing criticism found: some feel the meme/animation-heavy delivery is "unprofessional" ([Modulo review](https://www.modulo.app/all-resources/foolproofme)).

### Khan Academy Personal Finance + Khanmigo

Self-paced, free, video+article+practice-exercise course (units span budgeting/saving, credit, financial goals, loans/debt, investments/retirement, insurance, taxes, scams/fraud); recently added 8 dedicated **Teacher Resource** lessons with discussion guides and a 2-3 min "About this Unit" video from Sal Khan for teachers ([Khan Academy blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/customize-your-curriculum-empower-your-students-with-financial-literacy/)). Built for a broad range but written to "readily integrate into high school curriculums" per Khan's own framing — **no distinct middle-school edition** was found; a 6th-8th-grade teacher would be assigning high-school-leveled content. **Khanmigo** (AI tutor/assistant) is free for teachers; district student licensing runs roughly **$15-35/student/year** depending on source ([khanmigo.ai/pricing](https://www.khanmigo.ai/pricing), [edusageai.com](https://www.edusageai.com/blogs/how-much-does-khanmigo-cost-pricing-for-teachers-and-schools-in-2026)) — i.e., the AI layer is **not free at scale**, unlike the base course.

### Junior Achievement — JA Finance Park and JA BizTown (the in-person simulation bar)

This is the most structurally serious comparandum for BOW because it is also a **multi-session-plus-simulation** design, not a single sitting.

- **JA Finance Park:** 13 required 45-minute in-class lessons taught by the classroom teacher, culminating in a **one-day, volunteer-mentored, on-site (or mobile-unit or virtual) simulation**. Students are assigned a randomized adult persona — career, salary, family situation, credit score — and must build and manage a monthly budget by physically/virtually visiting "storefronts" for housing, food, insurance, etc. Two tiers: Entry (middle school) and Advanced (high school) ([JA Finance Park Virtual](https://jausa.ja.org/programs/ja-finance-park-virtual)).
- **JA BizTown** (grades 4-6): 12 teacher-led prep lessons + 1 debrief lesson, culminating in a 4-5 hour visit where each student works a real job at one of 40+ simulated businesses, earns and spends simulated currency, pays rent/taxes/utilities, and votes for city officials ([JA BizTown brief](https://northernindiana.ja.org/dA/652bdd4744/file/JA%20BizTown%20Program%20Brief.pdf)).
- **Evidence produced:** a "financial health meter" during the simulation (Finance Park Virtual) and a completed monthly-budget worksheet — these are **process artifacts from the simulation day itself**, not a scored written explanation.
- **Independent evaluation:** a 2015 RMC Research Corporation study (commissioned by JA) found a **statistically significant, moderate-to-large effect size** gain on a 7-item financial-literacy knowledge pre/post test versus a matched comparison group who did not take the program, and 92% of participants self-reported the program helped them understand budgeting ([JA Finance Park Evaluation Summary, PDF](https://www.jawm.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/JA-Finance-Park-Evaluation-Summary.pdf)) — this is a vendor-commissioned but methodologically described (pre/post + comparison group) study, stronger evidence than most competitors offer.
- **Access model:** delivered through ~80 independent local JA chapters, so cost and format (facility vs. mobile vs. virtual) vary by region — some report free-to-school with only bus/transport cost (e.g. JA of New Jersey), others charge a small per-student fee with need-based aid ([JA Western PA](https://www.jawesternpa.org/finance-park/)). No single national price could be verified.
- **Criticism (structural, from Rethinking Schools):** JA is named specifically as historically funded by "Kraft Foods... UPS, ExxonMobil, Goldman Sachs, and New York Life Insurance," delivering curriculum "for free" while training corporate volunteers to "take over the teaching... for days, if not weeks" — a critique of who controls the content and pedagogy, not of the simulation's mechanical design ([Rethinking Schools](https://rethinkingschools.org/articles/youre-to-blame-how-financial-literacy-curricula-make-students-financially-illiterate/)).
- **Logistics burden this implies for the bar:** a full-fidelity, in-person, mentor-staffed one-day simulation is objectively **more expensive and harder to schedule** than BOW's ~20-minute browser world — this is a place a browser product should be honest it is choosing a different tradeoff, not silently losing to.

### Practical Money Skills / Financial Football / Financial Soccer (Visa)

- **Financial Football**: NFL-branded trivia-as-yardage arcade game, three age tiers (Rookie 11-14 fits middle school), playable 5-30 minutes, with accompanying classroom lesson modules ([Financial Football](https://www.practicalmoneyskills.com/en/play/financial_football.html)). The clearest, most quotable third-party verdict found anywhere in this research, from Common Sense Education (captured via search snippet before the review-archive sunset): **"Lessons do the teaching; the game only tests understanding,"** bottom-lined as "Solid financial literacy lessons with a fun football-themed reinforcement" — i.e., independent reviewers explicitly separate "teaches" from "tests," and rate Financial Football only as a *testing* layer bolted onto separate lessons, not as an application-after-instruction experience in itself.
- **Practical Money Skills** core curriculum: 22 lessons across 6 areas, aligned to Jump$tart/CEE/Common Core, written for grades 9-12 primarily; a disability-accessible version exists ([Practical Money Skills Teach](https://pmscom.siuat.visa.com/en/teach.html)).

### Hands on Banking (Wells Fargo)

Grades 1-12 tiered program; the Teen (grades 6-8) track is **5 online units plus an assessment**, paired with short character-narrated videos and printable activity sheets; explicitly recommended by reviewers for a flipped-classroom model (assign online portion at home, do activities in class) ([Common Sense review, via search snippet](https://www.commonsense.org/education/reviews/hands-on-banking-financial-education), [Middle School Instructor Guide, PDF](https://youth.handsonbanking.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/InstructorGuide_MiddleSchool.pdf)).

### SIFMA Foundation Stock Market Game — the multi-week classroom simulation with the most public academic criticism

- **Structure:** teams of 1-5 students manage a **hypothetical $100,000 brokerage account** in a live-market simulation running as a competitive season (fall/spring/year-long) or a flexible 8-12 week "enrichment" session ([pfew.org — How the Game Works](https://www.pfew.org/stock-market-game/how-it-works)). Grade grouping includes elementary (4-5), **middle (6-8)**, and high school.
- **Teacher tools:** a "Teacher Support Center" with lesson plans, quizzes, and standards correlations; portfolio value is the scoring mechanism, with regional/grade-band leaderboards and awards for top 3 teams per session/region/grade group.
- **Cost:** $20/team standard session, $30/team year-long, teacher's own practice portfolio is free.
- **Independent/vendor claim:** SIFMA cites that SMG players "scored significantly higher on mathematics and financial literacy tests" than non-players ([SIFMA Foundation](https://www.sifma.org/about/sifma-foundation)) — vendor-cited, not independently re-verified here.
- **Substantial third-party criticism found — this is the single richest critical vein uncovered in the entire research pass:**
  - *Dollars & Sense* (economics-education magazine): the short-timeframe, lump-sum design "teach[es] the wrong lessons" — winning students learn that concentrating in volatile stocks beats diversification, because in a several-week window that is factually the winning strategy, which is the opposite of sound long-term investing advice ([Dollars & Sense](https://www.dollarsandsense.org/teaching-about-stocks-for-fun-and-propaganda/)).
  - A finance-education blog lists five specific mechanical failures with direct quotes: (1) "Keeping a position open for only a few days or weeks is speculation, not investing"; (2) the timeframe is too short to realize dividends; (3) "Giving students $100,000 in virtual cash and telling them to spend it immediately sends the wrong signal" about how real investing works; (4) students never practice limit/stop orders, "leaving students with the impression that trading is all about guessing"; (5) the game never introduces safer derivatives concepts once stock ownership is mastered ([Saylor Financial Fundamentals](https://www.saylorfinancialfundamentals.com/post/a-stock-market-game-for-students-may-teach-the-wrong-lessons-5-reasons-why-better-alternatives)).
  - A separate essay frames this even more sharply as **"The Pedagogical Casino: Why the Stock Market Game is a Gateway to Disordered Gambling"** — arguing that short-horizon, high-variance simulated trading with public leaderboards trains reward patterns closer to gambling than investing ([financerevamp.com](https://www.financerevamp.com/post/the-pedagogical-casino-why-the-stock-market-game-is-a-gateway-to-disordered-gambling)).
  - A peer-reviewed critical review exists specifically evaluating cross-curricular integration of SMG ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/23991552_A_Critical_Review_of_Learning_from_the_Market_Integrating_The_Stock_Market_Game_across_the_Curriculum)) — I could not open the full text from this environment, so I am citing its existence/title only, not its findings.
  - **Why this matters for BOW specifically:** SMG is the field's most direct precedent for "a multi-week browser simulation as the assessment vehicle," and the dominant expert critique of it is precisely that a short, score-maximizing, leaderboard-driven simulation **teaches students to play the simulation, not to reason soundly about money** — i.e., the exact failure mode a decision-simulation product must design against. Any critic evaluating BOW's basketball/food-stall worlds will have this exact critique loaded and ready to fire if BOW's worlds reward "gaming the mechanic" over sound reasoning.

### H&R Block Budget Challenge — closest single-vendor structural analog to BOW (high school only)

A free **9-week online simulation**: students role-play a recent college grad six months into a first job, managing a real paycheck, checking account, 401(k), and recurring bills over the full 9 weeks, combined with quizzes for points, and it pays out **real classroom grants and student scholarships** (up to $3M) to top performers ([Education World](https://www.educationworld.com/a_news/hr-block-budget-challenge-fills-need-financial-literacy-learning-classrooms-1413804825), [H&R Block](https://www.hrblock.com/tax-center/around-block/our-purpose/introducing-hr-block-budget-challenge/)). Grades 9-12 / age 14+ only — **not eligible for a true middle-school class today**, but it is the strongest evidence that "a multi-week browser simulation with a persona, a paycheck, and recurring bills" is an established, well-funded, nationally-marketed format one tier up from BOW's target age band. A middle-school version does not appear to exist from any vendor found in this research — this is a notable white space, not a competitor BOW currently loses to.

### CFPB "Money as You Grow"

Free, federal (Consumer Financial Protection Bureau), but designed for **parents/caregivers and interdisciplinary activity use**, not a classroom application/assessment product — no simulation, no dashboard, no rubric ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/money-as-you-grow/)). Included for completeness; not a peer competitor to BOW.

### NYSED Grades 5-8 Personal Finance Learning Objectives — the standard BOW is claiming against

Full text extracted directly from the primary source PDF ([NYSED Learning Objectives, March 2026](https://www.nysed.gov/sites/default/files/programs/standards-instruction/ny-personal-finance-learning-objectives_march-2026.pdf)). This is essential context because it is the actual yardstick BOW cites, and because implementation is **mandatory starting the 2026-2027 school year** — i.e., this standard is brand new as of the date of this research.

The Grades 5-8 band has **23 total learning objectives across 5 topics**:
- **Topic 1, Budgeting and Money Management:** 1.1-1.6 (six objectives — needs/wants/values/goals; differing outcomes at similar income; **1.3 "Create a budget for a hypothetical income that includes planned expenses and savings"**; evaluating ad/source credibility; external influences; comparing payment methods)
- **Topic 2, Credit and Debt Management:** 2.1-2.4 (four objectives)
- **Topic 3, Earning Income:** 3.1-3.3 (three objectives)
- **Topic 4, Risk Management:** 4.1-4.4 (four objectives)
- **Topic 5, Saving and Investing:** 5.1-5.6 (six objectives)

BOW's claim of exactly one assessable objective (1.3) is thus **1 of 23** — a little over 4% of the state's own grade-band objective set — even though 1.3 is arguably the single objective a two-world budgeting/spoilage/income-shock simulation is best positioned to assess directly (it is literally "create a budget for a hypothetical income," which is close to a description of both BOW worlds' core loop).

## 2. THE BAR

A critic evaluating a middle-school financial-literacy application/assessment product in 2026 should hold it to this standard: **the product exists to make a specific, already-taught concept visible in a student's decisions, not to teach the concept for the first time**, and it should produce a persistent, teacher-legible artifact of that decision-making that is stronger evidence than a multiple-choice score. It should give every student a durable identity that survives a closed tab and a school year, and give every teacher a class-management layer — a named roster, an assignment with a due date, and a per-student completion/score record — comparable to what a free nonprofit (iCivics) already ships at zero cost. Where the product claims a simulation is the assessment, the simulation's win condition must reward the reasoning the standard names, not reward "gaming the mechanic" — the single most damaging, most-cited failure mode in this category, established against the SIFMA Stock Market Game by name in multiple independent sources. The product should state, honestly and specifically, which state or national standard objectives it can actually produce evidence for, and that claim should be legible enough that a skeptical reader can check it against the primary standards document in under five minutes, the way this report did. A session should take an amount of time proportionate to what it claims to assess: a 15-minute arcade game earns the right to test recall, not application; a claim of "application under uncertainty" earns scrutiny proportional to how much classroom and calendar time it actually costs a teacher to run. Independent evaluation — even a modest pre/post comparison-group study, the bar JA Finance Park clears — outranks a vendor-stated ESSA tier or an internal percentage-gain claim. Cost and sponsorship should be disclosed plainly, because a critic in this space has real, citable precedent (Rethinking Schools, naming EverFi and JA specifically) for treating corporate/bank sponsorship as a pedagogical red flag, not just a funding footnote. Finally, the product should be honest about what its artifact is: a "written explanation a person reads" is a stronger evidence claim than "the student clicked through 25 minutes of story," but it is a weaker, slower, harder-to-scale claim than "an auto-graded, exportable, per-student, per-lesson score report," which is what the category's best-funded competitor (EverFi) already ships.

## 3. TESTABLE CRITERIA

Each is a concrete pass/fail check a critic can run against BOW directly.

1. **Roster persistence.** Log in as a student, complete part of a world, close the tab, and return a day later using only the class code and seat number. **Fail** if the seat-number model cannot distinguish "the same student returning" from "a different student who picked the same open seat" — compare against iCivics, where a bulk-added student gets a permanent, teacher-assigned username that survives indefinitely ([iCivics teacher guide](https://www.nhcivics.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/icivics_teacher_user_guide.pdf)).
2. **Teacher account recoverability.** Have the teacher clear browser storage or switch devices. **Fail** if the class becomes unrecoverable — every competitor researched (NGPF, iCivics, EverFi, JA) ties the teacher identity to an emailed, re-loginable account, not a single bearer token in one browser's localStorage.
3. **Objective coverage vs. the primary standard.** Open the NYSED Grades 5-8 PDF ([source](https://www.nysed.gov/sites/default/files/programs/standards-instruction/ny-personal-finance-learning-objectives_march-2026.pdf)) and count how many of the 23 listed objectives BOW's evidence model actually claims. **Fail** if the claimed count is 1 (current state) against a 23-objective standard whose other four topics — Credit and Debt, Earning Income, Risk Management, and most of Saving and Investing — are entirely untouched by either world's mechanics as described (no credit, no taxes/payroll, no insurance/identity-theft, no interest-rate comparison across accounts appears in an 8-week basketball season or a 4-Saturday food stall).
4. **Assessment artifact format.** Compare BOW's written explanation against NGPF's unit exam (20 MC recall questions, [Docsity](https://www.docsity.com/en/docs/ngpf-budgeting-complete-exam-questions-and-answers/11603442/)) and EverFi's auto-graded pre/post quiz per lesson. **Pass** condition for BOW: the written explanation must visibly require synthesis across multiple in-world decisions (not recall of a single term) to score above the rubric floor — verify by reading 3 real (non-fixture) submitted explanations end-to-end.
5. **Win-condition integrity (the SIFMA test).** For each world, identify the highest-scoring path a student could take. **Fail** if that path is achievable by a strategy that contradicts the concept the world claims to assess (the exact critique leveled at the Stock Market Game — see [Saylor Financial Fundamentals](https://www.saylorfinancialfundamentals.com/post/a-stock-market-game-for-students-may-teach-the-wrong-lessons-5-reasons-why-better-alternatives) and [Dollars & Sense](https://www.dollarsandsense.org/teaching-about-stocks-for-fun-and-propaganda/)) — e.g., if a student can "win" the basketball savings goal by a mechanic that has nothing to do with sound budgeting, or "win" the food-stall world by exploiting spoilage/demand math rather than reasoning about it.
6. **Teacher completion/score report.** Open BOW's teacher dashboard and try to answer, in under 60 seconds: "which of my 30 students have NOT started, which are stuck, and which finished with the strongest evidence?" **Fail** if this requires opening each student's individual evidence trail one at a time — compare against EverFi's stated real-time completion-rate + quiz-score dashboard, and iCivics' green-checkmark assignment roster.
7. **Session-length-to-claim ratio.** Time an actual playthrough of each world end-to-end (student-reported: ~20-25 min basketball, ~18-24 min pop-up). **Fail** if a single ~20-minute session is the sole evidence source claimed for objective coverage broader than one topic — compare against JA Finance Park's 13 taught lessons + a half/full day simulation, or the Stock Market Game's 8-12 week minimum enrichment session, as the field's precedent for how much time a "the simulation is the assessment" claim usually costs.
8. **Independent or comparison-group evidence.** Ask: has BOW's evidence-to-objective mapping (the 18 micro-skill observations → rubric → competency pipeline) been checked by anyone other than the team that built it? **Fail** if no pre/post or comparison-group study exists — JA Finance Park clears this bar with a 2015 RMC Research study showing a moderate-to-large effect size ([evaluation summary](https://www.jawm.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/JA-Finance-Park-Evaluation-Summary.pdf)); FoolProof claims a 34.3% pre/post gain; EverFi claims ESSA Level III. BOW currently has none of these, only internal rubric scoring.
9. **No-AI-grading claim, verified.** BOW claims written explanations are "scored only by a person; no AI touches student writing." **Test:** find the human-scoring workflow in the teacher product (a reading queue) and confirm it is load-bearing, not vestigial — i.e., confirm a submission cannot reach a competency result without a human override/approval step. If confirmed, this is a genuine differentiator (see Section 4) — no other product researched makes or needs to make this claim, because none of them use free-text scoring at all; they are exclusively MC/quiz (NGPF, EverFi, Financial Football) or a game score (Arcade, Stock Market Game portfolio value).
10. **Sponsorship/cost transparency.** Confirm BOW discloses, in a place a teacher would actually see it, that the product is free and who funds it. **Fail** condition would be silence on this exact point while marketing "no strings" — given the live precedent (Rethinking Schools naming EverFi/JA specifically for undisclosed-to-students sponsorship dynamics), a critic will check for this.
11. **Age-appropriateness of the identity model relative to precedent.** iCivics supports teacher-bulk-added, no-email students as young as elementary grade — proving "no student email" is solvable **without** falling back to an anonymous seat number. **Fail** if BOW cannot articulate why its seat-number model is a deliberate tradeoff (e.g., FERPA/COPPA minimization for under-13s) rather than an unaddressed gap, since a free competitor already solved the identical constraint with a stronger identity model.
12. **Standards claim legibility.** Time how long it takes an outside reader (this researcher) to open BOW's own objective-mapping documentation and verify the "only 1.3" claim against the primary NYSED PDF. **Pass** if under 5 minutes (achieved in this research pass); this is a real, met bar — flag it as a genuine strength, not a weakness, per Section 5.

## 4. WHERE BOW LIKELY LOSES

- **Breadth of standards coverage.** 1 claimed-assessable objective out of 23 in the Grades 5-8 band is a thin claim next to every full-course competitor researched (NGPF's 9-unit course, EverFi's 7-lesson FutureSmart, Hands on Banking's 5 units, Khan Academy's multi-unit course) that at least *attempts* coverage across all five NYSED topics, even if their assessment format (MC recall) is weaker per-item than BOW's. Four of five NYSED topics — Credit/Debt, Earning Income, Risk Management, and most of Saving/Investing — are simply absent from BOW's two worlds as described (no credit use, no payroll/taxes, no insurance, no interest-rate comparison shopping). A critic will ask why a "decision-making simulation" product, which should be *better* than MC recall at real application, is choosing to claim coverage of a *smaller* slice of the standard than weaker-format competitors claim.
- **Identity and account architecture.** A seat-number-in-localStorage model is weaker than the free-nonprofit bar (iCivics: persistent named students, teacher-recoverable accounts, class codes plus a real roster) and weaker than every corporate competitor (EverFi, NGPF where relevant, Khan Academy). An unrecoverable single-browser teacher bearer token is a genuine outlier-bad pattern next to every competitor's emailed/re-loginable teacher account.
- **Teacher-facing score legibility.** "A dashboard driven by what students decided rather than by scores" will read, next to EverFi's real-time auto-graded score dashboard and iCivics' green-checkmark/game-session view, as **slower for a teacher to triage at scale**. A teacher with 150 students across 5 sections cannot skim 150 decision-trails the way they can skim a score column. This is the single most concrete, most testable gap against the strongest-funded competitor in the set.
- **No independent evaluation.** JA Finance Park has a 2015 comparison-group study; FoolProof and EverFi cite pre/post gain numbers (vendor-reported, but at least *claimed and quantified*). BOW's evidence pipeline (18 micro-skills → rubric → competency → NYSED mapping) is elaborate and internally consistent but has, as far as this research found, never been checked against an outside measure or a comparison group.
- **Win-condition integrity is unverified from outside.** The single sharpest, most citable critique in this entire research pass — SIFMA's Stock Market Game teaching students to game a leaderboard instead of reasoning soundly — is a template a critic can and will apply to BOW's basketball savings-goal mechanic and food-stall spoilage/demand mechanic. This research could not verify from outside whether BOW's highest-scoring paths reward sound reasoning or reward exploiting the simulation's numbers; that is an internal build question, but the external bar (Section 3, criterion 5) is now on record as the test a critic will run.
- **Sponsorship-critique immunity is not proven, only structurally plausible.** BOW has no visible sponsor, which sidesteps the Rethinking Schools line of attack in principle — but this research found no place where BOW states that plainly to a teacher, so the advantage is currently unclaimed, not banked.

---

## 5. WHAT BOW MIGHT ALREADY WIN ON

- **Assessment format is a real step up from the category median.** The dominant assessment artifact across this entire research pass — NGPF's unit exams, EverFi's lesson quizzes, Financial Football, Zogo's per-module quiz — is **multiple-choice recall**, explicitly separated by Common Sense's own reviewers from "teaching" ("Lessons do the teaching; the game only tests understanding," of Financial Football). BOW's closed-event-vocabulary → derived-facts → rubric → human-read written-explanation pipeline is a structurally more ambitious evidence claim than almost everything else surveyed, NGPF's own Case Studies (the field's best application-task design found) being the nearest peer, and Case Studies are ungraded discussion artifacts, not a scored, standards-mapped pipeline.
- **Human-only scoring of student writing is a genuine, rare differentiator.** No other product in this research uses free-text student writing as its central evidence at all — every competitor's "assessment" is a quiz, a portfolio-value score, or a simulation completion checkbox. BOW committing to a person reading every explanation, with no AI in that specific loop, is a claim none of NGPF, EverFi, Khan Academy/Khanmigo, or the Arcade-style games are positioned to make, and it directly answers a criticism (shallow, gameable, auto-graded "understanding") that is otherwise endemic to the category.
- **No visible corporate/bank sponsor.** Given a live, citable critique (Rethinking Schools) naming EverFi and Junior Achievement specifically for sponsor-shaped content, BOW's apparent lack of a financial-services sponsor is a structural advantage over roughly half the products in this survey (EverFi, Banzai, FoolProof, Financial Football/Visa, Hands on Banking/Wells Fargo, SIFMA/securities industry, JA) — assuming BOW actually discloses this rather than leaving a teacher to assume otherwise.
- **A legible, checkable standards claim.** This researcher was able to open BOW's claim (1 objective, 1.3) and check it against the actual primary-source NYSED PDF in a few minutes, and confirm the claim is *literally true and precisely scoped* — 1.3 is "create a budget for a hypothetical income that includes planned expenses and savings," which is a close, honest match to what a savings-goal-under-income-shock world and a spoilage/demand-forecasting food-stall world can actually produce evidence for. That precision and restraint is unusual: most competitors surveyed (NGPF, EverFi, Hands on Banking, FoolProof) make broad "aligned to Jump$tart/CEE/Common Core/state standards" claims that are far harder for an outside reader to verify item-by-item, because they don't name which specific numbered objectives map to which specific in-product evidence. BOW's narrowness is a coverage weakness (Section 4) but its precision is a credibility strength a critic should recognize as different from the sector's normal vague-alignment marketing.
- **No logistics burden.** Unlike JA Finance Park/BizTown (a facility visit, a mobile unit, or a scheduled virtual session with volunteer mentors) or the Stock Market Game (an 8-12-week minimum commitment), BOW's ~20-minute browser worlds can run in one class period with zero scheduling coordination — a real, practical advantage for a teacher deciding what fits in a single 45-minute period, which is the unit NYSED's own Grades 5-8 guidance repeatedly assumes ("stand-alone courses... module in Financial and Consumer Literacy").
