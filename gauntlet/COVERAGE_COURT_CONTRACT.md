# The coverage court contract

**Canonical. A verdict that did not run this is not a verdict.**

This exists because four courts ran the same-sounding question and did not apply the same test.
Modules 3 and 5 scored their winners against the rows in `competencies.ts`. Module 4 scored its
winner against the designer's own rubric — four of Six Kits' twelve rows belong to
`plan-for-the-unexpected`, whose `evidenceRequirements` array in shipped code is `[]`. The
verdict read "+3 objectives." The truth was +1. Nobody lied and nobody was careless; the two
courts were answering different questions and the answers were reported in the same column of
the same table.

That is the failure mode this document is against: **not dishonesty, divergence.** Two honest
readings of "does this cover 4.1" that differ by two objectives, published as one number.

---

## 1. What a court judges

> **Shipped reality. The code that would run if a student opened the product right now.**

A court judges, in this order of authority:

1. **What the product does when used.** The golden path, walked. A screenshot beats a source file.
2. **The spine as it ships** — `src/domain/competency/competencies.ts`, `availability.ts`,
   `src/domain/standards/mappings/nysed-2026.ts`, and the completion rules beside them.
3. **The generated artifacts** — `MODULE_COVERAGE.md`, which is regenerated and drift-tested, so
   it is the code speaking rather than a person summarising the code.

A court **never** judges against:

- a design brief, a prototype write-up, or a mechanics-war entry;
- the designer's rubric, table of rows, or claimed coverage;
- a competency version that has since changed (see §5);
- a roadmap row, a "planned" marker, or a partially-present UI;
- the previous court's verdict, restated.

**The test for whether you are judging shipped reality:** you can name the file and line that
would have to change for your verdict to become wrong. If you cannot, you are judging a brief.

## 2. The two ladders, and which words are canonical

Two different questions get asked with the same English word, and conflating them is how a
statement about *this product* becomes a statement about *somebody's child*.

### 2a. What BOW can assess — a fact about us

Canonical values, generated by `scripts/module-coverage.ts`:

| Word | Means | The bar |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **demonstrable** | A student *could* settle this objective here | A competency mapped `full` is available — or, where a completion rule bundles several skills under one number, **every** competency it names is |
| **evidence only** | Work here speaks to the objective and cannot settle it | A `partial` mapping exists and is available. Two partials are still not a demonstration; there is no arithmetic that turns them into one |
| **not assessable** | BOW has no world that asks a student for this | Everything else |

*Available* has one meaning: some built world produces **every required evidence requirement** of
the competency. All-or-none, and a competency whose requirements array is empty is never
available, because a check that passes vacuously is the most dangerous kind of pass.

### 2b. What a student has shown — a fact about them

Canonical values, from `resolveObjectiveCoverage`: **demonstrated** · **partially-assessed** ·
**not-assessed**. These are per student and never aggregate upward into a score.

### 2c. The brief's four words, mapped

The V4 brief asks for *assessed / practiced / exposed / not-covered*. Those are the right four
distinctions and they are finer than the shipped three, so the mapping is stated once here and a
court uses **the shipped words in verdicts** and these as reasoning:

| Brief word | Shipped word | Test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **assessed** | demonstrable + a rubric that separates levels | Every required row produced, *and* a student who thought poorly can score below 5 |
| **practiced** | demonstrable, weak rubric | Every required row produced, but the obvious move scores top. **Report as demonstrable and file the rubric defect.** Do not silently downgrade — say both |
| **exposed** | evidence only | The idea appears; nothing observes the student doing it |
| **not-covered** | not assessable | — |

The one that gets lost is **practiced**. A world where the virtuous-looking answer scores 5 is
covering the objective in the availability sense and failing it in the assessment sense. Both
sentences are true and a court that writes only one of them has misled someone.

### 2d. Confidence in a single observation

Attached to a claim about a student, never to a coverage figure:

**observed** (the log contains the act) · **derived** (computed from acts in the log) ·
**student-stated** (they wrote it; it is their claim, not ours) · **teacher-judged** (a person
decided) · **unknown**. `null` is not zero, anywhere, ever: "the world never presented the
opportunity" is not a low score.

## 3. The verification protocol

Run this per objective claimed. It is six steps and takes minutes; skipping it is what cost two
objectives last time.

1. **Open `competencies.ts` and read the required rows.** Not the design's list — the file's.
   `requiredEvidenceRequirementsFor(id).length` is the number to beat.
2. **For each required row, name the act in the world that produces it,** and say where in the
   log that act lands. A row you cannot route is a row the world does not produce, however
   obvious the routing seems.
3. **Check the kinds.** A `kind: "explanation"` row needs written words from the student. A world
   that produces four decisions against a competency requiring three decisions and an explanation
   produces **three of four**, and lights nothing.
4. **Check `explanationRequired` against the rows.** They have disagreed three times in this
   repository. `competencyShape.test.ts` now guards it; a court still reads it, because a guard
   tells you they agree and not whether either is right.
5. **Apply the mapping honestly.** `full` can light an objective. `partial` cannot, alone or in
   combination. Where a completion rule exists it wins outright — an objective bundling three
   skills is not demonstrated by whichever of the three happens to be built.
6. **Check the student view, not just the product view.** `studentOutcomeFor` requires every
   `allOf` competency to have a usable result *for that student*. An objective can be demonstrable
   at product level and permanently partial for a student who plays only one world. Say which.

**Then state the count twice:** as the designer scored it, and as the spine scores it. Where they
differ, the spine wins and the difference is the finding.

## 4. Standing over-claim prohibitions

A verdict that does any of these is void. All six have happened here or were one review from
happening:

1. **Scoring a competency with no authored requirements.** Writing rubric rows in a design does
   not create them. The array in the file is the contract.
2. **Summing partials.** Two `partial` mappings are two pieces of evidence, not one demonstration.
3. **Counting a completion-rule objective from one of its parts.**
4. **Counting a mapping as an assessment.** A mapping records which skill sits behind an
   objective. It says nothing about whether anything can observe a student doing it.
5. **Adding a required row because it turns a cell green.** A reasoning box added for coverage,
   not because the objective's verb demands the student say something, is coverage theater — and
   it raises the availability bar on an all-or-none competency, so it can cost more than it buys.
6. **Publishing "coming soon" for an unfunded module.** `not assessable` is a legitimate,
   generated, tested state. A false yes is a statement about their students.

## 5. Version drift, and why a stamp exists

`is-the-add-on-worth-it` had four required rows when a world was designed against it. It gained a
fifth. The design was never re-read, the court scored the old shape, and a coverage figure went
out two objectives wrong. **Nothing in the build was capable of noticing**, because a coverage row
recorded *which* requirements a world produces and never *which version of the competency* that
was true of.

So every `WorldEvidenceCoverage` row now carries `contract: competencyContract(competencyId)` — a
fingerprint of its required ids and kinds — and `worldContractFreshness.test.ts` fails the build
when a stamp and the competency disagree, naming every affected world and the replacement stamp.

**The fingerprint moves** when a required row is added or dropped, or a decision becomes an
explanation. **It does not move** on a reworded rule, label or misconception — a guard that fired
on a typo would be re-stamped reflexively, which is how a guard becomes a formality.

Two rules follow, and they bind courts as much as builders:

- **A court records the contract it judged against.** Every verdict about a world's coverage
  carries the `competencyContract(...)` string for each competency it scored. A verdict without
  one is a verdict whose shelf life nobody can check.
- **Re-stamping is an act.** It means a person opened the world and checked it against the new
  contract. Bumping a stamp to make a test pass is the failure this whole section is about,
  performed deliberately.

## 5a. What must be true before a world's rows are stamped

Seven rules, earned by five designs converging on the same mistakes in one blind war. They bind
every world and they are checked **before** any row lands in `competencies.ts`, because a row is
a claim about a student the moment it ships.

1. **No keyword-whitelist scoring of an explanation row.** Three of five designers independently
   reached "the explanation passes if it contains token X." It is the path of least resistance
   under `explanationRequired: true` and it turns a judgement row into a vocabulary row.
2. **A world may not print, in student-facing text, the analysis a row scores.** If the screen
   says who benefits, the who-benefits row measures transcription.
3. **A "decide whether" competency needs at least one verdict-correctness row** — the student's
   conclusion against authored ground truth, with an explicit escape for justified dissent. A
   rubric can be eight rows of impeccable process and never score the decision.
4. **No conditional in a required row's rule.** Availability is all-or-none, so every `if` is a
   legal student path on which the world produces nothing.
5. **Timing is a data-quality annotation, never a row.** A row whose pass state means "was not
   fast" asserts nothing about a competency, and it scores reading speed and assistive tech.
6. **A world about influence authors at least one true urgency or true crowd signal.** Where every
   pressure signal is fake, the world teaches the mirror-image misconception cleanly.
7. **Seed-swappable truth.** Which claim is false rotates per class, or the answer crosses the
   lunchroom by 12:15 and the second period measures who has friends.

The full reasoning, with the specific designs each rule came from, is in
`READ_AND_JUDGE_WAR_VERDICT.md`.

## 6. Verdict format

A coverage verdict states, for each objective claimed:

```
2.2  claimed: demonstrable
     rests on: decide-to-borrow  [decide-to-borrow:4:xxxxx]
     required rows: 4 (3 decision, 1 explanation)
     routed:  er1 → the WAIT/SIGN beat, log: BORROW_DECIDED
              er2 → ... (all four, or it is not demonstrable)
     mapping: full · no completion rule
     student view: settles for a student who plays only this world — yes
     rubric: separates levels? yes/no — if no, "demonstrable, rubric defect filed"
     spine count vs designer count: 4/4 vs 4/4
```

Anything shorter is a claim. This is the shape that can be checked eighteen months later by
somebody who was not there.

## 7. What a court may not be asked

A court is not asked whether the coverage is *enough*, whether to fund a build, or whether a
number is embarrassing. It reports what the product does. The lead decides what to do about it.
A court that softens a count because the count is inconvenient has removed the only reason to
run one.
