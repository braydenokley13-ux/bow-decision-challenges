## IN CHARACTER VERDICT

It flips between game and homework-in-a-costume more than once, and I can point to exactly where each time:

- **Feels like homework** from 0:18 to about 1:35 (the four-payments wall of text, straight into the first TOTAL box) — this is a worksheet with a jersey on.
- **Flips to feels like a game** the second "NOT THAT ORDER" shows up in red — that's the first real reaction the site gave me.
- **Flips back to homework** through the four "Build Avery's plan" question screens — same box, same button, four times in a row.
- **Flips to game** hard at "The $1,000 Making the Cut Bonus is gone" — this is the one part that felt like something was actually happening to a character, not to a spreadsheet.
- **Flips to homework** again at "Explain your plan" — write two to four sentences is an English-class instruction no matter what font you put it in.

**What I'd tell my friend it is, one sentence:** "It's this money game where you're in charge of a basketball player's / food truck guy's cash for a few weeks, and there's a part in the middle where something actually goes wrong that's kind of good, but you have to type a lot of dollar amounts into boxes to get there."

**Which world I liked more:** Food truck, and it's not close. I don't really care about basketball — I don't watch it, don't play it, the "showcase" stakes didn't mean anything to me going in — so the basketball world's whole hook (make the team, get the sports-media course) needed the game to sell me on caring, and it mostly didn't until Week 5. Food truck didn't need me to care about anything first — "you have money, you have four Saturdays, don't run out" is instantly understandable with zero sports knowledge required, and running my own booth into the ground by being greedy with "Your cut" is a much more relatable disaster than losing a bonus over a game I didn't watch. If you don't like sports, the basketball world spends its first two minutes talking about practices, rosters, and a "showcase" like you already care, and if you don't, that's two minutes of screens you're skimming purely to get to numbers you understand.

## ANALYST SEPARATION

Breaking character. Sorting what I reacted to into **design defects** (the product's fault, fixable) versus **school is boring** (not the product's fault — it's homework, homework is homework, no amount of UI polish removes that).

**Design defects:**
- The repeated, unvaried "TOTAL $ [blank] → Check" widget appears roughly five times across a single basketball playthrough (lodging cost, and all four "Build Avery's plan" questions) with no change in interaction style or visual framing between instances. By the third occurrence a student has learned the pattern, not the math — the fix is worth making (vary the input mechanism, or reduce the count) because it is teaching pattern-matching against a genuinely reusable UI, not budgeting.
- Food-truck's "Send the rest to one line" shortcut lets a student route 100% of starting capital into "Your cut" with a single, unweighted, unwarned tap, which zeroes the Stock line and produces four consecutive "0 sold" Saturdays with no in-context warning at the decision point and no return path to the original allocation screen once the market opens. The game still runs the student through a full "how would you run it again" reflection as if the run were valid data, rather than flagging that nothing actually happened. This is a real gap between "technically completable" and "produced the intended learning experience."
- The Week 5 "Two calls" screen stacks two independent yes/no decisions on one screen, but the reminder that a second decision is still pending ("One call still to make") is small text at the very bottom of a long screen, easy to miss after resolving the first toggle, especially directly under a big visible primary button that looks like a natural stopping point.
- The auto-solve/auto-balance shortcut buttons ("Fill in one plan that balances," "Send the rest to one line") are present on every budget-allocation screen in both worlds and are the single most tempting button on the page for a disengaged player. Their existence as a stress-relief valve is reasonable, but their prominence relative to the manual controls means a student can complete the entire "adapt-a-plan / plan-within-income" portion of both challenges without ever doing the arithmetic themselves. Worth a design conversation, not necessarily a removal.
- The ranking puzzle ("Which place costs the least?") is the very first interactive puzzle a student meets, gives no starting signal that the pre-filled order needs to change, and — unlike the later, harder budget-reallocation screens — has no "show me the answer" escape hatch if a student gets stuck after a wrong guess. The easiest puzzle in the product is the one most likely to leave a stuck kid with nowhere to go.
- Empty, unused vertical space below the fold on the "Two ways in, you pick one" join screen, on both a 1366px desktop and a 1024×600 Chromebook — the screen visually ends about a third of the way down the viewport, which can read as "the page is still loading" or "something's missing" on a first-ever visit, the worst possible screen for that impression.
- Three-card comparisons (lodging options, food-truck booths) that work well as a side-by-side row on desktop become a long single-column scroll on a 390px phone, meaning comparing option 1 against option 3 requires scrolling back and forth rather than a glance. Given that a real classroom mix likely includes phones, this measurably raises the cost of doing the comparison the puzzle is asking for.
- Minor vocabulary friction stacked across both worlds for a 7th-grade reading level: "cushion," "banked," "reserve" used for two different things in the same story, and "sports-media course" named as a goal but never actually described. None of these individually blocks comprehension, but they add up across a 20-minute session aimed at middle schoolers.

**School is boring (not the product's fault):**
- Not caring about the premise of a story you weren't going to pick for yourself (a kid indifferent to basketball skimming the opening beats) — that's a taste problem, not a UI problem, and the food-truck world's more universal premise is evidence the team already understands this and hedged for it by offering a second world.
- Skimming explanatory paragraphs in favor of the biggest bold number on the screen — this is just how a 12-year-old reads anything, a syllabus included, and no amount of good writing fully fixes it.
- Writing the final "2–4 sentence" reflection feeling like an English assignment — it is one, structurally, by design (a teacher is meant to read it), and that's a legitimate assessment format, not a design failure.
- Being less engaged during the plain arithmetic screens than during the narrative-crisis screens — a bored reaction to "do the multiplication" versus an engaged reaction to "a bonus you were counting on just disappeared" is the expected, correct response of a kid to a word problem versus a plot twist. The product is not obligated to make multiplication feel like a plot twist; the fact that it built at least one genuine plot twist at all is doing real work here.
- Wanting the whole thing to be shorter — an 18–25 minute single-sitting budgeting exercise is going to feel long to a 12-year-old regardless of execution; the product already surfaces an honest time estimate on the world-choice screen, which is the right mitigation, not a UI fix.

## FINDINGS

**HIGH**
1. **Food-truck "Send the rest to one line" → Your cut produces a silent, unrecoverable zero-stock run.** A single first-instinct tap during the opening budget screen removes all money from Stock, and every subsequent Saturday completes with 0 trays cooked / 0 plates sold, through to a final "reflection" screen that treats the run as normal data to write about. No warning appears at the moment of the tap, and no path back to the original allocation screen was found once the market opens. Recommend: either a confirmation/warning when a shortcut would zero out Stock, or a distinct end-state screen when zero sales occur across all four Saturdays, rather than routing into the standard reflection flow. Screens: `m-foodtruck-16.png`, `m-foodtruck-26.png`, `m-foodtruck-30.png`.
2. **The core "type a total, hit Check" widget repeats identically ~5 times in a single basketball playthrough with no variation**, training pattern-recognition rather than reinforcing the arithmetic it's meant to test. By the third instance, engagement visibly drops (self-reported skimming, guessing the biggest number on screen). Recommend varying either the interaction (e.g., some screens use a slider or multiple-choice check instead of free numeric entry) or reducing the count. Screens: `verify-sticky-q4-scroll400.png` (Question 4 of 4, representative of the repeated pattern), `orderb-03-result.png`.

**MEDIUM**
3. **Auto-solve shortcut buttons ("Fill in one plan that balances," "Send the rest to one line") are the most visually tempting action on every budget-allocation screen**, letting a disengaged student complete both worlds' core "build/adapt a plan" objective without performing the underlying math themselves. Not a bug, but worth a deliberate design decision about prominence/framing given how directly it undercuts the stated competency (`adapt-a-plan`, `plan-within-income`). Screen: `verify-sticky-q4-scroll400.png`.
4. **Three-card comparison screens (lodging options, food-truck booths) do not scale well to a 390px phone** — each card is tall enough that comparing option 1 to option 3 requires scrolling back and forth rather than a glance, undermining the point of a side-by-side comparison. Screen: `vp-phone390-06-foodtruck-booths.png`.
5. **The first puzzle a student meets (lodging-cost ranking) has no "show me the answer" fallback**, while later, harder budget-reallocation puzzles do offer one. A student stuck on the easiest puzzle in the product has strictly less help available than one stuck on a harder one later. Screens: `m-basketball-01.png`, `orderb-03-result.png`.
6. **Noticeable unused space below the fold on the class-code join screen**, on both a 1366px desktop and a 1024×600 Chromebook — the page visually "ends" partway down the viewport on the very first interactive screen a student sees, which risks reading as an unfinished or broken page on first contact. Screens: `01-after-start-click.png`, `vp-chromebook1024x600-01-join.png`.
7. **A second, independent decision on the "Week 5 · Two calls" screen is easy to miss** after resolving the first, because the "one call still to make" reminder is small text below a full-width primary button, rather than a more prominent signal that the screen isn't finished. (Text-log evidence in the play-through; not separately screenshotted.)

**LOW**
8. **Vocabulary above a comfortable 7th-grade level appears without in-line explanation**: "cushion" (food-truck budget line), "banked," "organiser" (British spelling, reads momentarily as a typo), and "sports-media course" (named as the goal throughout but never described). None individually blocking, but they accumulate over a ~20-minute session aimed at middle schoolers.
9. **"Seat" is overloaded** — it means the classroom login seat number on the join screen and a reserved course seat in the basketball storyline. Not confusing in context, but an avoidable collision.
10. Two `fullPage` screenshots taken during this review (`m-basketball-18` region, not included in the final screenshot set) showed a duplicated sticky header when captured via Playwright's full-page mode. **Verified via direct viewport screenshots at multiple scroll depths (`verify-sticky-q4-scroll400.png`, etc.) that this does not occur during normal scrolling** — it is a screenshot-tool artifact from capturing a `position: sticky` header across a stitched full-page capture, not a defect a real student would ever see. Noted here only so it isn't mistaken for a live bug by anyone re-reading this report's screenshots.
