# Flipside

*You build the listing that sells your stuff to strangers — then you spend what it earned inside a marketplace where every other listing is built with the exact same tricks, and one of them is lying to you.*

## Verbs

**BUILD · SCOUT · CHECK · NAME · WEIGH · SPEND**

BUILD — drag components onto your own listing and watch a buyer-interest meter react. SCOUT — open other people's listings for the thing you actually need. CHECK — pull an outside source that doesn't work for the seller. NAME — tag the specific thing on a listing that's pushing you to decide fast. WEIGH — drag evidence into a verdict card and commit to Trust or Don't trust. SPEND — buy, with your own balance, before the clock the whole world runs on hits zero.

## The premise

*(as the student reads it, screen one)*

Flipside is the resale app half your school uses. You've got one thing to sell: your old handheld console, cracked in one corner, battery not what it used to be, forty games still loaded on it. Flipside's assistant, Nudge, will help you build the listing — what photo, what words, what price, what badge. Sell it well and it goes fast.

Then you're the buyer. Tryouts for the team are Saturday. You need cleats, and whatever your console earned is what you've got to spend. Four sellers have what you need. Their listings are built exactly like yours was — badges, timers, glowing reviews, a friend's message pushing one of them. One listing is telling you something that isn't true. You won't be told which. You have to find out — and you have to buy something, because Saturday is coming whether you're ready or not.

## Signature mechanic

**Why two builds, not one.** A world where the student only ever constructs a pitch for somebody else produces neither competency — it's a copywriting exercise with no spending decision and no disagreeing sources in it. So Flipside is built as two halves that share one hand-act. In the first half the student *builds a persuasive object*. In the second half the student *builds a judgment* — the same drag-and-assemble action, now pointed at evidence instead of at a photo tray, ending in a real purchase made with a real, earned, spendable balance under a real deadline. The competencies are produced entirely in the second half. The first half is not decorative — it's what makes the second half legible, because the student recognizes every trick on someone else's listing as one they placed themselves an hour earlier — but it is not where either rubric row lives, and this document doesn't pretend otherwise.

**Half one — the Listing Canvas.** The screen is a phone mock-up, portrait, centered, with a component tray running down the right side: five labeled slots — Photo, Claim line, Price display, Badge, Urgency banner — each a horizontal shelf of 2–3 swatches the student drags onto the phone frame. Nothing is pre-filled. Above the phone, a single number: **Predicted interest: 40**, with a thin bar under it that fills as the number climbs (cap 100). Every swatch carries two hidden numbers the student never sees directly: an interest weight and a return-risk increment. Photo A (an honest, well-lit shot that shows the crack) is +8 interest, 0 risk. Photo B (angled so the crack is out of frame) is +14, 0 risk. Photo C (a catalog stock photo, not actually this unit) is +22, +1 risk. Claim line runs the same pattern: the true line ("Hairline crack in the corner, doesn't affect the screen. Battery lasts about two hours.") is +6/0 risk; "Small mark, barely noticeable. Great battery life!" is +12/+1 (the battery claim is false); "Like new condition" is +20/+2. Price display: a flat "$100" is +5/0; a fabricated anchor "Was $180, now $100 — 44% off" (it was never $180) is +18/+1; a payment-plan frame "$100, or $20/mo for 5 months" is +10/0. Badge: none is +0; "Trending 🔥" (shown regardless of anything true) is +10/0; "✓ Verified Seller" (only available because the student's own account is genuinely verified) is +12/0. Urgency: off is +0; "Selling fast — 2 people watching" (set by the toggle, not by actual traffic) is +15/+1; a countdown tied to nothing real is +15/+1.

Every drop animates instantly: the phone frame updates, the interest number ticks up with a small "+14" flash, and a row of three buyer-avatar faces below the phone shift expression (skeptical → neutral → convinced) as the total crosses 55 and 75. At the end of the build, a sale always happens — this isn't a pass/fail gate — but the **sale price** scales with final interest (price = $70 + 0.5 × interest, so a 40-only listing sells around $90 and a maxed-out one around $120), and if the hidden risk counter reached 2 or more, a buyer message arrives two screens later: *"This wasn't like new — the crack is way bigger than in the photo. I want $15 back."* — and $15 comes straight off the balance the student is about to carry into the next half. This is the only consequence Act One produces, and it produces it on the student's own future spending money, not on a grade.

**Half two — Case File and Verdict Card.** The Scout screen is a 4-card grid, each card a small listing preview (photo, price, any badges, one claim line). Tapping a card opens the **Case File**: a four-tile tray — *Seller says* (the listing's own words, always present), *Buyers say* (star rating and review count, with a one-line note on what those reviews actually attach to), *Outside check* (a lookup tool returning one fixed, independently-sourced fact — a manufacturer price page, or a reverse-photo search that reports whether this exact image appears elsewhere), and *From a friend* (present only on the one listing a scripted message pushes). Below the tray sits the **Verdict Card**: four empty slots — Evidence for, Evidence against, Who benefits, and a Trust / Don't trust toggle — plus a required "Why" text field. The student drags tiles from the tray into the card's slots; a tile can be dragged into more than one card across different listings, so comparing two listings never destroys either one's evidence. The toggle and BUY button both stay disabled until at least one tile sits in Evidence-for or Evidence-against and the toggle has been actively set — there is no default.

Around the listing card itself, small icons the student taps (not passive labels) form the **Pressure Tray**: on the flashiest listing these are a 🔥 badge, a "👀 6 viewing now" counter, and the struck-through price — plus, deliberately, a ✓ Verified-seller icon sitting in the same row, which is *not* pressure and is included so ticking every icon in sight is a mistake, not a shortcut. Tapping an icon drags a tagged chip into the tray; nothing is pre-tagged. A persistent corner ticker reads "Time until tryouts: 1 day, 6 hours," and it counts down on qualifying actions (opening a listing, viewing a tile), not on the wall clock — so a student who reads slowly is never punished for reading, but a student who dawdles across every listing without ever committing eventually watches it read "20 minutes" and then "QuickKicks_deals just sold out," which is real, not decorative: one listing genuinely disappears from the grid when its own fake countdown expires.

**SPEND** is a single confirm button on the Verdict Card once Trust is set: *"Buy for $95?"* It charges the visible balance immediately and routes to a resolution screen that always tells the student, in plain narrative, what was actually true about the listing they bought from — whether they were right.

## The trap

**Where a suspicious student gets it wrong.** cleatsbycasey is the listing where the evidence actually holds up — three specific, mutually consistent reviews, an outside price-check that lines up with the seller's own price, a photo that matches the description on zoom — and it is also the listing with no verified badge, only three reviews, and a flat "no returns" line. A student running a blanket "no badge, few reviews, no returns means don't trust it" rule rejects the one listing that survives every check, and rejects it for reasons that read as caution but were never actually tested against the case file open in front of them. With the clock now inside an hour, they fall back on the thing that *feels* safest under pressure — Verified Seller, 4.9 stars, 212 reviews — and buy StrideKing_22 for $95: the same badge-and-star reflex they were supposedly too skeptical to fall for, now used as a tiebreaker because something has to be. The shoes are real and they arrive on time — this isn't a scam — but the "44% off" was invented (retail is $92) and the reviews belong to the seller's whole shop, not this pair. They paid $95 for something a price-check would have shown them was worth $92, and skipped the $78 listing where every number matched. Distrust wasn't wrong because trusting is always right; it was wrong because it was applied as a rule instead of as a reading, and the rule pointed away from the listing where the reading actually supported trust.

**Where a credulous student gets it wrong.** QuickKicks_deals is the one listing built entirely out of manufactured pressure — a 12-minute countdown, "14 viewing now," "1 left" — over a price that undercuts everyone. A credulous student opens the Outside-check tile (so the fact was retrieved, not withheld) and it reads: *"This exact photo also appears on listing Q118, posted 6 weeks ago, by TurfDepot_Verified, size 7, $110."* Read correctly, that's a fraud signal — a stolen photo, a different seller, a different size. Read the way enthusiasm reads it, it's "other people have seen this too" — social confirmation instead of a warning — and they buy. Two days pass with no tracking update; the seller doesn't answer; Flipside support reports the account was suspended for reusing another listing's photos, and the refund lands the Wednesday *after* tryouts. The evidence that would have caught this wasn't hidden. It was opened, read, and misread in the direction the countdown timer had already pointed them.

*(A fourth listing, grabngo_99, exists precisely so "plain and cheap" isn't a safe fallback either: it's unbadged, unflashy, $50 — and a photo-zoom the case file makes available shows mismatched shoe tags, left 6 / right 6½, never mentioned in the seller's claim. No blanket rule survives this world; only reading the specific tile does.)*

## Proposed evidence requirements

### `judge-a-claim` — `read-and-judge`

**J1 — Compares sources that disagree before deciding** *(decision)*
Observable rule: before BUY is pressed, the student has opened the Case File on at least two listings whose Seller-says or Outside-check tiles conflict on the same fact (price history, review applicability, or photo authenticity) for the same item.
Misconception if absent: *(no single named misconception; this is the structural gate the other rows depend on)*.
Produced by: opening ≥2 `marketplace.listingOpened` events before the `purchase.committed` event in one session.

**J2 — Doesn't treat a rating as proof of the specific claim** *(decision)*
Observable rule: for the listing bought from, the Evidence-for slot used to reach the Trust verdict includes at least one *Outside-check* tile — the Buyers-say tile (rating/review count) alone never satisfies the slot.
Misconception if absent: **"A review is evidence."**
Produced by: `verdictCard.slotFilled {slot: evidenceFor}` compared against `caseFile.tileViewed {tileType: outsideCheck}` for the same `listingId`, at `verdictCard.verdictSet {verdict: trust}`.

**J3 — Doesn't treat a longer claim as a truer one** *(decision)*
Observable rule: if the Seller-says tile is used in Evidence-for, an Outside-check tile is also present in Evidence-for or Evidence-against for that same listing before the verdict is set.
Misconception if absent: **"More detail means more true."**
Produced by: same `verdictCard.slotFilled` stream, checked for co-occurrence of `sellerSays` with `outsideCheck` on the listing carrying `verdictAtPurchase`.

**J4 — Names who benefits from the claim, and why that matters** *(explanation, required)*
Observable rule: the Why field, submitted at purchase, names a specific party who gains from the claim being judged (the seller, or the friend on the one listing carrying a DM) and states how that changes how much the claim can be trusted — not a restatement of the listing's own words.
Misconception if absent: *(none named; this row exists to satisfy "identify who benefits" directly, and a restated-prompt answer is scored 2, not 5, under the standard rubric — see the reflex path)*.
Produced by: `verdictCard.whyTextSubmitted`, human-scored, gated to fire only at `purchase.committed`.

**J5 — Verifies a price or condition claim against an independent source before trusting it** *(decision)*
Observable rule: for the listing bought from, an Outside-check tile (manufacturer price page or reverse-photo check) was opened and viewed before the Trust/Don't-trust toggle was set.
Misconception if absent: **"A review is evidence"** (the outside-check tile is the one tile in the case file that isn't the seller's, a buyer's, or a friend's — skipping it is the specific failure this row exists to catch).
Produced by: `caseFile.tileViewed {tileType: outsideCheck}` timestamped before `verdictCard.verdictSet` on the purchased `listingId`.

**J6 — Acts on the more credible one** *(decision)*
Observable rule: the purchase is made from the listing whose Verdict Card verdict was set to Trust. If no listing ever reaches a completed verdict before the deadline expires, this row is not demonstrated — there is no credit for never deciding.
Misconception if absent: *(none named; this row is the honest close of the competency's own third bullet, "act on the more credible one," and it is the row that scores a paralyzed, never-buys run at 0)*.
Produced by: `purchase.committed {verdictAtPurchase}`, or `deadline.expired` with zero `purchase.committed` events in the session.

### `notice-influence` — `choose-under-pressure`

**N1 — Tags algorithmic urgency as pressure, not as proof** *(decision)*
Observable rule: for the listing bought from, every scarcity/urgency icon actually present on that listing (countdown, live-viewer count, "X left") is dragged into the Pressure Tray before BUY unlocks.
Misconception if absent: **"Urgency is information."**
Produced by: `pressureTray.tagged {iconId, correct}` — `correct` computed against the listing's authored icon set — for the `listingId` matching `purchase.committed`.

**N2 — Tags a friend's unprompted push as pressure, not as a neutral opinion** *(decision)*
Observable rule: if the friend DM fired during the session, and its listing is the one bought from, the Pressure Tray includes the friend-nudge tag before BUY.
Misconception if absent: **"Ads don't work on me"**, extended to the case a kid actually meets most often — a friend's plug, not a company's.
Produced by: `pressureTray.tagged {iconId: "friendNudge"}` on the purchased `listingId`.

**N3 — Tags price framing as a push, independent of whether the deal turns out real** *(decision)*
Observable rule: if a struck-through "was/now" price or a payment-plan frame appears on the listing bought from, the Pressure Tray includes a price-framing tag before BUY — this fires the same way whether the anchor price is later shown to be fabricated (StrideKing) or absent (cleatsbycasey has none to tag).
Misconception if absent: **"Urgency is information"** (the price-framing variant: a discount frame reads as a fact about value, not as a way of showing a number).
Produced by: `pressureTray.tagged {iconId: "priceFrame"}` on the purchased `listingId`.

**N4 — Makes the decision inside the real deadline, rather than stalling past it** *(decision)*
Observable rule: `purchase.committed` occurs before `deadline.expired` in the session.
Misconception if absent: *(none named; this is the row that scores a paralyzed run's actual failure — not deciding — as distinct from J6, which reads the same failure against "acted on the credible one" rather than "acted at all")*.
Produced by: `purchase.committed` timestamp vs. the session's `deadline.ticked` stream.

**N5 — Explains how the named pressure did or didn't change the choice** *(explanation, required)*
Observable rule: the Why field names at least one specific tag from the student's own Pressure Tray for the listing bought from (not a generic "ads" or "pressure") and states whether it changed which listing was picked or how much they were willing to pay, with a reason.
Misconception if absent: **"Ads don't work on me"** — an answer that denies any influence while the student's own Pressure Tray carries two or three tags is graded against that contradiction, not taken at its word.
Produced by: `verdictCard.whyTextSubmitted` (the same field as J4 — one Why box, cross-read for both competencies) cross-checked against `pressureTray.tagged` on the same listing.

## The reflex path

A student moving as fast as possible, not reading: taps the top card in the Scout grid (StrideKing_22 — it sorts first, being "Trending"), skips the other three cards entirely, opens its Case File once, drags the Seller-says tile and the Buyers-say tile into Evidence-for (the two biggest, brightest tiles, sitting closest to the tray's top), never opens Outside-check, taps Trust, types "it has good reviews and looks new" into Why, taps the Pressure Tray's one obvious icon (🔥 Trending) and skips the smaller price-strike icon and the Verified-seller distractor, dismisses the friend DM without dragging it anywhere, and taps **Buy for $95**.

Scored honestly, row by row:

- **J1** — fails. Only one listing was ever opened. **0.**
- **J2** — fails. Evidence-for is Seller-says + Buyers-say; no Outside-check tile anywhere in the card. **0.**
- **J3** — fails, same reason. **0.**
- **J4** — the Why text names no one who benefits and doesn't move past what the listing already said. Scored 2 by a person: a real answer, not a restated prompt verbatim, but it clears no bar. **2.**
- **J5** — fails. Outside-check was never opened. **0.**
- **J6** — **passes mechanically.** The purchase matches the Trust verdict that was set. This row checks *coherence between stated verdict and action*, not whether the verdict was earned — and it wasn't. **5**, and it is the only 5 in the set on the judge-a-claim side.
- **N1** — the Trending icon was tagged; the price-strike icon was not, on a listing that carries both. Partial. **2.**
- **N2** — the friend DM was dismissed unread; nothing was ever dragged into the tray for it. **0.**
- **N3** — fails, same reason as N1's missed half. **0.**
- **N4** — **passes mechanically.** The purchase happened well inside the deadline — moving fast is, trivially, moving before the clock runs out. **5.**
- **N5** — the Why text names no pressure element at all, and directly contradicts a Pressure Tray that itself under-tags what was on screen. **0.**

Nine of eleven rows land at 0 or 2. Two land at 5, and both of those two are rows built to check *coherence* — did the action match the stated verdict, did the purchase happen before the clock ran out — not *whether the verdict or the timing were any good*. That gap is intentional and is named here rather than hidden: a fast, unthinking run can look decisive without being informed, and this design accepts that two specific, narrow rows will say so. There is no composite anywhere in this proposal that could turn those two 5s into a mastery claim; a reader of the full eleven-row spread sees nine low scores sitting next to them.

## Accessibility

The construct being judged is never a picture — it is a set of tiles with text on them, a toggle, and a countdown expressed as a number. All three survive intact off-screen.

**Keyboard.** The Listing Canvas's component tray is five `<fieldset>` groups, one per slot, each a set of labeled radio options carrying the exact swatch text a sighted student reads; arrow keys move between options, and the interest number and buyer-face row are a live region that announces on every change ("Predicted interest: 54, up from 40"). The Case File and Verdict Card need no drag at all: each tile is a focusable item: pressing Enter on a tile opens a four-item menu — *File as: Evidence for / Evidence against / Who benefits / Discard* — and selecting one does exactly what the drag does, logging the identical `verdictCard.slotFilled` event. The Pressure Tray is the same list of icons rendered as a checklist with real labels, **including the Verified-seller distractor in the same list** — removing it for keyboard users would remove the actual judgment the row exists to test, so it stays, worded identically: "Verified Seller — this one is not pressure, it's a real check." Tab order visits every tile and every icon before BUY, so nothing reachable by a mouse is unreachable by keyboard.

**Screen reader.** The Case File and Verdict Card are one live table, not a separate route: `Tile | Source | Text | Filed as`, updated by the same events the visual card renders from — the exact tile text a sighted student sees, read in full, in the same order. The corner countdown reads as "Time until tryouts: 20 minutes" on request, not merely on change, so a screen-reader user isn't dependent on catching an unannounced visual update.

**Reduced motion / muted audio.** The interest meter's fill and the buyer-face row change state instantly rather than animating; the "+14" flash becomes a static delta printed beside the number. All feedback is already text (buyer messages, resolution screens, tile contents), so muting audio loses nothing — no cue in this world is carried by sound alone.

**200% zoom / narrow viewport.** The Case File becomes a single-tile-at-a-time stack with next/previous controls and a sticky Verdict Card summary ("Filed: 2 for, 0 against, Who benefits: empty · Trust not yet set"); the Scout grid becomes a single-column list, one card per row, in the same left-to-right reading order the grid used.

**Colour.** Trust/Don't-trust is a labeled toggle, never a colour alone; the risk counter and every tile's provenance ("Seller says," "Outside check") are words on the tile, not a border colour; the "not pressure" distractor is a sentence, not a different icon shape a colourblind student might miss.

Nothing here is a simplified version of the judgment. A student using any of these paths reads the same four tiles, sees the same disagreement, and sets the same toggle a mouse user does.

## What it costs to build

**New UI primitives.**
1. **Listing Canvas** — a component-tray-to-frame compositor with a live weighted-sum meter (5 slots × 2–3 swatches, each swatch carrying `{interestWeight, riskIncrement}`); reusable for any future "build the pitch" beat.
2. **Marketplace grid** — a sortable card list rendered from the *same* listing schema the Canvas writes, so authored NPC listings and the student's own listing are one data shape, not two.
3. **Case File panel** — a fixed four-tile-type evidence tray (`sellerSays | buyersSay | outsideCheck | fromAFriend`) with tile provenance metadata.
4. **Verdict Card** — four drop slots plus a required toggle and a required text field, with a keyboard "file as" menu as the non-drag equivalent.
5. **Pressure Tray** — a tap-to-tag overlay of listing-specific icons, each flagged at authoring time as `pressure: true/false`, always including at least one `false` (legitimate-signal) icon per listing that carries any `true` ones.
6. **Outside-check tools** — two small fixed-output lookups: a manufacturer/price-check (returns one comparison number) and a reverse-photo-check (returns either a matching-listing record or "no other postings found").
7. **Action-driven deadline ticker** — a countdown that decrements on qualifying interaction events, not wall-clock time, with a hard `deadline.expired` state that can end the session without a purchase.
8. **Resolution renderer** — four authored branch texts (one per listing), triggered by `purchase.committed`, always surfacing whichever claim on that listing was true or false.

**New log events.** `listing.componentChosen {slot, variant, truthFlag, weight}` · `listing.published {predictedInterest, riskCounter}` · `marketplace.listingOpened {listingId}` · `caseFile.tileViewed {listingId, tileType}` · `verdictCard.slotFilled {listingId, slot, tileType}` · `verdictCard.verdictSet {listingId, verdict}` · `verdictCard.whyTextSubmitted {listingId, text}` · `pressureTray.tagged {listingId, iconId, correct}` · `deadline.ticked {actionsElapsed}` · `deadline.expired {}` · `purchase.committed {listingId, priceAtPurchase, verdictAtPurchase}` · `outcome.revealed {listingId, wasTrue, consequence}`.

**Reading load.** The screen copy actually drafted above — every swatch, tile, icon label, DM, and resolution branch — totals **763 words**, counted (`wc -w`), not estimated. A typical run reads the premise, one full Act-One build, two to three Case Files in depth (not always all four), and exactly one of the four resolution branches — call it **~1,050–1,250 words** for a real playthrough, with the full 763-word inventory only fully read by a student who opens every tile on every listing before deciding. Both figures sit well under the ~2,200 budget, with room to spare for onboarding chrome and the teacher-facing reteach copy this document doesn't draft. Play time: Act One (build) ~6 min; Scout ~3 min; Case File / Verdict Card work across 2–4 listings ~9 min; Pressure Tray + Buy ~3 min; resolution + reflection ~3 min — **~24 min** total.
